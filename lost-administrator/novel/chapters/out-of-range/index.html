# Chapter 07 — Out of Range

Wednesday, August 5, 2026

08:15 AM

The small conference room on the ground floor of Chesapeake Signal Tech had once again become part of the investigation before most of the building had settled into the morning.

Officer Daniels had connected his laptop to one of the wall displays. Henry sat across from him between printed customer lists, old site summaries and handwritten notes.

On the largest screen, a blue line ended west of Port St. Lucie.

It followed Midway Road, jumped onto Interstate 95, left again at the next exit and continued west along Okeechobee Road. Small rectangular markers lay at intervals along the route. Each represented a camera, a traffic record or another confirmed sighting of the white GMC Yukon.

The farther the line traveled west, the greater the distance between the markers became.

Beyond the last one, there was only road.

Daniels worked through the responses that had arrived since the previous evening. A notepad lay beside him. Almost every line ended with the same short remark.

NO MATCH

Henry stood at the end of the table and held a printed list in both hands, although he had not looked at it for several minutes.

"How many?" he asked.

Daniels continued scrolling.

"Automated hits or ones someone actually reviewed?"

"Both."

Daniels leaned back and rubbed the bridge of his nose with two fingers.

"Enough to know the system is working."

It was not an answer.

Henry knew it.

Several preview images filled the second screen. Some showed only the rear of vehicles under poor lighting. In others, individual plate characters were blocked, overexposed or blurred by motion.

One system had reported a white SUV with a similar sequence during the night. The vehicle had been recorded several hours too late and had a visibly different rear profile. Another hit belonged to a gray pickup truck. On a third, the recognition software had invented two characters from a steep camera angle.

None of the images showed the Yukon.

None showed XQH 742.

Daniels opened the next entry, compared the timestamp, vehicle class and direction of travel, then added another note.

"The sequence has been distributed widely enough now," he said. "Participating agencies, license-plate readers, traffic systems. If that plate passes another connected camera, we should hear about it."

"If they are still using it."

Daniels looked up at Henry.

"Yes."

Henry placed the list on the table. It contained former customers, remote facilities and companies that had worked with Chesapeake Signal Tech over the years. Some entries had already been crossed out. Others carried question marks or short handwritten additions.

"Then the plate does nothing for us right now."

"It does something," Daniels said. "We know it was not a random misread. We know it was made to look like an ordinary Florida plate. And we know what vehicle we are looking for."

"But we don't know where it is."

Daniels turned toward the large display.

The last marker on the blue line sat beyond the dense commercial streets and developed areas. From there, the route entered a region where cameras no longer stood at every major intersection and traffic systems did not create a continuous record of movement.

"The Yukon didn't disappear," Daniels said. "It drove out of the area where vehicles are constantly documented."

Henry studied the empty road beyond the last marker.

"That doesn't sound better."

"It wasn't meant to."

For a moment, only the quiet fan of Daniels' laptop could be heard.

He opened a second map. It showed no camera hits. Only roads, narrow access routes, commercial parcels and scattered technical sites west of the city.

"So far, we have mostly been looking for a vehicle and for people who might have access to one like it," Daniels said. "We keep doing that. But I want you to go through your records differently."

Henry looked down at the papers.

"Differently how?"

Daniels pointed at the map.

"Not who knew Michael. Where Michael knew."

Henry looked first at the map and then at the papers in front of him.

"We already checked the customers."

"The current ones."

"And some former ones."

"I want everything west of the city, or anything that used to be reached from that direction. Remote sites. Radio installations. Pump stations. Storage yards. Workshops. Relay points. Closed service agreements. Anything Chesapeake no longer maintains."

Henry looked again at the blue line.

The short stretch on the interstate.

The exit.

The road west.

"That is years of work."

"Then start with the places where Michael worked himself."

Henry remained silent.

"For that, I need more than the customer lists," he said at last. "I need the old maintenance records."

"Where are they?"

"Some are digital. Some are in the regular archive."

Daniels waited.

"And the rest?"

Henry looked toward the conference-room door.

"Downstairs," he said. "In the old archive and backup room."

Daniels disconnected his laptop from the wall display and closed it.

"Show me."

Henry remained seated for another moment.

"The room is still part of the investigation."

"The evidence team has finished its initial processing," Daniels said. "You can handle the remaining records. Nothing leaves the room until we know what it is."

Henry nodded.

When they stepped into the corridor, the building was quieter than it would have been on an ordinary Wednesday. A few office doors stood open, but only some of the desks were occupied. Conversations softened when Daniels passed.

Henry led the way.

The lights above the stairwell came on with a brief delay as they descended.

"Who knew the old records were kept down here?" Daniels asked.

"Anyone who had been with the company long enough."

"That is not a very precise number."

"No."

Henry continued down the stairs.

"Most people still wouldn't know what to look for," he said. "Current records are upstairs or on the servers. Down here is everything nobody wanted to throw away and nobody needed often enough to move."

"Michael needed it."

Henry did not look back.

"Sometimes."

At the old archive and backup room, fine dark residue from fingerprint powder still remained in the recesses of the lock and handle. A strip of evidence tape above the frame had been opened and marked with a date and initials.

Daniels opened the door.

The desk lamp was off.

Under the ceiling lights, the room seemed larger and emptier than it had on the evening Henry found Michael's Leatherman on the desk.

The tool was gone.

So was the IBM ThinkPad T43.

Only a lighter rectangle in the dust showed where the old laptop had stood.

Henry stopped at the threshold.

His eyes went first to the desk and only then to the shelves.

"The secured items are still with forensics," Daniels said.

"I know."

Henry entered.

Metal cabinets with narrow label holders lined the left wall. Some labels had been printed. Others were yellowed pieces of paper covered with abbreviations and years in black marker.

Open shelves stood opposite them, filled with binders, boxes and old equipment manuals. Documentation for radio modules, controllers and hardware Chesapeake had not used in years was packed between them.

Daniels placed his laptop on a clear corner of the desk.

"Where do we start?"

Henry looked over the cabinets.

"Not with customer names."

"Why not?"

"Some sites changed operators several times. Others existed internally only as site or contract numbers. If we search names, we may miss the oldest ones."

"How were they filed?"

Henry went to the first of three cabinets and opened it.

"By year. Then by service region. Most of the time."

"Most of the time?"

"Michael tried to bring order to it eventually."

Daniels looked over the uneven rows of labels.

"Did it work?"

Henry separated several folders with his fingers.

"For Michael."

The first row contained closed maintenance agreements from 2018 and 2019. Henry removed two binders, worked through their tabs and set them on the desk.

Daniels opened his laptop again.

"Are we looking for addresses?"

"Service regions first."

Henry pulled another binder from the cabinet. Only a year was written on its spine. Inside were tables of site numbers, customer abbreviations and assigned technicians.

Many lines had been crossed out. Others carried handwritten notes such as MIGRATED, CLOSED or NO LONGER SERVICED.

Daniels turned the binder slightly toward himself.

"How far back do we go?"

"I don't know yet."

"When did Michael begin handling field calls?"

Henry thought about it.

"Before I joined the company. Later, he went out less often. Eventually he handled almost only the difficult sites."

"What made a site difficult?"

"Systems nobody understood anymore."

Daniels looked at him.

Henry continued through the pages.

"Old controllers. Protocols nobody had installed in twenty years. Radio equipment without replacement parts. Things like that."

"And Michael could maintain them."

"Michael could usually work out what the person before him had done."

The first locations west of Port St. Lucie appeared several pages later. Some belonged to agricultural businesses. Others were pump installations, storage areas or small technical facilities along older utility routes.

Daniels entered the addresses into the map one by one.

None lay directly on the reconstructed route of the Yukon. Several were miles north or south of SR 70. Others could be reached only by narrow side roads.

Henry placed another folder beside the first.

"These should all be in the current system. They were still active when the new database was introduced."

"Should be?"

"I will check them anyway."

They continued in silence.

Henry read out site numbers and former customer names. Daniels compared them against the map and the records already reviewed. Several facilities still existed under new operators. One pump station had been completely modernized. A storage yard had been sold and demolished years ago.

After almost twenty minutes, Henry removed a gray archive box from the bottom shelf.

On its spine was written:

FIELD SERVICE — WEST

A second label had been added beneath it.

RETIRED / LEGACY

Henry placed the box on the desk and removed the lid.

It did not contain complete customer files. Inside were thin maintenance folders, folded overview maps and individual service reports that had apparently never been placed in the regular binders.

"That doesn't look very systematic," Daniels said.

"That is why it was on the bottom."

Henry lifted out the first stack.

The edges of several pages had yellowed. Some carried an older Chesapeake Signal Tech logo that had not been used in years. Most reports were short.

Date.

Site number.

Reported fault.

Parts used.

Technician.

"Read me anything west of the last camera," Daniels said.

Henry took the first sheet.

"Water-level sensor, County Line Canal. Too far south."

The next.

"Private transmitter mast. Contract ended."

A third.

"Agricultural pump controller. The system was replaced."

He set the reports aside one by one.

Beneath the stack lay a narrow index sheet. The upper half was filled with site numbers. Some had full names. Others only internal abbreviations.

Henry followed the column with one finger.

Then stopped.

Daniels noticed immediately.

"What is it?"

"A number I don't recognize."

"Is that unusual?"

"Not for the oldest records."

Henry leaned closer.

There was no company name beside the site number.

Only one word.

RANGE

The service region in the next column was simply WEST.

No address was listed.

Daniels turned the laptop toward Henry.

"What does range mean?"

"It could mean almost anything. Radio range. Measurement range. A test range. Maybe it was only an internal name."

Daniels indicated the last column.

The assigned technician was identified by two initials.

M.W.

Henry stared at them for several seconds.

"Michael."

"You're sure?"

"Those were his initials in the old service records."

Daniels wrote down the site number.

"How often was it serviced?"

Henry searched the box and found two reports with the same number, then a third. They belonged to older, long-closed contract periods.

Michael's initials appeared on all three.

"At least three times," Henry said.

"Do we have an address?"

"Not here."

"A customer?"

Henry checked the pages again.

"Only an old contract number."

"Can you do anything with it?"

Henry studied the sequence.

"Maybe. The current agreements are in the new system. The old data was never transferred completely."

"Where is it?"

"A read-only legacy database. I need a company workstation."

Daniels photographed the contract number and site identifier.

"Then let's use one."

A few minutes later, Henry sat at an unoccupied desk on the ground floor. Daniels stood behind him while a plain login screen appeared on the monitor.

It looked as though no one had changed it in more than a decade.

Henry entered his credentials.

The system took several seconds to respond. Then a gray interface appeared with narrow fields and small menu buttons.

"You still use this?" Daniels asked.

"Use is an ambitious word."

Henry opened the contract archive and entered the number from the service report.

At first, nothing happened.

Then a single record appeared.

The contract had been closed for years. The former customer was no longer listed under a company name, only as a public or institutional entity. Several fields were empty. The telephone number was inactive. The contact person had been marked as no longer current.

But the service location remained.

Henry read the name twice.

"Do you know it?" Daniels asked.

"No."

"Michael did."

Henry looked at the service history below the site information.

The same technician appeared on every entry.

WEBER, MICHAEL

Daniels leaned closer to the screen.

The site lay west of Port St. Lucie, several miles from SR 70, at the end of a narrow access route.

The record named it:

CYPRESS MERIDIAN TELEMETRY ANNEX

Daniels read the words quietly.

"What is a telemetry annex?"

Henry moved the pointer over the individual fields.

"An outlying facility. Probably for measurement data or remote monitoring."

"Probably?"

"Telemetry can mean a lot of things. Radio links. Sensors. Water levels. Weather data. Equipment status. Without the site documents, I don't know what they monitored."

Below the site name were several grayed-out fields.

The service status read:

RETIRED

The assigned region was:

WESTERN FIELD OPERATIONS

The operator field contained no name.

Instead, it read:

TRANSFERRED — SEE SITE FILE

"Transferred to whom?" Daniels asked.

Henry clicked the reference.

A small window opened.

DOCUMENT NOT AVAILABLE IN DATABASE

A file identifier appeared beneath it.

CMTA-SF-12

Daniels wrote it down.

"Could the file be stored somewhere else?"

"Maybe in the old document archive. Maybe on a server that no longer exists. Or only on paper."

Henry scrolled farther.

The location field did not contain an ordinary street address. After SR 70 came an access designation and an internal mileage reference. Coordinates appeared beneath it in an older format.

Daniels transferred them to a mapping application.

A marker appeared west of the last confirmed sighting of the Yukon.

Not directly beside the highway.

A narrow route turned south from SR 70 and continued for several miles between water, low vegetation and large undeveloped parcels. On the current satellite image, no facility could be identified clearly at the end.

Daniels enlarged the area.

"How old is this address?"

"At least as old as the contract."

"And the coordinates?"

"Probably the same."

Daniels switched between the road map and satellite image.

"The access road has no name on the current map."

Henry looked at the legacy record.

"It did here."

He read the field again.

MERIDIAN ACCESS ROAD

Daniels searched the name.

No result.

"It may have been renamed," Henry said. "Or it may never have been an official road name."

"Who worked there?"

Henry opened the service history.

There were only a few entries. Some came from an earlier documentation system and used internal abbreviations no longer explained in the current database.

Every assignment listed the same technician.

WEBER, MICHAEL

"He went there more than once," Daniels said.

"Yes."

"Alone?"

Henry opened the first report.

The field for a second technician was blank.

The next report was the same.

A third contained only a short addition.

FIELD ACCESS CONFIRMED — M.W.

"It looks that way," Henry said.

Daniels opened the individual service descriptions.

One call concerned a failed data link.

Another involved a power-supply unit.

A third required inspection of several old radio modules.

Beneath the technical information was a separate notice for assigned technicians.

SITE SECURITY ORIENTATION REQUIRED BEFORE ACCESS

Two lines followed.

VALID PHOTO IDENTIFICATION REQUIRED

NO ENTRY WITHOUT CURRENT CLEARANCE

Daniels pointed at the screen.

"What clearance?"

Henry read the fields again.

"Probably site-specific authorization. Safety orientation, access rules, conduct on the property."

"Issued by whom?"

The field labeled ORIENTATION AUTHORITY was empty.

So was CLEARANCE ISSUED BY.

"That should be listed," Henry said.

"It isn't."

Henry opened one of the older service reports. It confirmed that Michael had completed the required orientation.

SITE ORIENTATION VERIFIED — M.W.

An issue date was present.

The responsible authority was not.

Daniels opened the next report.

Again, Michael.

Again, confirmed access.

Again, no record of who had granted it.

"Could every technician go there?" Daniels asked.

"Apparently not."

"But Michael could."

Henry looked at the entries.

"Yes."

The final service report contained another note beneath the technical details.

NO COMMERCIAL CELLULAR SERVICE AT SITE

Daniels read it twice.

"No cell service."

"At the time," Henry said. "The report is old."

"The age of a radio installation doesn't necessarily change where it stands."

Henry did not answer.

Daniels looked again at the map. The last confirmed trace of the Yukon ended on SR 70. The old site lay farther west, beyond the areas from which usable camera footage had been obtained.

"Did Michael ever mention this name?"

"Not to me."

"Cypress Meridian? C.M.T.A.? Meridian Access Road?"

Henry slowly shook his head.

Then he looked back at the file identifier above the record.

CMTA-SF-12

"Not like that," he said.

Daniels took out his phone.

"Before anyone goes there, we establish who owns the property now and whether the access road still exists."

"And if no one is responsible?"

Daniels looked at the marker beyond the closely monitored road network.

"Someone is always responsible for land."

He photographed the complete record.

"Find the site file."

Henry copied CMTA-SF-12 into the search field of the old document archive.

The system worked for several seconds.

Four results appeared.

None was the site file itself.

The first was an old invoice. The second was a material request for two radio modules made by a manufacturer that no longer existed. A third entry referred to an earlier revision of the security instructions.

Henry opened the fourth.

It was not a document.

It was an archive index.

SITE FILE: CMTA-SF-12
FORMAT: PHYSICAL / PARTIAL DIGITAL
RETENTION CLASS: PERMANENT
STORAGE LOCATION: WEST FIELD RECORDS
BOX: WFR-04
FOLDER: 12

Daniels read over Henry's shoulder.

"West Field Records."

"That may be the cabinet downstairs."

"May be?"

"There used to be a small storage site for field-service records. Not in this building. It was closed years ago."

"Where?"

Henry tried to remember.

"Near an old workshop. Chesapeake kept replacement parts and antenna components there."

"Does the location still exist?"

"The workshop was sold. I don't know what happened to the files."

Daniels indicated the box number.

"But someone recorded where the original file was stored."

"At the time."

Henry opened the security document from the search results.

It consisted of a single scanned page. C.M.T.A. appeared at the top, followed by a revision number.

Most of the instructions matched the legacy database.

Security orientation before the first visit.

Valid government-issued photo identification.

No access without current site authorization.

Farther down was a certification section.

TECHNICIAN: WEBER, MICHAEL
ORIENTATION STATUS: CURRENT
ACCESS VALID THROUGH: —

The expiration field was blank.

Daniels leaned closer.

"No expiration?"

"Maybe it was carried on a separate credential."

"Is there an identification number?"

Henry scrolled.

SITE CREDENTIAL ID: CM-047

Another field appeared beneath it.

ISSUING AUTHORITY:

Empty.

Daniels pointed.

"Again."

"Yes."

"Is that normal in these old records?"

Henry opened the invoice and compared the structure of the form.

The customer, billing address and responsible department were all complete.

"Not that normal."

Daniels photographed the page.

"Could Chesapeake have issued the authorization itself?"

"Not for someone else's site."

"Then someone trained Michael, checked his identity and issued that number."

Henry looked at CM-047.

"Apparently."

"But no one recorded who."

Henry returned to the archive index. Beneath the storage location was a list of the last documents registered in the physical file.

Several were identified only by number.

Two had descriptions.

ACCESS MAP — REVISION 6

SITE TRANSFER NOTICE

Daniels pointed to the second line.

"We need that."

Henry clicked it.

The same message appeared.

DOCUMENT NOT AVAILABLE IN DATABASE

"Then we find the box," Daniels said.

"If it still exists."

"If it doesn't, we find out who last had it."

Henry wrote down WFR-04 and FOLDER 12.

Daniels stepped away from the desk and took out his phone.

"I will have the property checked in the meantime."

"By whom?"

"Official records first. Ownership, access rights, responsible agency. Then we determine which department can actually go there."

Henry looked at the coordinates in the old database.

"You believe the place still exists."

"I believe Michael knew it and it lies west of the last confirmed sighting."

"That isn't the same thing."

"No."

Daniels dialed.

Henry remained seated in front of the gray interface.

Four letters appeared in several lines of the archive index.

C.M.T.A.

Beside them were contract numbers, file identifiers and closed service records.

Everything about it looked old.

Only the direction was right.

Later that morning, Henry stood at the reception desk looking at the two lines he had copied from the archive system.

WEST FIELD RECORDS
BOX WFR-04 — FOLDER 12

Daniels stood beside him while Henry tried again to remember the old field-service storage site.

"The workshop was sold," Henry said. "I just don't know what happened to the records afterward."

Brenda looked up from her screen.

"Which records?"

Henry turned the note toward her.

"Old field-service files. West Field Records."

Brenda looked at the abbreviation.

"WFR."

It was not a question.

Daniels moved a little closer.

"Does that mean something to you?"

"Of course it does."

Brenda did not need to take the paper from Henry.

"Those were the records from the old field-service site."

Henry rested one hand on the reception counter.

"Do you know where they went?"

Brenda looked at him.

"You weren't with the company then."

"That explains why I don't remember it."

"We cleared the site on a Friday when it was closed down." Brenda indicated the box number. "The files were in gray archive boxes. WFR-01 through at least WFR-09. Maybe more."

Daniels opened his notebook.

"Who organized the move?"

"I did."

She said it as naturally as if he had asked who opened the mail in the morning.

"I drove back and forth several times that day. The heavy equipment cases went in a company vehicle. I did not want the records transported with replacement parts and old tools."

"Where did you take them?"

Brenda did not answer immediately.

Her eyes remained on WFR-04.

"To the records storage assigned during the closure of the old field-service site," she said. "At least until someone decided what had to be retained permanently."

Henry frowned.

"I didn't know Chesapeake had separate storage then."

"As I said, you weren't here."

For a moment, something warmer entered Brenda's expression.

"When we finished, I put out a large box of donuts for everyone in the break room. People had been moving boxes since early morning and nobody had remembered to eat."

Henry gave a faint smile.

"That sounds like you."

"The donuts were gone by the end of the day," Brenda said. "The records were not."

Daniels indicated the box number.

"Do you remember this specific box?"

"Not the contents."

Brenda looked at the note again.

"But I remember the numbers. I entered every box on the transfer list before it left the old site."

"Does that list still exist?"

Brenda was already turning toward the low filing cabinet behind the reception desk.

"Yes."

She opened the top drawer and separated several carefully labeled folders.

"Because I do not throw away important records just because someone decides they are old."

She removed a dark blue folder and placed it on the counter.

Its label read:

OFF-SITE RECORDS — TRANSFER LOGS

The lettering had faded but remained legible.

Inside were carbon copies of old handover forms, transport lists and inventory sheets with handwritten additions.

Henry moved beside her.

"When was the old field-service site closed?"

"Let me look."

Brenda turned several pages. Her movements were calm and certain. She did not need to study how the lists were organized.

She had created them.

Daniels watched her eyes move across date columns, box numbers and destinations.

Then her finger stopped.

"Here."

Nine gray archive boxes were listed individually on the form.

WFR-01
WFR-02
WFR-03
WFR-04

The list continued through WFR-09.

Each number had a brief content description. Some read only FIELD SERVICE RECORDS. Others listed old schematics, site maps or closed maintenance agreements.

Daniels pointed to the fourth line.

"What does that one say?"

Brenda read it aloud.

"Permanent site records. Access documentation. Legacy field locations."

Henry looked at Daniels.

"That's it."

Daniels followed the line to the destination column.

It did not name an outside warehouse.

5904 COMMERCE SIGNAL PARKWAY
LOWER ARCHIVE — CABINET C

Henry frowned.

"Then the box is here."

"It was brought here," Brenda said.

"Why does the old system still say West Field Records?"

"Because nobody changed the original index."

She turned the page over. Later handwritten additions covered the back.

"The boxes were reorganized after the move. Some stayed intact. Others were divided because the records did not all have the same retention period."

Daniels indicated a note beside WFR-04.

"Is that your handwriting?"

"Yes."

Brenda read the line.

REBOXED — PERMANENT RECORDS P-17

Beneath it:

CABINET C — LOWER DRAWER

Henry closed his eyes briefly.

"We were in the right room."

"But the wrong cabinet," Daniels said.

Brenda examined the transfer sheet again.

"Cabinet C stood against the back wall."

"Is it still there?" Henry asked.

"As far as I know."

"Why wasn't the file left with the other field-service records?"

"Because WFR-04 did not contain only routine maintenance reports." Brenda pointed to the description. "Access records and permanent site documents were kept separately. Even after a contract ended, Chesapeake could still be required to retain some of them."

Daniels indicated P-17.

"And that number should be on the new box?"

"It should."

"Should?"

Brenda looked up.

"It has been a long time, Officer Daniels. I can tell you where we put it. I cannot promise no one moved it later."

Henry wrote beneath WFR-04:

P-17 — CABINET C — LOWER DRAWER

Daniels photographed the transfer sheet.

"I will need a copy."

"You can have one," Brenda said. "The original stays here."

Daniels looked at her briefly.

"Agreed."

Brenda smoothed the page with one hand.

"Frank wanted most of the old paper disposed of at the time. Not for any special reason. There was too much of it, and the company was trying to make the new site look clean and modern."

Henry glanced toward the stairs.

"Who decided what stayed?"

"The technicians marked what might still be needed for technical reasons. I checked what had to remain for administrative or contractual reasons."

"And WFR-04?"

Brenda looked at the entry.

"The entire box was marked for permanent retention."

Daniels raised his eyes from the phone.

"By whom?"

Brenda searched the approval section at the bottom of the form.

Her own signature appeared beside RECORDS TRANSFER VERIFIED. Another belonged to the field-service supervisor at the time.

The field beside RETENTION AUTHORITY was blank.

Brenda stopped.

"That's strange."

Henry leaned over the sheet.

"Another empty field."

"I normally would not have marked the entire box permanent without authorization."

"Do you remember who gave it?" Daniels asked.

Brenda looked at the old form.

For a moment, she was back at the field-service site.

Open shelving.

Dusty equipment.

Gray boxes she had carried in her own car.

People moving antenna brackets and heavy tool cases while someone complained about the heat. Later, open donut boxes and paper plates on a break-room table.

But she could not see the person who had told her WFR-04 had to remain.

"No," she said at last. "But I would not have decided it by myself."

Daniels looked at Henry.

"Then let's see what someone wanted preserved."

Brenda did not close the folder.

"Officer Daniels."

He turned back.

"When you open the box, keep the order intact."

"Why?"

"Site records were not filed chronologically. Current access documents were kept at the front. Plans, technical reports and older versions came behind them."

Daniels nodded.

"We won't disturb the order."

Brenda looked at Henry.

"And if my red sticker is still on the lid, it is the right box."

"What does the sticker say?" Henry asked.

"Nothing."

Brenda rested her hand on the closed folder.

"Red meant do not destroy."

Daniels and Henry headed toward the stairwell.

Brenda remained behind the reception desk with the transfer sheet open in front of her.

A single box had carried a different name for years.

P-17

She waited until the stairwell door closed behind them.

Then she looked once more at the empty field beside RETENTION AUTHORITY.

And tried to remember who had insisted that the file remain.

In the living room of Apartment 312, Michael's blue hoodie sat upright on the sofa as though someone were inside it.

Emma had drawn her legs up and wrapped the fabric around her shoulders. The sleeves were too long. Her hands had almost disappeared inside them while the ThinkPad T480 rested on her knees.

A map filled the screen.

SR 70 ran west in an almost straight line. Beyond the last dense developments, street names became less frequent. Residential areas disappeared. Large green and brown spaces, waterways and unfamiliar place names spread between the remaining roads.

Emma zoomed in.

New side roads appeared.

She zoomed in again.

Some of them lost their names.

Sarah sat at the small dining table between the living room and kitchen. A cup of coffee had stood untouched in front of her for almost an hour. Her phone lay beside it, screen facing upward.

"How far west?" Emma asked.

Sarah looked at her.

"What?"

"The last camera."

"They didn't tell us exactly."

"But it was on SR 70."

"Yes."

Emma moved the pointer along the road.

"West of Port St. Lucie can mean anything."

Sarah stood and crossed to the sofa. She sat beside Emma without touching the laptop.

"Officer Daniels said they are continuing to check the route."

"But they don't have another recording."

"Remain reachable. I will contact you after we inspect the site, or sooner if something significant changes."

"And if you don't have service there?"

Daniels looked at the note he had copied from the old service report.

NO COMMERCIAL CELLULAR SERVICE AT SITE

"Then I will call as soon as we are back in range."

Sarah did not answer immediately.

"Officer Daniels."

"Yes?"

"Tell me even if you find nothing."

"I will."

He ended the call and put the phone away.

Brenda looked at him.

"She knows now?"

"She knows we are checking a possible location."

Henry took his jacket from the back of a chair near reception.

"Then we can go."

Daniels checked his notes, the deputy's phone number and the agreed meeting point one final time.

Brenda came around the reception desk.

"Henry."

He stopped.

She did not tell him again to be careful.

Instead, she smoothed a small crease from the sleeve of his jacket, as though for a moment he was not sixty-three years old but one of the young technicians she had once guided through long workdays with archive boxes and donuts.

"Call," she said.

"I will."

Frank stood at the railing on the first floor and watched as Daniels and Henry crossed the lobby.

He said nothing.

Henry raised one hand briefly.

Daniels opened the glass door.

Heat already shimmered over the parking lot.

Michael's car still stood in its usual space.

Henry looked at it as they passed.

Then he got into Daniels' vehicle.

A few minutes later, they left Chesapeake Signal Tech and drove west.

Toward the place where the cameras had stopped.

Toward the place where Michael's phone would no longer have been able to reach anyone.

And toward a name that had waited for years inside a gray archive box.

Cypress Meridian Telemetry Annex.
