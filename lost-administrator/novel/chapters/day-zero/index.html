CHAPTER 1

Day Zero

Friday, July 31, 2026

06:30 AM

A single beep cut through the silence.

Before the alarm clock could sound a second time, Michael reached over and switched it off.

Silence returned.

He sat on the edge of the bed for a moment, rubbing the sleep from his eyes before straightening the blanket with a few practiced movements. Not perfectly. Just the way someone living alone left a room they planned to return to that evening.

The house was quiet.

Emma was spending the morning with Sarah. Later, Sarah would take her to Chloe’s, and Michael would pick her up there at 3:05 that afternoon.

He stepped into the hallway and, almost without thinking, glanced into Emma's room as he passed.

Her bed was neatly made.

Colored markers rested on the desk beside an unfinished sketch.

Everything waited exactly where she had left it.

Satisfied, Michael continued downstairs.

The aquarium greeted him with its familiar, gentle hum.

He paused for only a second.

Filter running.

Water moving.

Grissom was nowhere in sight, which usually meant he was tucked beneath his favorite piece of driftwood.

Status nominal.

Michael smiled faintly.

The coffee machine started with a soft mechanical click.

Michael reached for the ceramic mug waiting in the cupboard above it.

The words on its side read:

Everything is fine.

He poured the coffee, added a small splash of milk and took the first sip while it was still cooling.

Warm enough to taste.

Cool enough to drink.

Two slices of bread disappeared into the toaster.

By the time they popped up, the coffee had reached exactly the temperature he liked.

Peanut butter.

Both slices.

No jam.

No elaborate breakfast.

Those belonged to the mornings when Emma was home.

Pancakes.

Scrambled eggs.

Sometimes bacon.

Breakfast somehow always became an event when Emma sat at the table.

Today, two slices of toast were enough.

Michael carried his breakfast to the table without bringing his phone.

It still rested on the kitchen counter where he had left it the night before.

There would be enough screens waiting for him at Chesapeake.

Instead, his eyes wandered to the aquarium across the room.

The gentle current moved the plants ever so slightly.

Nothing seemed to be awake yet.

Grissom remained hidden beneath the driftwood while the house slowly came alive around him.

Michael took another sip of coffee.

Everything was exactly as it should be.

Michael carried the empty mug back to the kitchen.

A quick rinse under running water.

The plate followed.

He placed both into the dishwasher and closed it with a quiet click.

One last look around.

Coffee machine off.

Aquarium filter running.

Kitchen lights off.

His phone disappeared into his right pocket.

The Leatherman found its familiar place in the left.

His Timberline Lodge pen followed.

Finally, he picked up the car keys from the wooden bowl beside the front door.

Ready.

The front door clicked shut behind him.

Warm Florida air greeted him the moment he stepped outside.

The neighborhood was already beginning to wake up.

Somewhere in the distance, a lawn mower coughed to life.

A pair of mourning doves called from the trees lining the street.

Michael locked the front door with a single turn of the key.

No second check.

He never needed one.

His car waited on the driveway in front of the garage.

He had meant to park inside again.

Eventually.

The left side of the garage was occupied by several neatly stacked boxes waiting to be sorted.

Network cables.

Power supplies.

Old switches.

Adapters he was certain would become useful again someday.

The right side belonged to Emma.

Her bicycle leaned exactly where she had left it.

A few moving boxes stood nearby, filled with things that had never quite found a permanent place after the separation.

Michael had considered clearing the space more than once.

Then Emma would come home, wheel her bike inside without thinking about it, and somehow the garage felt exactly the way it was supposed to.

He unlocked the driver's door, slipped into the seat and closed the door behind him.

For just a moment...

...the world became quiet again.

Michael fastened his seat belt and placed his phone in the center console before pressing the start button.

The engine came to life almost soundlessly.

His hands settled on the steering wheel.

One last glance into the rear-view mirror.

Always.

Satisfied, he shifted into reverse and slowly backed onto the quiet street.

Only after leaving the neighborhood behind did he reach for the radio.

The opening notes of a familiar synthwave track filled the car.

Not loud.

Just enough to accompany the drive.

Fifteen minutes.

Long enough to leave home behind.

Short enough to arrive before the day had fully settled in.

The city was slowly coming to life.

Morning sunlight spilled across quiet streets lined with palms and live oaks.

Traffic slowed briefly near the next intersection.

Michael eased off the accelerator.

There was no reason to hurry.

A moment later, the road cleared.

Traffic remained light.

A few contractors' vans.

A delivery truck.

Someone walking a golden retriever along the sidewalk.

Just another Friday.

The synthwave track faded into the next.

Michael knew the playlist well enough that he rarely looked at the display anymore.

Most mornings followed the same rhythm.

By the time the third song began, Chesapeake Signal Tech was usually only a few turns away.

By the time Michael turned into the employee parking lot, the third song had just ended.

Only a handful of cars were there.

He guided his car into the same space he used every morning, straightened the wheel, and shut off the engine.

The music stopped with it.

For a few seconds, the sudden quiet felt almost as complete as it had inside the house.

Michael removed his phone from the center console and slipped it into his right pocket.

The familiar weight of the Leatherman remained in the left.

Keys in hand, he stepped out into the growing heat.

The Chesapeake Signal Tech sign stood ahead of him, unchanged and unremarkable.

He had walked past it thousands of times.

Michael locked the car and headed toward the entrance.

Another ordinary workday was waiting inside.

Michael let himself into the building and stepped into the quiet reception area.

At this hour, Chesapeake Signal Tech sounded different.

No phones.

No conversations drifting between offices.

Only the steady breath of the air-conditioning and the faint electrical hum of equipment that never truly slept.

Michael preferred it that way.

He continued past the empty reception desk and toward the break room. The overhead lights came on one section at a time as he moved through the hallway.

The coffee maker stood exactly where it had the evening before.

Someone had left the empty pot on the warming plate.

Michael removed it, rinsed it once, and filled the machine with fresh water.

While the coffee brewed, he opened the cabinet above the counter and reached for his work mug.

White ceramic.

A red heart.

Large black letters.

I ❤️ RoboDad.

Emma had given it to him two birthdays ago.

The lettering had begun to fade slightly near the handle, but Michael always positioned the mug so the words faced outward.

He added a small splash of milk and carried the coffee toward his desk.

The office was still quiet when he sat down.

For a few minutes, it would remain that way.

Michael woke his monitors, set the mug beside the keyboard, and began reviewing the overnight system reports.

Nothing urgent.

A completed backup.

Two routine warnings.

One failed login from an employee who had almost certainly forgotten the new password policy again.

Friday.

From the front of the building came the sound of the main door opening.

A moment later, Brenda Montgomery appeared at the end of the hallway with her handbag over one shoulder and a travel cup in her hand.

She spotted Michael at his desk and smiled.

“Morning, Micha.”

“Morning, Brenda.”

“You beat me again.”

Michael glanced at the clock in the corner of his monitor.

“Three minutes.”

“Of course you counted.”

“I looked at the clock.”

Brenda shook her head, still smiling, and continued toward the reception desk.

Michael returned his attention to the overnight reports.

Behind him, Brenda began opening the narrow windows along the rear wall of the foyer. Warm morning air slipped into the building and briefly competed with the air-conditioning before settling into a faint current through the reception area.

She placed her handbag beneath the desk, straightened a stack of visitor forms and adjusted the small ceramic honey pot that held the pens near the sign-in book.

Everything had its place.

Michael understood that.

A few minutes later, he heard the coffee maker begin another cycle in the break room.

That pot was not for the office.

It was for Frank.

Black, prepared before he arrived, the same way Brenda had made it for years.

Michael opened the first warning from the overnight report.

A storage threshold on one of the internal archive volumes had crossed eighty percent. Nothing urgent. The scheduled cleanup had already reduced it to seventy-four.

He checked the timestamps, confirmed the job had completed without errors and closed the report.

The second warning came from a temperature sensor in the server room.

A brief increase at 2:13 AM.

Two degrees above normal.

Back within range three minutes later.

Michael opened the environmental log and compared it with the air-conditioning cycle.

Expected behavior.

He marked the warning as reviewed.

The failed login required even less investigation.

Three incorrect attempts against the company mail server from Steve Harper’s home address, followed by a successful login forty seconds later.

Michael leaned back slightly.

“New password,” he said to the empty office.

From the reception desk, Brenda answered without looking up.

“Steve?”

“Steve.”

“I’ll add patience to the supply order.”

Michael took a sip from the RoboDad mug.

“That may require executive approval.”

Brenda laughed quietly.

The front doors opened again.

Footsteps crossed the foyer at an unhurried pace, followed by the soft scrape of a shoulder bag being adjusted.

Henry Sullivan appeared in the hallway, looking as though he had been awake long enough to function but not long enough to approve of it.

He raised one hand as he approached.

“Morning, morning, Base-Station.”

“Morning, Henry.”

Henry stopped beside Michael’s desk and glanced at the two reports still open on the monitors.

“Anything burn down overnight?”

“Not yet.”

“Good. I left my marshmallows at home.”

Henry continued to his own desk, dropped the bag beside his chair and lowered himself into it with a quiet sigh.

His computer took longer to wake than Michael’s had.

Henry watched the loading screen for several seconds.

“Every morning, it acts surprised that I came back.”

“It remembers yesterday.”

“That explains the hostility.”

Michael took another sip from the RoboDad mug.

Across the office, Brenda laughed softly without looking up from the reception desk.

Henry’s desktop finally appeared.

He opened the ticket system.

Michael returned to the overnight reports.

For several minutes, neither of them spoke.

They did not need to.

The office slowly came online around them.

At 7:32, the front doors opened with considerably more energy than before.

Steve Harper entered with a premium backpack over one shoulder, a travel mug in one hand and the newest smartphone in the other. His smartwatch lit up before he had taken three steps into the foyer.

“Good morning, team.”

“Morning, Steve,” Brenda said.

Henry raised one hand without turning away from his monitor.

Michael glanced at the time in the corner of his monitor.

Steve noticed.

“I know. Two minutes later than yesterday.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You looked at the clock.”

“I was checking the time.”

“That is exactly what someone says after documenting my arrival.”

Steve placed his phone onto the wireless charger at his desk. A thin ring of light appeared beneath it.

Michael returned to the overnight report.

“Your mail account survived,” he said.

Steve paused halfway through removing his backpack.

“I never doubted it.”

“Three failed logins at 2:06.”

“New password. Old muscle memory.”

“Forty seconds later, successful login.”

Steve pointed at him.

“See? Self-healing user infrastructure.”

Henry looked across the divider.

“That’s not what that means.”

“It could be.”

“It isn’t.”

Steve lowered himself into his chair and woke all three monitors. Two displayed the company dashboard. The third opened to a carefully arranged presentation slide bearing the title:

CUSTOMER STATUS EXPERIENCE — CONCEPT PREVIEW

Steve leaned slightly toward Henry.

“I sent you the new prototype last night.”

Henry stared at his own screen.

“I was asleep last night.”

“That’s why email is asynchronous.”

“That word doesn’t make me want to open it more.”

Steve smiled, apparently taking this as encouragement.

Before he could continue, the main telephone rang at reception.

Brenda answered.

“Chesapeake Signal Tech, good morning. This is Brenda.”

She listened for several seconds, then looked toward the office.

“Steve? Harbor Point is asking about the overnight service warning.”

Steve’s expression changed immediately.

The restless enthusiasm remained, but it found a direction.

He reached for his headset.

“I’ve got them.”

Michael opened the relevant system log.

Henry pulled up the customer environment.

Steve accepted the transferred call.

“Good morning, this is Steve Harper. I’ve got the technical team with me, and we’re looking at your system now.”

His voice was calm.

Clear.

Reassuring.

The person on the other end stopped speaking quite so quickly.

Michael and Henry exchanged a brief glance.

This was why Steve was here.

Michael opened the customer dashboard and found the warning almost immediately.

A single monitoring check had failed during the night.

The next one had completed normally.

No interruption.

No lost data.

No customer impact.

Henry leaned closer to his screen.

“Agent missed one heartbeat.”

Michael checked the timestamps.

“During the scheduled service restart.”

Henry nodded.

“Expected.”

Steve listened to the caller for another moment.

“I understand why that message looked concerning,” he said. “The good news is that the system itself remained available. What you saw was a monitoring delay during routine maintenance, not an outage.”

He paused.

“No, you didn’t miss anything. We’re confirming the logs now.”

Michael typed a short note into the ticket.

Henry added the maintenance reference.

Steve glanced toward them.

Michael raised one finger.

Confirmed.

Steve turned back to the call.

“We’ve verified it. Everything completed normally, and there was no interruption to your service.”

The tension left his shoulders as the person on the other end responded.

“Absolutely. I’ll send you a written summary in the next few minutes so you have it for your records.”

Another pause.

“You’re welcome. Have a good Friday.”

Steve ended the call and set his headset down.

“Harbor Point has survived the night.”

Henry looked at the clock.

“Remarkable.”

“They were worried.”

“They always are.”

“That’s why they call us.”

Michael closed the system log.

“And why they keep paying us.”

Steve pointed at him.

“Exactly. Customer retention through emotional stability.”

Henry leaned back in his chair.

“Put that on a slide.”

Steve’s eyes shifted toward the presentation already open on his third monitor.

“I could.”

“That wasn’t permission.”

Steve smiled and began typing the customer summary.

The office continued filling around them.

Doors opened.

Chairs rolled across the floor.

The first internal messages appeared in the team channel.

Someone in accounting discovered that the printer on the second floor was out of paper and reported it as a network failure.

Someone else replied that they had added paper.

The original reporter asked whether the network had been repaired.

Henry read the message twice.

Then he slowly turned toward Michael.

Michael kept his eyes on his monitor.

“No.”

“I haven’t said anything.”

“You were about to.”

“I was going to suggest we disable messaging before eight.”

“Denied.”

“Worth a try.”

At the reception desk, Brenda greeted each arriving employee by name.

She remembered who had taken Friday off, who was expecting a delivery and whose daughter had started college applications that week.

Within twenty minutes, the quiet building Michael had entered was gone.

Phones rang.

Keyboards clicked.

A printer began somewhere down the hallway.

Then stopped.

Then began again.

Michael lifted the RoboDad mug and found it nearly empty.

The day had started.

A few minutes later, the front doors opened once more.

Frank Kessler entered without hurry.

No phone in his hand.

No briefcase swinging at his side.

Just a thin folder tucked beneath one arm and the calm expression of a man who had learned long ago that arriving faster rarely improved anything.

“Good morning, Frank,” Brenda said.

“Morning, Montgomery.”

She had already placed his white porcelain mug beside the coffee maker.

Frank picked it up, tested the temperature with a careful sip and gave Brenda a small nod.

Still hot.

Exactly right.

His attention shifted toward the hallway, where the accounting printer had resumed producing pages after its alleged network failure.

“Recovered?” he asked.

Brenda glanced in the same direction.

“Paper was installed.”

Frank considered that.

“Remarkable progress.”

From the office, Henry raised his voice just enough to carry down the hallway.

“We’re documenting the root cause.”

“See that you do, Sullivan.”

There was no sharpness in Frank’s reply.

Only the faintest suggestion that he might be enjoying himself.

He walked into the technical office, coffee in one hand and the folder still beneath his arm.

“Morning, Weber.”

“Morning, Frank.”

“Sullivan.”

“Still here.”

Frank looked toward Steve’s desk.

“Steve.”

“Good morning, Frank.”

Steve straightened slightly without appearing to realize that he had done so.

Frank’s eyes moved across the three active monitors in front of him.

“New project?”

Steve’s face brightened.

“Customer status experience. I sent Henry the prototype last night.”

Frank looked at Henry.

Henry remained focused on his screen.

“I was asleep.”

“So I’ve heard.”

Frank took another sip of coffee.

“Show me when it is ready, Steve.”

“It’s nearly ready.”

“Then show me when it is ready.”

Steve nodded, taking the instruction far more seriously than the words required.

Frank continued toward the staircase leading to the executive offices.

At the bottom step, he paused.

“Anything I need to know?”

Michael glanced at the overnight reports.

“One expected monitoring interruption. No customer impact. Archive storage returned below threshold. Steve’s password works again.”

Frank looked toward Steve.

Steve raised both hands.

“Self-corrected.”

“Good.”

Frank climbed the stairs at the same unhurried pace with which he had entered.

The office returned to its normal rhythm behind him.

Henry waited until Frank had disappeared from view.

“He looked at my screen.”

Steve turned toward him.

“And?”

“I may have to open your email now.”

Steve smiled.

“I knew the executive visibility would help.”

Michael lifted his mug.

“Careful. That sounds like a slide title.”

Steve glanced toward the presentation on his third monitor.

“It does, actually.”

Henry closed his eyes.

“Base-Station, stop encouraging him.”

“I didn’t.”

“You existed in the same room. That was enough.”

For the next half hour, the office settled into its usual Friday rhythm.

Michael reviewed the remaining overnight jobs while Henry worked through the open maintenance queue beside him.

Steve finished the written summary for Harbor Point, reread it twice and sent it to Michael for approval.

Michael opened the message.

Three paragraphs.

A heading.

Two bullet lists.

A blue status banner.

He removed the word critical from the first sentence and sent it back.

Steve’s reply appeared almost immediately.

Why?

Michael typed:

Because it wasn’t.

Three dots appeared.

Disappeared.

Appeared again.

Fair.

Michael closed the message.

Across the room, Henry watched the exchange without turning his head.

“How many adjectives?”

“Only one.”

“Slow morning.”

The internal phone on Michael’s desk rang.

He picked it up.

“IT. Michael.”

A woman from accounting explained that her computer had stopped recognizing the shared printer again.

Michael listened without interrupting.

“Does it show an error?”

She read the message aloud.

Michael looked toward the second-floor printer visible through the glass partition at the far end of the hallway.

Its status light was green.

“Can you open the print queue?”

A pause.

“I don’t know where that is.”

“That’s fine.”

Michael stood.

“I’ll come over.”

He set the receiver down and reached automatically for the RoboDad mug before noticing it was empty.

Henry looked up.

“Network failure?”

“Accounting printer.”

“Same thing, apparently.”

Michael crossed the office and walked down the hallway.

The employee was waiting beside her desk when he arrived, already apologizing.

“I probably clicked something.”

“Probably.”

She looked even more worried.

Michael pulled the spare chair closer and sat beside her rather than taking her place.

“Show me what you did.”

She opened the document, selected the printer and pressed Print.

Nothing happened.

Michael examined the queue.

Nine copies of the same twelve-page report waited behind a paused document from the previous afternoon.

He pointed at the first entry.

“This one stopped the queue.”

“Oh.”

“You didn’t cause it.”

He removed the failed job and resumed printing.

The machine at the end of the hallway came to life.

Paper began sliding into the output tray.

The employee smiled with visible relief.

“So it wasn’t the network?”

“No.”

“Should I cancel the other nine copies?”

Michael looked at the growing stack of paper.

“Yes.”

She clicked Cancel All.

The printer stopped after page twenty-seven.

Michael stood.

“I’ll recycle those.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I’m already here.”

He collected the unwanted pages and carried them toward the recycling bin.

Behind him, the employee returned to her work without feeling foolish.

That mattered more than fixing the printer.

Michael returned to his desk carrying the abandoned pages beneath one arm.

Henry glanced at the stack.

“Network debris?”

“User-generated.”

“More dangerous.”

Michael dropped the pages into the recycling bin and sat down.

His mug was still empty.

Before he could stand again, Brenda appeared beside the desk and placed a fresh cup of coffee in front of him.

Same mug.

Small splash of milk.

Michael looked up.

“I was going to get that.”

“I know.”

Brenda continued toward reception before he could say anything else.

Henry watched her leave.

“She has administrator privileges.”

“Higher.”

“Thought so.”

Michael took a sip.

The temperature was already right.

The rest of the morning passed without anything deserving the word emergency.

A user account needed unlocking.

One backup report contained a malformed date field.

A customer asked whether scheduled maintenance could be completed without scheduling maintenance.

Steve handled that conversation.

Michael and Henry listened from their desks as he explained, with considerable patience, that work requiring a service interruption could not also guarantee uninterrupted service.

After the call, Steve removed his headset.

“They would like us to take everything offline without taking anything offline.”

Henry nodded.

“Modern infrastructure.”

“I offered a maintenance window.”

“Reckless.”

Steve looked toward Michael.

“You’re both impossible.”

“Redundant,” Michael said.

“That isn’t better.”

By late morning, sunlight filled the office through the eastern windows. The air-conditioning worked harder against the growing heat outside, and the printer in accounting had managed almost two uninterrupted hours without becoming a network problem.

Michael finished documenting the archive cleanup and moved the ticket into review.

At 10:42, a small notification appeared in the corner of his screen.

New message.

The sender’s name made him stop before he read the subject.

Emma Weber.

The message had been addressed to the family alias she always used.

robodad@michamailgate.com

From there, it had been forwarded automatically into his Chesapeake inbox, just as Michael had configured it years ago.

Henry noticed the change in his expression.

“Data-Stream?”

Michael nodded.

The subject line read:

New printer cartridge?

Steve looked over from his desk.

“Personal support ticket?”

“Priority user,” Michael said.

Henry leaned back.

“Escalate immediately.”

Michael opened the message.

From: Emma Weber <emma@michamailgate.com>
To: Michael Weber <robodad@michamailgate.com>
Subject: New printer cartridge?

Hey Robodad,

Chloe and I were just about to print the new Major Tom pages, but the cartridge in her parents’ printer is empty.

Do you still have one of the same kind in the cabinet in your office at home?

I didn’t want to ride over and start looking through your things without asking. :)

Emma

Michael read the final sentence again.

It was her home too.

She still asked.

He clicked Reply.

Yes.

Office cabinet, top shelf. Gray box marked PRINTER. Take the unopened cartridge on the left.

Helmet. Lock the door behind you.

I’ll pick you up at 3:05.

Dad

He read the message once, then sent it.

Henry glanced across the divider.

“Resolved?”

“Field replacement pending.”

“Qualified technician?”

Michael looked back at Emma’s message.

“Highly motivated.”

A reply arrived less than a minute later.

Thanks, Dad.

Chloe says thank you too.

Major Tom reports that the printing systems may still be recoverable.

Michael read it once and moved the message into the folder marked EMMA.

There were hundreds of messages inside.

Drawings.

Questions.

Photographs of Byte sleeping in impossible positions.

Requests for technical assistance that ranged from recovering a damaged sketch file to explaining why the moon sometimes appeared during the day.

Michael kept all of them.

Henry noticed the folder before Michael closed it.

“How large is the Data-Stream archive now?”

“Four point eight gigabytes.”

Henry looked impressed.

“Mostly mission-critical?”

“Entirely.”

Michael checked his calendar.

The final entry of the afternoon was already there.

3:05 PM — PICK UP EMMA — VANCE HOUSE

He had created it three days earlier, although he did not need the reminder.

When Michael said 3:05, he meant 3:05.

The notification was only redundancy.

He returned to the maintenance queue.

By eleven, the archive warning had been closed, two user accounts had been restored and Steve’s customer summary had acquired a fourth revision despite containing almost exactly the same information as the first.

Steve stood beside Michael’s desk, reading from his tablet.

“I changed ‘service interruption’ to ‘brief maintenance-related visibility event.’”

Michael looked at him.

“Why?”

“It sounds less alarming.”

“It sounds less understandable.”

Steve lowered the tablet.

“Fair.”

Henry turned in his chair.

“What happened to the system?”

“One monitoring check failed during a restart,” Michael said.

Henry pointed toward Steve’s tablet.

“Write that.”

“That’s very direct.”

“That’s why people might understand it.”

Steve returned to his desk, still unconvinced.

As noon approached, the smell of warmed food began drifting from the break room.

Someone opened the refrigerator.

Someone else complained that their lunch had disappeared.

Brenda announced from reception that the container in question had been moved to the lower shelf because it had been blocking the milk.

The crisis ended without technical intervention.

Henry checked the time.

“Lunch?”

Michael finished the line he was typing before answering.

“In five.”

“You said that ten minutes ago.”

“I wasn’t finished ten minutes ago.”

“You’re not finished now.”

Michael saved the configuration file.

“I am now.”

Henry stood and reached for his wallet.

Steve looked up immediately.

“Where are we going?”

“We?” Henry asked.

Steve was already locking his workstation.

“I heard lunch.”

“You hear validation from three rooms away.”

“That too.”

Michael picked up the RoboDad mug and carried it toward the break room.

Brenda was standing beside the counter, cutting a cinnamon roll in half.

She placed one portion on a small plate and slid it toward him.

Michael looked at it.

“I’m fine.”

“I know.”

“That usually means I don’t need emergency pastry.”

“It isn’t an emergency.”

Brenda picked up the other half.

“It’s Friday.”

Michael accepted the plate.

There was no useful argument against that.

Michael carried the plate into the break room.

Henry had already claimed the chair nearest the window. Steve stood at the counter, reading the instructions on a container as though they contained a firmware update.

“It says two minutes,” Steve said.

“In a microwave?” Henry asked.

“That is generally how microwave instructions work.”

“You’ve been staring at it for three.”

“I’m accounting for variables.”

Michael sat across from Henry and opened the lunch he had brought from home.

Nothing elaborate.

A sandwich, an apple and a small container of pretzels.

Henry looked at the cinnamon roll beside it.

“Balanced.”

“Brenda.”

“That explains the medical intervention.”

Steve placed his container into the microwave and entered two minutes and thirty seconds.

Michael noticed.

“The instructions said two.”

Steve pressed Start.

“I prefer a safety margin.”

“That’s the opposite of a safety margin,” Henry said.

The microwave began turning.

Steve leaned against the counter.

“So. The status prototype.”

Henry unwrapped his sandwich.

“No.”

“You haven’t seen it.”

“I’m preserving that.”

“It would take five minutes.”

“Then why is the presentation thirty-two slides?”

Steve looked genuinely offended.

“Context.”

Michael ate half the cinnamon roll before opening his sandwich.

Steve turned toward him.

“You understand what I’m trying to do.”

“Yes.”

Steve waited.

Michael took another bite.

“And?”

“The customers need clearer information.”

“Exactly.”

“The dashboard doesn’t need an animated loading sequence.”

Steve folded his arms.

“It communicates activity.”

“It communicates waiting.”

Henry pointed at Michael with his sandwich.

“That’s going on a shirt.”

The microwave beeped.

Steve opened the door, immediately pulled his hand back and stared at Michael.

“Too hot.”

Michael continued eating.

“Safety margin.”

Henry laughed into his coffee.

For the next several minutes, the conversation moved without direction.

A customer who insisted on receiving status updates every fifteen minutes but never read them.

The second-floor printer.

Whether Steve’s wireless desk lamp genuinely required an account.

It did.

Henry considered that sufficient evidence against modern civilization.

Michael mostly listened.

He spoke when there was something worth adding and remained quiet when there was not.

Nobody at the table mistook the silence for disinterest.

His phone vibrated once in his right pocket.

He left it there until he had finished eating.

Only after placing the empty container beside the sink did he check the screen.

A message from Emma.

Found it.

Chloe’s printer accepts the offering.

Major Tom lives.

Below the text was a photograph of several freshly printed comic pages spread across the Vances’ dining table. Major Tom floated beside a brightly colored planet covered in server racks. Chloe’s handwritten dialogue filled the speech bubbles.

Michael enlarged the image.

One of the servers had a small red warning light.

Beside it, Major Tom said:

HAVE YOU TRIED TURNING THE PLANET OFF AND ON AGAIN?

Michael smiled.

Henry noticed.

“Successful field replacement?”

Michael showed him the screen.

Henry adjusted his glasses.

“Sound troubleshooting.”

Steve leaned between them.

“The visual hierarchy is excellent.”

Henry looked at him.

“She’s twelve.”

“That doesn’t make me wrong.”

Michael typed a reply.

Confirmed.

Planetary systems operating within specifications.

See you at 3:05.

He put the phone away.

Steve carried his overheated lunch to the table.

“Three-oh-five exactly?”

“Exactly,” Henry said before Michael could answer.

Steve nodded.

“Right. Stupid question.”

Michael rinsed his lunch container and placed it in the dishwasher.

When they returned to the technical office, the morning had fully given way to afternoon.

Sunlight pressed against the windows.

The parking lot shimmered in the heat.

Michael checked the time in the corner of his monitor.

12:18 PM.

Two hours and twenty-seven minutes until he needed to leave.

Plenty of time.

He opened the next ticket.

The next ticket came from operations.

A scheduled file transfer had completed, but the confirmation report showed one directory as empty.

Michael opened the transfer log.

The files were there.

The report was wrong.

Henry leaned toward his second monitor.

“Parser?”

“Probably.”

“New version?”

“Wednesday.”

Henry made a quiet sound that carried several years of experience.

Michael compared the raw output with the previous version. One field separator had changed from a colon to a semicolon. The transfer script had accepted it. The reporting script had not.

He corrected the parser, tested it against the last seven reports and watched the missing directory reappear.

Henry checked the result from his own workstation.

“Good.”

Michael committed the change.

“Temporary fix?”

“Compatible with both formats.”

“Better.”

Michael added a note to the ticket explaining what had changed and why. No blame. No speculation. Just the fault, the correction and the verification.

Across the office, Steve was speaking to a customer whose project manager had discovered the phrase data migration and decided it meant immediate disaster.

“No, nothing is being deleted,” Steve said patiently. “We’re copying the existing records into the new structure. The original data remains available throughout the process.”

He listened.

“That is a reasonable question.”

Henry looked toward Michael.

“It wasn’t.”

Michael kept typing.

“It was to them.”

Steve continued without any sign that he had heard.

“Yes. We’ll verify the record count before and after the migration, and you’ll receive the comparison report.”

The caller’s voice softened enough that it no longer carried through Steve’s headset.

Henry returned to his screen.

“Human firewall.”

Michael nodded once.

At 12:43, Brenda appeared in the doorway holding a padded envelope.

“Delivery for technical.”

Steve raised his hand.

“Could be mine.”

“It weighs more than a phone accessory.”

Steve lowered his hand.

Brenda passed the envelope to Michael.

The return label belonged to a customer in Fort Pierce. Inside was a failed solid-state drive wrapped in an antistatic bag, accompanied by a handwritten note asking whether anything could still be recovered.

Michael read the note twice.

Henry saw the address.

“Same office with the accounting archive?”

“Yes.”

“Backup?”

“Partial.”

Henry sighed.

Michael did not.

He examined the drive without opening the bag.

“No visible damage.”

“Controller?”

“Maybe.”

Steve ended his call and rolled his chair closer.

“Can we recover it?”

Michael looked at the note again.

A payroll archive.

Employee tax records.

Years of scanned documents.

“Maybe,” he said. “First we image it. No repairs until we know what it still gives us.”

Steve nodded, though the answer had not been meant as instruction for him.

Michael carried the package to the secured workbench and entered the drive into the intake log.

Customer.

Serial number.

Date received.

Condition.

Every field mattered.

He attached a temporary evidence label to the bag and placed it inside the locked cabinet beneath the bench.

Henry watched from his desk.

“Today?”

“After the afternoon checks.”

“You’re leaving at two forty-five.”

“I know.”

“That sounded like a reminder.”

“It was.”

Michael returned to his workstation.

At 1:02, Frank came down from the executive floor carrying his empty white mug.

He stopped near Steve’s desk.

“Status prototype.”

Steve turned immediately.

“Ready for review.”

Henry looked at Michael.

Michael looked at his monitor.

Frank noticed both.

“Nearly ready?”

Steve hesitated.

“One final pass.”

“Then it is not ready.”

“No.”

Frank gave a small nod.

“Good answer.”

He continued toward the break room.

Steve waited until he was out of earshot.

“I think that went well.”

Henry opened a new ticket.

“You admitted reality. Management appreciates novelty.”

Michael took the final sip from his coffee.

The mug had gone cold.

He carried it to the sink, rinsed it once and placed it in the dishwasher.

When he returned, Steve was standing beside Henry’s desk with the tablet again.

“I reduced it to twenty-four slides.”

Henry did not look up.

“That is not the achievement you think it is.”

“The animation is gone.”

Michael sat down.

Henry glanced toward him.

“Progress?”

“Observable.”

Steve smiled.

“I’ll take that.”

At 1:17, the afternoon system checks began automatically.

Michael watched the status list populate one line at a time.

Mail relay.

Available.

Customer gateway.

Available.

Archive replication.

Available.

Environmental controls.

Available.

Backup verification.

Running.

Nothing required intervention.

The office had settled again after lunch. Conversations became quieter. The phones rang less often. Even the accounting printer appeared to have accepted the limits of its existence.

Michael opened the schedule for the rest of the day.

One configuration review.

The failed drive intake.

Final backup verification.

Then Emma.

At 1:26, the verification completed.

All scheduled sets valid.

Michael marked the result and closed the report.

For the first time since lunch, no warning icons remained on either monitor.

Henry leaned back.

“Clean board.”

“For now.”

“You could enjoy it.”

“I am.”

Henry studied Michael’s expression.

“That’s enjoying it?”

“Yes.”

“I’d hate to see celebration.”

Michael checked the time in the corner of his monitor.

1:28 PM.

He reached for his car keys.

“I’ll be right back.”

Henry raised one hand without looking away from his monitor.

Michael slipped the keys into his palm and walked toward the front of the building.

Behind him, the office continued working.
