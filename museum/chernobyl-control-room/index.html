Graphite moderator

D · Documented

Graphite slows neutrons so that they can sustain fission more effectively. It remains in the core when cooling water turns to steam.

Engineering note & source

Moderation and cooling are separate jobs in this design. Graphite is not fuel; the water also absorbs some neutrons. The balance changes with reactor conditions.

INSAG-7 §§2.1–2.2; NEA, reactor description ↗
Why steam can change the chain reaction

Water cools the fuel and absorbs some neutrons. Graphite supplies most of the moderation. If water becomes less dense as steam forms, absorption can fall while the graphite remains. In the accident configuration this could increase reactivity. Fuel temperature also gives feedback; the net response depends on the state and spatial distribution. The exhibit draws this relationship without solving reactor physics.

Engineering note & source

MW(th) describes heat produced in the reactor. MW(e) describes electrical output. Mixing these units makes historical comparisons misleading. A single power number also hides spatial variations inside the core.

СКАЛА

SYSTEM INSPECTOR

SKALA

D · Documented

SKALA acquired and processed plant information. DREG recorded selected signals; PRIZMA calculated reactor parameters that were not directly measured. These functions did not form one all-powerful safety computer.

Technical detail & source

INSAG-7 describes different recording cycles, low-priority DREG work and gaps associated with system restarts. A magnetic-tape snapshot at 01:22:30 was later used for calculations; the results were not available to personnel at that moment.

INSAG-7 Annex I §§1-4.3.2, 1-4.5 ↗
01:22:30 — what did the operators actually know?

Annex I describes a magnetic-tape state recording at 01:22:30, then calculations performed after the accident at Smolensk with PRIZMA-ANALOG. The calculated operating reactivity margin must not be mistaken for an instrument reading seen by the crew at that time. ORM is a reactivity-equivalent quantity, not a simple head count of inserted rods.

INSAG-7 Annex I §1-4.5 · printed pp.64–65 ↗

25.04.1986 · An electrical test in a nuclear plant

D · Documented

Before a maintenance shutdown, a turbine rundown test was planned. It concerned the interval between a loss of electrical supply and emergency diesel power becoming available. A grid-related delay prolonged operation at reduced power.

The calendar compresses many hours into one chapter. It is not a real-time replay.

INSAG-7 §3, Annex I Table I-I; NEA chapter I ↗

26.04 · ~00:28 · An unintended power reduction

D · Documented

During a change of power-control mode the thermal power fell to about 30 MW, according to the operating-log chronology. The causes and reactor response cannot be captured by a single “operator error” label.

The source distinguishes thermal power from neutron-power indication. The exhibit shows thermal power only.

26.04 · ~01:03 · Low power, changing neutron balance

D · Documented

Power was raised and stabilized at about 200 MW(th). Xenon absorption, the spatial power distribution and the rod configuration mattered. Low power alone is not a complete explanation of the accident.

The patterned core is a schematic, not a calculated xenon or neutron-flux distribution.

INSAG-7 §§2–3; Annex I Table I-I, printed p.54 ↗

26.04 · 01:22:30 · A recording is not yet a calculation

R · Reconstructed

SKALA recorded a reactor-state snapshot on magnetic tape. The operating reactivity margin for that state was calculated after the accident using PRIZMA-ANALOG. It was not a live result available to the control-room team at 01:22:30.

The timestamp is documented; the inferred margin belongs to post-accident reconstruction. No retrospective value is drawn onto an operator instrument.

INSAG-7 Annex I §1-4.5, printed pp.64–65 ↗

26.04 · 01:23:04 · The turbine rundown begins

D · Documented

Closure of the turbine-generator No. 8 stop valves marks the test start. Changes in pump delivery, steam formation and reactivity must be considered together. The diagram shows their relationship, not a numerical prediction.

This is a documented anchor event. A large power surge before AZ-5 is not asserted here.

26.04 · seconds after AZ-5 / Sekunden nach AZ-5 · A dangerous initial response

R · Reconstructed

Post-accident analyses identify how graphite displacers could initially add reactivity in the lower core. In this configuration, steam feedback and the spatial power distribution amplified the danger. The precise evolution is reconstructed, not fully recorded.

The shaded core is symbolic. No peak power or second-by-second physical simulation is claimed.

1986 → · The consequences were human

D · Documented

Workers and emergency responders suffered fatal injuries and radiation illness. Evacuation, displacement and contamination changed many lives. UNSCEAR separately evaluates acute effects, thyroid cancer and long-term evidence; no single counter can represent the suffering.

This station covers the accident mechanism. The linked health assessments have their own dates, populations and uncertainties.

5 / TECHNICAL FINDINGS

A failure across several layers.

Reactor physics, protection design, operating decisions and safety culture must be examined together.

01

Reactivity feedback

The positive void effect could reinforce a power increase in the accident configuration. The core was large, and spatial effects mattered.

INSAG-7 §2.2 ↗
02

A protection-system design flaw

Initial rod insertion could add local reactivity. A command intended to shut down the reactor was not reliably protective in the relevant state.

INSAG-7 §§2.2, 4 ↗
03

Information and operating conditions

Operating constraints and decisions mattered, but the hazards were not adequately communicated. A calculated margin was poorly integrated into real-time protection.

INSAG-7 §§2.3–3 ↗
04

Revising the narrative

INSAG-7 revised the emphasis of the 1986 assessment. Technical design, regulation and organizational safety culture were essential to understanding the accident.

INSAG-7 §§4–5 ↗
What changed after 1986?

Subsequent measures addressed control-rod design, shutdown performance, reactivity characteristics and operating restrictions. This exhibit concerns the 1986 Unit 4 configuration; it does not describe all later RBMK variants or evaluate current plants.

INSAG-7 §2.2 · Annex II §II-5 ↗

The people affected are part of this history. Technical explanation cannot measure the loss experienced by workers, responders, families and displaced communities.

Sources, interpretation and limits

D identifies a report-supported fact, not necessarily a direct sensor reading. R identifies an inferred state or interpreted layout. S identifies our explanatory graphics. A documented timestamp never makes the accompanying diagram a historical measurement.

Source hierarchy & reconstruction limits

The accident chronology and SKALA distinctions follow INSAG-7, particularly Annex I §§1-4.3–1-4.6 and Table I-I. Its annexes retain different analyses and uncertainties. The NEA overview supplies plant context but retains some older simplified narrative; it does not override INSAG-7 on AZ-5, ORM or the pre-scram power history. UNSCEAR supplies the human-consequences context. Page references use printed pagination, not PDF viewer page numbers.

This is a local, deterministic presentation of curated chapters. It does not execute SKALA code, solve neutron transport, model thermohydraulics, reproduce the real control-room layout or connect to a plant. No dial can change the historical outcome.
