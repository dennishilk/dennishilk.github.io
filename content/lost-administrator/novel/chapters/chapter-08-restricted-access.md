# Chapter 08 — Restricted Access

*Wednesday, August 5, 2026*

**11:28 AM**

The meeting point was a broad strip of gray asphalt on the north side of SR 70.

It ran beside the highway for less than fifty yards before ending at a locked metal gate. Beyond it, a narrow service road disappeared between low palms, dense brush and a drainage ditch.

A faded sign prohibited unauthorized vehicles. Beneath it hung a newer orange warning placard whose lower edge had begun to peel away from the metal in the heat.

There was no name for the place.

No street number.

Nothing that said Cypress Meridian.

Officer Daniels parked behind a white county pickup.

The Ford F-250 had dried mud around the wheel wells and several fine scratches along the passenger side. Two locked plastic equipment boxes stood in the bed beside a folded barricade and a bundle of orange marking stakes.

Henry released his seat belt but did not open the door.

A man in a tan uniform waited beside the pickup. He stood in the narrow shade of the open driver's door and watched traffic pass on SR 70.

Daniels switched off the engine.

"That should be Deputy Grant."

Henry nodded.

He had said little since they left Chesapeake Signal Tech. His hands rested on his thighs. He had brought nothing with him. No bag. No laptop. Not even the small flashlight he normally kept in his car.

Daniels had asked him not to.

Henry had not argued.

When they stepped outside, the heat pressed against them immediately. A truck passed on SR 70 and left a deep mechanical roar behind it for several seconds.

The deputy came out of the shade.

He was close to Henry's height and appeared to be in his late forties. His brown hair was cut short and had begun to turn gray. The skin around his eyes carried the permanent lines of someone who spent more time outside than behind a desk.

"Officer Daniels?"

"Yes."

They shook hands.

"Lewis Grant."

Daniels indicated Henry.

"Henry Sullivan. Chesapeake Signal Tech."

Grant looked at him briefly.

"You're the technical support?"

"Only if there is still any of our old equipment out there," Henry said. "And only to identify it."

"Good."

Grant took a thin black folder from the passenger seat of the truck. It contained several printed pages, a handwritten checklist and a copy of the property owner's written authorization.

"The owner confirmed access to the property," he said. "That covers the exterior areas and any building or section that can be assessed as safe on site. It does not cover the restricted ground around the former generator area or the old battery station. Nobody enters either one."

Daniels read the first page.

"Is anyone from building safety joining us?"

"Not on the drive in. County Fire Rescue is trying to free someone up. Until then, we do not enter anything visibly damaged or already documented as unsafe."

Grant turned to Henry.

"You stay with Officer Daniels or with me. You do not touch anything until it has been documented and one of us specifically clears it. No cabinets. No wiring. No switches. Not even if you think you know exactly what they do."

"Understood."

"If you recognize something, tell us. If you think anything may still be energized, tell us immediately. If you are not sure, say that too."

Henry nodded.

Grant unfolded a map. It showed the larger property as an irregular area between old access routes and drainage lines. One path had been traced in black pen.

"We go through this first gate, follow the service road and reach the current property perimeter here. That one has a keypad. I received the code from the county contact this morning. After that, the road gets worse."

His finger followed the marked line west.

"The paved section ends here. What remains beyond it depends on how generously you define the word road."

Henry studied the map.

The name Meridian Access Road appeared nowhere on it.

Grant did not fold it away immediately.

"I know the property," he said. "At least from the outside."

Daniels looked up.

"You've been there before?"

"Not at the buildings. But I've worked this part of the county for more than twenty years. There used to be fewer fences and more roads you could actually drive. We occasionally had calls about an open gate, damaged fencing or someone who thought the property was a shortcut."

"What was the site?" Henry asked.

Grant studied the map for a moment.

"Most people called it the old relay station. Sometimes the telemetry site. Technicians went out there. Utility vehicles once in a while. Never a large crew, and I never saw anyone stationed there full-time."

Henry pointed toward the western section of the property.

"Chesapeake had service calls there."

"That would fit," Grant said. "But I couldn't tell you what they transmitted or monitored. There were several technical sites in this area. Pump stations. Radio towers. Small utility compounds. From the road, one looked much like another."

"Have you ever heard the name Cypress Meridian Telemetry Annex?" Daniels asked.

Grant shook his head.

"Not before your call."

He folded the map.

"I only remember that the road used to be better. After the storms, it got a little worse every year. Eventually the newer gate went in, and after that we had almost no reason to travel farther west."

"How close did you ever get?" Henry asked.

"Close enough to see part of a roof between the trees. Not close enough to identify the building."

Grant placed the map on the passenger seat.

"That is everything I know about the place."

It was more than the current owner had known.

And less than Henry had hoped.

Grant opened the rear door of the Ford.

"Let's look at the actual gate."

---

Daniels took the passenger seat. Henry sat behind him while Grant placed the folder beside the center console.

The pickup smelled of hot plastic, dust and old coffee. A county radio was mounted beneath the dashboard. Beside it sat a second handheld unit and a GPS display whose map showed little more than a yellow highway and a large unmarked area.

Grant started the engine.

The gate beside SR 70 controlled only the narrow service route where they had met. Grant opened it with a key, drove through and stopped again while Daniels closed it behind them.

For the first few minutes, the public highway remained audible.

The service road ran roughly parallel to SR 70, separated from it by brush, shallow ditches and low palms. The sound of passing trucks appeared between the trees, faded, then returned more quietly after the next bend.

The asphalt was old but mostly intact. Grass grew through fine cracks. Broken branches had been moved just far enough aside for a vehicle to pass.

Henry took out his phone.

Four bars had become three.

A minute later, there were two.

He did not open anything. He only watched the display while Grant guided the Ford around an eroded section near the edge of the road.

"It starts earlier than I expected," Daniels said.

Grant glanced at his own phone in its dashboard mount.

"Out here, reception can depend on which side of a drainage ditch you're standing on."

The road turned northwest.

SR 70 disappeared behind the vegetation. A distant engine could be heard once more, then only the steady sound of the tires and the dry scrape of branches along the passenger side remained.

Henry's phone showed one bar.

Then none.

Several seconds later, one returned.

"That is why we use the radio," Grant said.

Ahead, the road narrowed. The asphalt gave way to a lighter, uneven surface where sand and gravel had pushed through. A broken post leaned in the grass. Whatever sign had once been attached to it was gone.

Grant slowed.

After another bend, a high chain-link fence appeared between the trees.

It looked newer than the road.

The metal posts stood straight. Three rows of outward-angled wire ran along the top. A wide double gate blocked the road, with a black weatherproof keypad mounted on a separate post beside the driver's side.

No sign named an owner.

None mentioned Cypress Meridian.

A single white board hung from one gate leaf.

**RESTRICTED UTILITY PROPERTY**  
**AUTHORIZED ACCESS ONLY**

Grant stopped several yards away.

Henry's phone showed one bar again.

By the time he looked down, it was gone.

Grant left the engine running and stepped outside.

For a moment, nobody else moved.

The chain-link fence disappeared into dense growth on both sides. Vines had begun to climb through the lower sections. The gate remained straight in its hinges, but dark lines ran down its galvanized supports where years of rain had carried dust and plant residue over the metal.

The keypad was newer than the fence, but it did not look frequently used. Sunlight had dulled the plastic cover. Fine sand filled the edges around several buttons. An old spiderweb hung between the housing and its post.

Grant wiped two fingers across the front.

The numbers became clearer beneath the dust.

He pressed one button.

Nothing happened.

He waited, pressed it again and held it slightly longer. A faint red light appeared above the keypad.

"It was asleep," he said without turning around.

Daniels looked back at Henry.

"Reassuring."

Henry said nothing.

Grant removed a folded paper from his shirt pocket and entered the code slowly.

Each button produced a quiet tone. After the final digit, the red light remained.

One second passed.

Then another.

A relay clicked inside the post.

The light turned green.

A low mechanical hum came from the right gate leaf. The drive started with a jerk, stopped, then began pulling both leaves inward.

Metal scraped against metal.

Dry leaves and twigs shifted along the bottom edge. One side moved faster than the other. When the opening became wide enough for the Ford, the motor stopped before either leaf reached its intended fully open position.

Grant studied the gap.

It was sufficient.

Daniels got out.

"Does it close automatically?"

"It should."

Grant found a small metal panel on the inside post. It opened only after he pressed firmly against its lower edge.

A second control sat behind it.

"If it doesn't, we close it from here."

He walked several steps beyond the gate and looked at the ground.

The road behind the fence was no longer asphalt. Pale packed sand continued west between overgrown shoulders. Grass grew along the center. Old tire marks remained visible, but their edges had softened and were broken by rain, wind and animal tracks.

Grant crouched without touching anything.

Daniels joined him.

"Recent?"

"Not recent enough to tell us anything."

He indicated a shallow depression in the sand.

"It may belong to the boundary contractor. It may be weeks older."

The marks were not two clean tracks from a single pass. The ground carried overlapping remnants of several vehicles, some nearly erased.

"No reason to make more of it," Grant said.

Daniels nodded.

They returned to the truck.

Grant eased the Ford through the gate. The front wheels crossed from old asphalt onto pale sand, and the vehicle leaned slightly when the right tire entered a shallow wash.

Behind them, the gate motor started again.

Henry turned in his seat.

The leaves closed slowly over the road. One reached the stop first. The second continued several inches before locking into place with a dull metallic sound.

The green light went out.

Grant waited until the gate showed no visible gap in the mirror.

Then he continued west.

Henry's phone displayed one bar for an instant.

It disappeared before the truck reached the first bend.

---

The sand road ran nearly straight at first.

Grant kept the Ford below twenty miles per hour. Even shallow depressions were enough to make the heavy vehicle roll slowly from side to side. The air conditioner worked audibly against the heat while loose branches struck the body with dull taps.

Henry checked his phone once more.

No service.

The display remained empty even when he held the device higher for several seconds.

He put it away.

"That answers that," Daniels said.

"For this section," Grant replied. "A few hundred yards farther on, a bar may appear long enough to send a text if you're lucky. I would not rely on it for a call."

He tapped the county radio.

The faint hiss from the speaker was more reassuring than silence.

The road descended.

A broad wash crossed the route ahead. Rainwater had stripped away the sand and exposed a harder, darker layer beneath. Water remained in a long depression on the right. Leaves and a thin green film covered the surface.

Grant stopped and switched on the hazard lights.

"That should be the first damaged section in the report."

He removed the handheld radio.

"We check it on foot. Only the road."

Daniels got out. Henry waited until Grant motioned for him to follow.

The air outside was heavy and wet. Once the engine became only a sound behind them, other noises emerged: insects in the grass, a bird somewhere above the trees and the slow drip of water from the vegetation along the wash.

Grant went first.

He stayed on the firmer center and tested the ground with the heel of his boot. Daniels followed several steps behind. Henry remained beside Daniels.

Nobody needed to remind him.

Grant reached the lowest section and examined both sides.

"The center is firm. We stay away from the right. That edge is undercut."

Daniels pointed at two shallow tire impressions crossing the left side.

"The contractor?"

"Probably."

Grant crouched.

The tracks were not fresh. Loose sand had already filled their edges, and small windblown leaves lay inside them.

"Nothing from the last few days," he said. "I would not claim more."

Henry looked beyond the wash.

The road disappeared into thicker vegetation.

Something pale stood among the plants.

At first, he thought it was a broken fence post. Then he recognized the rectangular shape.

"What is that?"

Grant followed his gaze.

"Stay here."

He continued alone, with Daniels several steps behind him.

The object stood roughly thirty yards beyond the wash on the left side of the road. It was a low concrete marker, no higher than a person's knee, with a small metal plate attached to its face.

Grant checked the ground around it before approaching.

"No visible wire," he said.

Daniels motioned Henry forward.

The concrete was cracked and stained dark on one side. Rust had formed around the screws in the plate. Only several letters and an arrow remained from the original wording.

Henry leaned slightly forward without touching it.

"It may be a route marker."

"Power?" Daniels asked.

"Possibly. Or data. Control cable. Markers like this were often placed where a buried line crossed a road."

He followed the faint arrow.

It pointed across the route toward the opposite ditch.

A second marker stood there, almost completely hidden by ferns and tall grass.

Grant looked between them.

"I never noticed those before."

"Maybe they weren't overgrown then," Daniels said.

"Or I had no reason to look for them."

Henry examined the plate again.

A narrow line of stamped characters showed beneath the rust. Most were unreadable.

One section remained.

**...CABLE**

Below it was a number whose final digit alone had survived.

"Not a Chesapeake marker," Henry said.

"You're sure?"

"The old ones I know used a different format."

Daniels photographed the marker and its counterpart on the other side of the road.

"Could the line still be active?"

Henry straightened.

"Not without testing. The age of the marker tells us nothing about what is beneath it. An old cable may be dead. It may also have been replaced without anyone removing the marker."

"Or something still uses the same route," Daniels said.

"That is possible too."

Grant indicated the road.

"Then we treat anything coming out of the ground as connected until we know otherwise."

Henry nodded.

Farther west, more low shapes stood in the vegetation. They were too irregular for a fence and too widely spaced for road markers.

Some were concrete.

Others were short metal pipes with rust-brown open ends.

The road continued between them.

Grant looked ahead.

"I remember utility poles farther in," he said. "Wooden ones."

"Telephone?" Daniels asked.

"Maybe. One line. Most of the poles were already leaning the last time I saw them."

"When was that?"

Grant thought about it.

"Before Hurricane Ian. I did not reach the buildings. Part of the outer fence had been damaged after another storm, and someone reported that cattle might get onto the property."

"Were there cattle here?"

"On the neighboring land."

Henry looked down the road.

"Did you see any buildings?"

"Only part of a roof through the trees."

"Which one?"

Grant shook his head.

"Low. Pale. That is all I could tell."

They returned to the Ford.

Grant guided the truck across the left side of the wash. The tires held on the firmer surface, though the vehicle leaned sharply before climbing the far edge.

Beyond it, the road narrowed.

The concrete markers appeared at irregular intervals on both sides. Some remained upright. Others had fallen into the grass. Once, a line of sunken metal covers crossed the road, almost entirely buried by sand and plants.

Henry noticed it only after the Ford had already rolled over it.

"Something else runs underneath us," he said.

Grant lifted his foot from the accelerator.

"A cable duct?"

"Or old drainage. Maybe both."

Daniels turned partly toward him.

"Is it on the site plan?"

"No."

Henry looked through the side window toward the line behind them.

"The plans in P-17 were incomplete. The blacked-out label on the smaller building did not help."

After another bend, the first wooden pole appeared on the right.

It leaned among the palms. Its upper section had broken away. A single black insulator remained on a rusted metal bracket. No line was visible.

Fifty yards farther on stood another pole.

A short piece of cable still hung from it.

It ended in open air.

The road went deeper between the trees.

The outer gate was no longer visible behind them.

No building had appeared ahead.

Only the old road, the overgrown markers and an infrastructure that had stopped being complete one piece at a time.

---

The second damaged section had no clear beginning.

The road simply became narrower. The two tire paths moved closer together while vegetation pressed against both sides of the truck. Young branches brushed the mirrors. Grant repeatedly moved left to avoid deep holes along the right shoulder.

Then most of the outer track disappeared.

Rainwater had cut away one side of the road and left a diagonal channel from the raised left edge down toward the ditch. A strip of sand, exposed roots and hard material remained in the center, only a few feet wide.

Grant stopped.

The road continued for roughly forty yards before curving behind thick brush.

Neither Daniels nor Henry could see what lay beyond it.

Grant shifted into park but kept the engine running.

"The report was generous."

Daniels studied the channel.

"Can we cross?"

"Probably."

Grant looked in the mirror.

"The better question is whether we can come back."

He got out. Daniels followed. Henry waited for Grant's signal before joining them.

The ground near the edge gave slightly beneath their boots. Grant walked to the start of the erosion but remained on the firm side. He examined the exposed roots and then the narrower section beyond the bend.

"The Ford can make this," he said. "But if the road ends around that corner or gets any tighter, I would have to reverse the entire distance."

Daniels looked behind them.

Twenty yards back, the route widened into an irregular flat area. It may once have been a passing point or turnaround. The grass stood lower there, and a harder layer remained beneath the loose sand.

"We leave it there," Grant said. "While I can still turn around."

"How far are the buildings?" Henry asked.

Grant looked over the brush.

"Hard to say."

Then he stopped.

Something pale showed between two palms.

Only a narrow horizontal line above the vegetation. Flat, gray and darkened along one edge.

A roof.

Henry shifted half a step.

The sight line changed. For an instant, he saw more of it: a low roof edge, possibly metal or coated concrete, partly hidden by branches.

Then the opening closed again.

"I could not see that from here before," Grant said.

"Because the vegetation was thicker?" Daniels asked.

Grant shook his head.

"Or because I never came this far."

He watched the gap for several seconds.

"It cannot be much farther."

Grant reversed the Ford to the wider area and maneuvered in several movements until the front faced back toward the outer gate.

He switched off the engine.

The air conditioner stopped.

The silence became larger almost immediately.

No traffic.

No distant engine.

Only insects, wind in the palms and the quiet ticking of the cooling vehicle.

Grant reported their position by radio.

The reply came through mild static but remained clear.

"County vehicle parked," Grant said. "Three persons proceeding on foot. Next status after visual assessment of the buildings."

He waited for confirmation and clipped the radio back to his belt.

Daniels checked his own unit.

Henry took out his phone.

No service.

He put it away.

Grant opened one of the locked boxes in the truck bed. Inside were warning vests, work gloves, sealed masks and several compact safety items.

He removed a small field bag and two orange marking stakes, then closed the box.

"We stay on the visible road," he said. "No shortcuts through the grass. We do not know where the old cable routes are, and we do not know exactly where the contaminated areas begin."

Daniels nodded.

Henry looked between the trees again.

The roof was no longer visible from this position.

But he knew where it was.

Grant locked the Ford. The electronic chirp sounded unnaturally clear.

They returned to the damaged section.

Grant went first.

Daniels behind him.

Henry at Daniels' side.

They crossed the firm center one at a time. Beyond the bend, the road became narrower but remained walkable.

After several minutes, the roof appeared again.

This time, it did not disappear.

A dark, straight section of wall showed beneath its edge.

Farther left, something stood in the vegetation that was neither a tree nor an old utility pole.

Two vertical metal posts.

A crooked section of chain-link hanging between them.

---

The posts belonged to a gate.

Or to what remained of one.

As they approached, the old inner fence became visible across the width of the road. On the right, it still stood several feet high between the trees. On the left, a large fallen trunk had crushed the posts, chain-link and upper security wire nearly to the ground.

The tree had not fallen recently.

The exposed root mass was dark, weathered and partly covered with ferns. Smaller branches had lost their bark. New growth pushed through the collapsed fence.

One gate leaf still hung from its upper hinge.

Its lower edge rested diagonally in the sand.

The second leaf was almost completely hidden beneath the trunk.

Grant stopped several steps away.

"We do not just walk through that."

Daniels looked toward the lowered section.

"Even though it is open?"

"Especially because it is open."

Grant pointed at the ground. Rusted metal, broken wire ends and folded chain-link lay beneath the trunk, some of it almost invisible under grass and leaves.

Daniels began photographing the gate from several angles.

Henry looked up.

A camera hung above the surviving gatepost on a short metal arm.

Its housing had once been light gray. Dark runoff and green growth now covered it. The clear lens cover was gone. Two severed cable ends projected from the bottom, and the bracket had bent so far downward that the camera faced the ground immediately in front of the gate.

Even a person with no technical knowledge could see that it no longer watched anything.

"There was a camera," Henry said.

Daniels followed his gaze.

"Was?"

"The cable is torn out. The housing is open. And even if anything inside survived, it is looking at the sand."

Grant studied it.

"It may have been that way for years."

"It may have."

Henry looked along the surviving fence.

Several strands of ordinary barbed wire remained on the angled upper supports. Farther back, compressed coils of old razor wire lay across the fence top. Some loops had stretched apart. Others had rusted together and become threaded with vines.

Below them ran something different.

Three thinner wires had once been tensioned at even intervals through small metal brackets. Two now sagged. The third had broken near the gate and wrapped itself around a post.

Henry stepped closer but remained on the visible road.

"This site mattered."

Daniels lowered the camera.

"The razor wire?"

"Not only that."

Henry indicated the thinner rows.

"Those were probably part of a climb-detection system."

"Electric?"

"Not an electric fence."

Henry searched along the next post. Beneath rust and dirt, a small rectangular metal housing remained attached to it. The thin wires continued from the housing in both directions.

"I know a similar system from a secured technical section of a water treatment plant near Port St. Lucie," he said. "We worked there once. Nothing secret. But the perimeter protection used the same principle."

Daniels looked from the housing to the tensioned wires.

"How does it work?"

"Tension and movement. If someone pushed the fence down, lifted one of these lines hard enough or tried to climb over, the load on the wire changed."

Henry pointed to the small housing.

"A sensor detected that and reported it to a local alarm system or a security console."

Grant looked toward the fallen tree.

"Then that should have produced a fairly strong alarm."

"If the system was still active when the tree fell."

"Can you tell whether it is active now?" Daniels asked.

Henry shook his head.

"Not without tracing the wiring or testing it. But the broken line, damaged sensor housings and open camera do not suggest that it is."

He looked at the fence again.

"The more important question is why this site needed it."

"Maybe they only wanted people away from the equipment," Grant said.

"Possible."

"It is still a lot of security for an old relay station," Daniels said.

Henry did not answer immediately.

At the ordinary facilities where he had seen similar sensors, he had known what they protected.

A power system.

Communications equipment.

Control hardware.

Here, he did not.

"Whatever stood behind this fence," he said, "someone wanted to know immediately when something crossed it."

Daniels photographed the sensor brackets individually, then the broken wires and the destroyed camera.

Grant walked several steps to the right and examined the surviving fence line.

"There is a passage beside the gate," he said. "The ground looks firmer, and we do not have to pass under the tree."

He documented the narrow opening first.

Two posts had bent away from one another and created a gap just wide enough for one person at a time. Broken wire ends projected from the growth. Some lay loose in the grass. Others remained under tension between damaged brackets.

Grant took an orange stake from the field bag and placed it at the edge of the safe route.

"Nobody steps left of that marker. Nobody touches the fence."

He went through first.

Daniels followed after photographing the passage from close range. Henry waited until both men stood on the other side.

He kept his arms close to his body as he passed through. Above him, a flattened coil of razor wire hung among branches and rusted supports. Some of its edges had become dull and brown.

Others still looked sharp enough.

Beyond the fence, the road widened.

The surface remained sandy for several steps. Then rectangular concrete slabs appeared beneath grass and accumulated debris. Their joints formed straight lines under moss and roots.

Grant stopped on the first clear slab.

"This was paved from here."

Henry looked past him.

The site occupied a flat clearing that the vegetation was slowly reclaiming.

The building whose roof they had seen stood roughly a hundred yards away. It was long, single-story and pale, with a flat roof. Large dark stains ran from the roofline down the walls. Several windows were covered with weathered panels. One panel was missing, leaving a black opening.

"Operations Building," Henry said.

Daniels looked at him.

"From the plan?"

"The shape and position match."

To its right stood a smaller structure.

One side of its roof had collapsed inward. Part of the metal exterior skin had pulled loose and hung in long bent sheets. Beams and dark insulation showed beneath the damaged edge.

Henry indicated it.

"That should be the Equipment Shelter."

"The structure already listed as unsafe," Grant said.

"Yes."

Grant lifted his radio.

"Then nobody approaches it until someone qualified looks at it."

To the left of the access route stood a low concrete building, scarcely larger than a double garage. Dense growth concealed much of its front. On the old site plan, a structure in approximately that position had carried the deliberately blacked-out label.

Henry said nothing about it.

Not yet.

Behind the buildings rose a metal tower.

It was taller than it had appeared through the trees: a narrow lattice structure with empty mounts and short crossmembers. Only a few antenna elements remained. One hung at an angle. Another pointed straight down.

Several guy wires ran from the tower in different directions. Some remained tight. Others lay loose among grass and brush.

"Nobody walks beneath those," Grant said.

"Wasn't planning to," Daniels replied.

The paved area divided ahead.

One route continued toward the Operations Building. Another curved right toward the damaged shelter. A narrower path led left toward several low concrete foundations and an area marked by faded red posts.

A broken length of warning tape hung between two of them.

Grant compared the site plan with the clearing.

"Generator area."

Henry followed the orientation.

"The old battery station should be beyond it."

Grant put the map away.

"Then the red markers are the limit."

Daniels photographed the restricted ground from a distance. It looked no different from the rest of the site.

Grass.

Sand.

Several pieces of concrete.

That made it more dangerous, not less.

They remained on the center path.

Small palms grew between the slabs. A heavy fallen branch lay across one section and had been there long enough for fungus to grow beneath it. An old exterior light stood beside the route on a low pole. Its glass was broken, and an abandoned bird's nest filled the housing.

Nothing moved inside the buildings.

No motor.

No fan.

No electrical hum.

Henry had not expected anything to be running.

He listened anyway.

Daniels stopped and looked down.

"Tire marks."

Grant joined him without leaving the concrete.

At the right edge of the route, where wind had carried sand over the slabs, several broad impressions ran toward the buildings and overlapped one another.

Henry looked back toward the damaged access road.

"Could be the boundary contractor."

"The owner said the contractor does not normally drive this far," Daniels said.

"Not normally is not the same as never," Grant replied.

He crouched.

The impressions were shallow. Small leaves and insect tracks had collected in them. A recognizable tread pattern survived only in two short sections.

"Not fresh," he said. "Not today or yesterday."

Daniels photographed them anyway.

"Can you identify vehicle type or size?"

Grant shook his head.

"Not reliably. Wide tires. Pickup. SUV. Service vehicle. Anything more would be guessing."

The marks were wide enough for the Yukon.

They were also wide enough for Grant's Ford and almost any vehicle capable of reaching this point.

"We record them," Daniels said. "Nothing more."

A low barrier stood open across the paved route roughly fifty yards before the Operations Building.

It was not broken.

Not bent.

Simply open.

Grant stopped again.

"Was that on the plan?"

Henry thought about the documents in P-17.

"No."

"Then we treat it as a later addition."

Daniels photographed the barrier.

A small metal control box stood on its post. The cover hung open. Inside were cables, a corroded switch and a row of old terminals.

Henry studied it from a distance.

"It may once have been connected to the inner access system."

"May?" Daniels asked.

"That is all I can say without going closer."

Grant looked toward the Operations Building.

"We stay outside. First, we make a full exterior assessment. Then we decide what can be reached safely."

Daniels nodded.

Henry looked across the site.

C.M.T.A. did not look like a hidden command center.

It looked like a place that had stopped being needed.

And that someone had once considered important enough to protect with two fences, cameras, razor wire and climb sensors.

---

Grant approached the left side of the Operations Building first.

The paved route curved around the front, though roots had pushed several slabs upward. One had lifted high enough to create a sharp edge.

Grant marked it with an orange stake.

"We stay away from the wall," he said. "Especially beneath the roofline."

Daniels photographed the entire front.

The building was longer than it had appeared from the access road. Pale coating had peeled from the walls in broad patches. Dark stains ran below several windows. Some came from rain. Others were wider and had turned green.

A narrow metal frame remained above the main entrance.

The sign once held there was gone.

The glass doors had been covered by a gray panel secured with four large screws. One side had bowed outward slightly, but the fasteners remained in place.

"Doesn't look like anyone used the front doors," Daniels said.

"Not recently," Grant replied.

Henry studied the wall beside the entrance.

An old card reader remained there. Its housing had yellowed. A scratched protective flap covered the slot. Beneath it sat a separate twelve-button keypad.

Henry did not approach it.

"That may have belonged to the old site credentials."

Daniels looked at him.

"CM-047?"

"Possibly. We do not know whether Michael's credential was a card, a code or only an identification number in the records."

"Or whether any of it still works."

"Exactly."

No indicator light showed on the reader. A narrow crack ran through the wall below it and had filled with dark dirt.

Daniels photographed the equipment from several angles.

"Nothing gets touched," Grant said.

"Wasn't going to."

They continued around the building.

At the western corner, the roof edge was damaged. Part of the fascia hung downward and moved slightly in the wind. Dark insulation showed behind it. A rusted drainpipe ended several inches above the ground where its lower section had disappeared.

Grant stayed well clear of the area beneath it.

"Nobody walks under that."

Daniels marked the location on his copy of the site plan.

Several cable conduits ran beneath the roofline. Some ended at small gray junction boxes. Others had opened or pulled away from their mounts through corrosion.

"Chesapeake?" Daniels asked.

Henry shook his head.

"Not identifiable. Standard materials. We could have installed some of it. So could any other contractor."

A rusted air-conditioning unit stood behind a low protective frame. Its fan did not move. The lines entering the wall had become partly buried under growth.

No hum came from it.

No relay clicked.

Nothing suggested power.

At the rear, the trees stood closer. Broken branches lay on the roof, and a leaning palm brushed the back half of the structure.

Two narrow windows remained uncovered.

The first was dark.

The second was cracked but still held in its frame. A star-shaped fracture ran from the lower corner almost to the center.

Daniels photographed it.

"Hit from outside?" Grant asked.

Henry examined the pattern.

"I can't tell."

"Then that is what we record."

A ventilation opening sat beneath the window. Its grille had torn away from one side and rested at an angle in the grass. The opening behind it was large enough for small animals.

Grant did not shine a light inside.

"The building is no longer sealed," Daniels said.

"That does not make it safe to enter."

As they continued toward the north side, Grant's radio crackled.

He removed it from his belt.

"Grant."

The transmission was weak but understandable. Dispatch reported that a County Fire Rescue unit had reached the outer gate and was coming down the damaged access route.

Grant checked his watch.

"Who is responding?"

A shorter transmission followed.

His expression relaxed slightly.

"Understood. Tell Captain Mercer we are at the Operations Building and that the passage through the damaged inner fence is marked."

He confirmed the message and returned the radio to his belt.

Daniels waited.

"You know him?"

"Aaron Mercer. Nearly twenty years working this area. Technical rescue, storm damage, collapsed roofs. He is not a structural engineer, but he knows when a building is trying to kill someone."

Henry looked toward the route through the trees.

"How does he get here?"

"Not in a large truck. Fire Rescue has a utility ATV for brush and field responses. If the road behind our Ford has not completely washed away, he'll reach the inner gate."

"Can he tell us whether we may enter?" Daniels asked.

"He can identify obvious reasons not to. That is not the same as a formal structural clearance."

Grant looked back at the damaged roofline.

"And if Mercer says no, the answer is no."

The radio produced another burst of static.

Far behind the trees, a low engine sounded briefly.

Then it vanished.

Grant looked toward it.

"That should be him."

They continued only as far as the north side.

A narrow concrete strip led to another door. It was solid metal and had no visible handle, only an old keyway and a second card reader.

Above it, a rectangular section of wall was cleaner than the weathered surface around it.

Something had once hung there.

A sign.

Perhaps a designation.

It had been removed.

Henry looked at the empty rectangle.

Its proportions seemed familiar.

Not from this building.

From the old documents.

He said nothing yet.

---

The ATV engine grew louder.

It disappeared twice behind the trees before an orange utility vehicle emerged near the damaged inner fence.

The County Fire Rescue emblem was fixed to the front. A flat equipment box, a fire extinguisher and several rolled straps were secured behind the two seats.

The driver stopped outside the fallen gate.

He wore dark blue duty pants, heavy boots and a gray Fire Rescue shirt. A white helmet was strapped to the passenger seat. Before climbing out, he studied the fallen tree and the compressed coils of razor wire.

Grant walked toward him through the clearing.

The man removed his sunglasses.

"Lewis Grant," he said. "I thought I wouldn't see you again until the first Friday-night game."

Grant stopped beside the ATV.

"The season hasn't started."

"Exactly."

For a moment, Grant's mouth shifted.

It was not quite a smile.

It came close.

"Aaron Mercer," Grant said when Daniels and Henry approached the inside of the damaged fence. "County Fire Rescue."

Mercer lifted the helmet from the seat but did not put it on yet. He was in his late forties, strongly built, with short dark hair graying at the temples. His movements were calm and economical, as if he was accustomed to assessing a place before the people standing in it.

Grant introduced Daniels.

"Officer Daniels, Port St. Lucie Police."

Daniels offered his hand.

"Thank you for coming on short notice."

"Dispatch said there was a storm-damaged building in the middle of nowhere." Mercer looked past him toward the Operations Building. "It sounded better than the paperwork on my desk."

Grant indicated Henry.

"Henry Sullivan. Chesapeake Signal Tech. Technical identification only."

Mercer shook Henry's hand.

"You know the site?"

"No. Only some of the older systems Chesapeake may have serviced here."

"Good."

He said it almost exactly as Grant had beside SR 70.

"Then at least nobody is pretending to know more about this place than they do."

Grant pointed to the damaged gate.

"We entered through the gap on the right. Single file. I marked the safe edge. Several broken wires may still be mechanically tensioned."

"Climb sensors," Daniels added. "Mr. Sullivan recognized a similar system from a secured section of a water treatment plant near Port St. Lucie."

Mercer followed the thin wires to one of the corroded sensor housings.

"Then this was once more than a simple fence."

He looked through the marked opening toward them.

"You came through there?"

"One at a time," Grant said. "Nothing has moved since."

Mercer crouched without touching the fence. He studied the wire positions and the firm sand inside the marked line.

"All right. I use the same route."

He put on the helmet and left a larger equipment bag on the ATV. He took only a flashlight, protective glasses, work gloves and a compact field bag.

Grant waited on the inside of the opening.

"Do not lean against the right post."

"Lewis, I understand how a fence works."

"This one apparently doesn't anymore."

Mercer looked at him.

"Then you should be especially glad I came."

He passed through the gap carefully.

On the inside, he remained clear of the compressed wire and surveyed the site.

His gaze moved from the Operations Building to the collapsed roof of the Equipment Shelter and finally to the old tower behind them.

The easy familiarity from his greeting did not disappear.

It moved into the background.

"How far have you gone?" he asked.

Grant pointed to the long building.

"Almost one full exterior pass. No interior spaces. We have only viewed the shelter from a distance."

"Good."

Mercer studied the damaged roof edge.

"Show me your route first. I want to see what you saw and what you may have missed."

---

Grant led them back around the Operations Building.

He stopped at each location already documented. Daniels summarized the photographs. Henry added information only when a cable, enclosure or technical installation was involved.

At the north-side door, Mercer examined the clean rectangle above the frame.

"A sign was mounted here," Daniels said.

Mercer studied the remaining fastener points.

"Removed with tools. Not torn away."

"Can you tell when?"

"No."

He remained several steps away and shone his light across the wall and doorframe from a low angle. The beam revealed surface irregularities that direct sunlight had concealed.

"The frame is not visibly distorted," he said. "That is better than I expected."

Grant indicated the roofline.

"And the crack above it?"

Mercer followed the line with the light.

"May be superficial. I do not see obvious displacement in the wall here."

"Stable?" Daniels asked.

"I did not say that."

Mercer switched off the flashlight.

"I said this side does not appear ready to fall toward us in the next five minutes."

Grant nodded slowly.

"Comforting."

"That is why you called me."

At the rear, Mercer studied the cracked window from several angles and then the damaged vent beneath it.

"Animals are probably inside."

"How large?" Daniels asked.

"Anything that fits through there."

He pointed to dark residue along the lower edge of the opening.

"Possibly more than one generation of it."

Henry looked into the black space.

"The building used to be air-conditioned."

"Then it probably contains insulation, cable routes and suspended ceiling material," Mercer said. "After years of water intrusion, some of that may be saturated where we cannot see it. Add mold, animal waste and possibly older construction materials nobody should breathe."

"The property report mentions possible contaminants," Daniels said.

"Then we treat the risk as real until somebody proves otherwise."

Mercer spent the longest time at the damaged western corner.

The loose roof material moved with each gust and fell back with a flat metallic clatter.

He marked a wide exclusion area with two stakes and a short strip of warning tape.

"Nobody passes beneath this edge. Not even with a helmet."

He looked across the rest of the building.

"The structure as a whole does not appear immediately unstable from outside. But the roof has taken water, the fascia is loose, and we know nothing about the ceiling inside."

Daniels looked toward the front entrance.

"Can we at least approach the door?"

"The front, yes. Open it, not yet."

"What are we waiting for?"

"Respiratory protection, protective clothing and a reason to accept the risk."

"A missing person."

Mercer met his eyes.

"That is a reason. It is not a reason to walk in blind."

He looked over the panel covering the glass doors.

"Before anyone removes that, I want every other access checked. If we enter, it will not be beneath a damaged roof edge and it will not be through an opening we have to create by force."

Grant turned toward the smaller structure on the right.

"Equipment Shelter?"

Mercer looked at it.

Even from this distance, one side of the roof had settled lower. A wall no longer stood vertical. A dark gap showed between hanging exterior panels and the foundation.

"I will assess it," Mercer said. "Nobody comes closer than I tell them."

They approached along the paved route.

Thirty yards from the shelter, Mercer raised one hand.

"Here."

The others stopped.

Mercer continued several steps alone without leaving the concrete. His gaze moved from the collapsed roof to the walls and then to a tilted ventilation structure at the rear.

A thin metal pipe protruded from the damaged roof and moved in the wind.

Mercer remained there for less than a minute.

Then he returned.

"No."

Daniels looked past him.

"Completely excluded?"

"For us, yes."

Mercer indicated the left corner.

"The wall has shifted outward. The roof is no longer bearing evenly. At least one member has failed or slipped from its support."

"Could someone still have entered?" Grant asked.

"People make poor decisions every day."

Mercer removed his helmet briefly and wiped his forehead with his forearm.

"The door on the far side may still be accessible. That does not make the room behind it safe. If it needs to be searched, we bring in a technical rescue team and additional stabilization."

Daniels photographed the building from their position.

"Not even a quick look from the doorway?"

"Not by us."

His voice did not become sharper.

Only final.

Henry studied the loose exterior panels. A bundle of old cable ran behind one of them in a vertical channel. Several lines had been pulled free and hung nearly to the ground.

"A lot of equipment was connected there," he said.

"Can you tell what kind?" Daniels asked.

"Not from here."

Henry looked once more.

"And I am not going closer."

Mercer replaced his helmet.

"Then we understand each other."

From the main route, all three structures were visible at once: the unsafe Equipment Shelter, the long Operations Building and the low concrete building to the left whose designation had been blacked out in P-17.

Mercer followed Henry's gaze.

"What is the small one?"

Henry did not answer immediately.

"We don't know."

Daniels looked toward it.

"Then we assess that one next."
