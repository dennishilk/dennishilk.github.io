# Chapter 18 — Before Anyone Else

“Dead?”

Frank said the word quietly.

For a moment, no one answered him.

Daniels remained standing at the end of the table, his service phone still in his hand. Navarro had already closed the laptop in front of her, though she had not yet moved away from it.

Frank stared at Daniels.

“Calver is dead?”

“That is the information I received.”

“How?”

“The preliminary assessment is suicide.”

Frank lowered his eyes toward the table.

His coffee sat untouched beside the pot Brenda had placed there. The surface had gone still. Two dark drops remained where he had spilled it earlier.

Henry looked from Daniels to Navarro.

“What does that mean for Michael?”

Daniels did not answer immediately.

“It means Elias Calver is dead,” he said. “Michael is still missing.”

“But if Calver had him—”

“We don’t know that he did.”

Henry’s jaw tightened.

Daniels continued before anyone else could build another conclusion from the news.

“We don’t know whether Calver drove the Yukon. We don’t know whether he was present when Michael left Chesapeake. We don’t know whether his death is directly connected to Michael’s disappearance.”

Frank raised his head.

“But it has to be.”

“No,” Daniels said. “It may be. That is not the same thing.”

The room fell silent again.

Frank looked older than he had twenty minutes earlier. The color had drained from his face, and what remained of his earlier defensiveness was gone.

“He came back,” he said.

Daniels watched him.

“A year ago. He came here. He wanted the system again.” Frank’s voice became quieter. “Now Michael is gone, and Calver is dead.”

He swallowed.

“What if he wasn’t the only one?”

No one answered that either.

Daniels slipped the phone into his pocket.

“Navarro and I are going to Fort Myers.”

Navarro was already gathering the few items she needed.

Daniels looked toward Frank.

“You remain here until someone tells you otherwise. Do not call anyone connected to the former project. Do not contact Calver’s family. Do not discuss what happened in this room with anyone.”

Frank nodded.

“The recovery of your RK-03 file will continue as arranged. The officers at your house will contact you before they enter.”

“Yes.”

“You keep your phone with you, charged and audible.”

“Yes.”

Daniels turned to Brenda and Henry.

“You both remain available. If either of you remembers anything else about Calver, the project, or anyone who contacted Chesapeake under another name, call me.”

Brenda nodded.

Henry did not respond immediately.

“Daniels.”

Daniels stopped near the door.

“Find out what this means for Michael.”

The request was not angry.

That made it harder.

Daniels met his eyes.

“I’ll find out what the evidence allows us to find out.”

Henry looked away.

Navarro opened the door. Daniels followed her into the hallway, and within seconds their footsteps were receding toward the front of the building.

Then the outer door closed.

The conference room seemed larger without them.

Brenda remained in her chair.

Henry stood beside the table, staring at the empty doorway. Frank had lowered his head again. His elbows rested on the table, and both hands covered his face.

No one spoke.

The air conditioner continued its low mechanical hum.

Somewhere farther inside the building, the coffee maker clicked as its warming cycle ended.

Henry reached into his pocket and took out his phone.

He found Daniels’ number.

His thumb hovered above it.

He imagined Sarah answering.

*Did something happen?*

He imagined Emma close enough to hear the change in Sarah’s voice.

Calver had been found.

Calver was dead.

Michael had not been found with him.

Those were the only things Henry could tell them.

Nothing that would help.

Nothing that would make them safer.

Only another reason to be afraid.

He darkened the screen without placing the call.

Brenda watched him put the phone away.

“We should remain calm,” she said softly.

Henry looked at her.

“Michael is probably all right.”

The words did not sound like certainty.

They sounded like something Brenda needed the room to contain.

Henry looked toward Frank.

Frank had not moved.

“Go home to your wife, Henry,” Brenda said.

For several seconds, Henry remained where he was.

Then he nodded.

“All right.”

Brenda stood and gathered her bag. Henry moved toward the door with her, then stopped beside Frank’s chair.

Frank’s head remained lowered. His hands still covered his face.

Henry looked down at him.

The anger had not disappeared. Neither had the betrayal. Frank had watched Michael leave, destroyed the records that might have helped find him, and remained silent while everyone around him searched.

But Calver was dead.

Michael was still missing.

And Frank looked as though the weight of every word he had withheld had finally settled on him at once.

Henry placed one hand on Frank’s shoulder.

Frank did not look up.

Henry did not forgive him.

He did not say that everything would be all right.

He left his hand there for only a moment.

Then he followed Brenda into the hallway.

The door closed behind them.

Frank remained alone at the conference table, his cooling coffee untouched in front of him.

***

For the first several miles, neither Daniels nor Navarro spoke.

The sky had darkened over the western horizon. Rain moved in uneven curtains across the flat distance, visible in one field and absent from the next. The wipers crossed the windshield twice, stopped, then began again when another band reached the road.

Daniels drove.

Navarro sat with her closed laptop on the floor behind her seat and her phone in one hand. She had not opened a case file. She had not called ahead again.

They both knew there would be enough information waiting for them.

Eventually Navarro looked through the passenger window.

“If he called 911 himself, he expected to be found.”

“Yes.”

“That doesn’t mean he wanted us to understand why.”

“No.”

She turned the phone once in her hand.

“If Calver had Michael, his death changes everything.”

Daniels watched the road.

“And if he didn’t?” Navarro asked.

A truck passed in the opposite direction, carrying a wave of rain behind it.

“Then someone else still does.”

Navarro looked forward again.

Neither of them said anything for several seconds.

“We don’t go there to prove suicide,” Daniels said. “We go there to preserve what happened.”

“And what he left behind.”

“If he left anything.”

“He left a body and a phone call.”

“We don’t know yet what either one means.”

Navarro gave the smallest nod.

Daniels’ service phone rang through the vehicle’s audio system.

He answered.

“Daniels.”

The caller identified himself as one of the officers holding the Fort Myers scene.

“We have something inside the house you should know before you arrive.”

Daniels lowered the vehicle’s speed slightly.

“What?”

“The decedent’s old bedroom. His parents apparently kept it mostly the way it was after he moved out. It was being used for him when he stayed here.”

“What is wrong with it?”

“It looks searched.”

Navarro turned toward the dashboard.

“Put him on speaker,” she said.

Daniels already had.

“Define searched,” he said.

“Old boxes taken down from the top shelf in the closet. Several are open on the floor. Desk drawers are partly open. One of the lower drawers looks like it was pulled all the way out and put back wrong.”

“Anything overturned?”

“No.”

“Anything broken?”

“Not that we can see.”

“Missing?”

“We don’t know what was there.”

“Did Calver sleep in that room?”

“It appears so. The bed is unmade. There’s a charger plugged into the wall by the nightstand. No device attached.”

“Is there another guest room?”

“Yes. Right next to it.”

“And?”

“Made bed. Empty drawers. Clean towels. Looks like an actual guest room.”

Navarro leaned closer.

“Only Calver’s room appears disturbed?”

“Yes.”

“Any forced entry?”

“Nothing obvious. We are still checking doors and windows.”

“Do not begin moving the boxes,” Daniels said. “Photograph the room as it is. Keep everyone out except scene personnel.”

“Understood.”

“Garage?”

“Secured. Medical examiner has been notified. No one has disturbed anything beyond the initial response.”

“Any note?”

“Nothing visible.”

“Phone?”

“One mobile phone in his pocket. We have not accessed it. The 911 call came from the parents’ landline.”

Daniels looked briefly toward Navarro.

“A corded phone?”

“Cordless handset. Base station is inside the house.”

“Secure both separately after documenting them in place.”

“Yes, sir.”

“We are on the way.”

Daniels ended the call.

Navarro looked ahead.

“He had his phone with him and used theirs.”

“That tells us he made a choice.”

“Not why.”

“No.”

The rain thinned again.

A few minutes later, Daniels’ phone rang a second time.

The call came from the unit sent to Calver’s known residence in Lake Placid.

Daniels answered.

“You are at eighteen forty-six Heron Trace Lane?”

“Yes. Apartment is secure.”

“Condition?”

“Quiet. No current sign of a search. No open drawers, no obvious disturbance, no broken glass. Door was locked when we arrived.”

“Entry?”

“Property manager provided access after authorization. We documented before opening.”

“Any computers?”

“A modern laptop on a desk. Also an old computer or game system. Beige. Keyboard built into it.”

Navarro looked over.

“What does it say on it?” Daniels asked.

A short pause.

“Commodore. Amiga.”

“Do not power it on.”

“We haven’t touched it.”

“Anything nearby?”

“Old game boxes and disks. Another World. Turrican. Lemmings. Lotus Turbo Challenge.”

Daniels glanced at Navarro.

“Monkey Island?”

The officer searched while they waited.

“No box with that title in the first group. I’ll keep looking.”

“Do that without moving anything until it is photographed.”

“Yes.”

“Any damage to the apartment door?”

“Old repaired damage around the latch side. Doesn’t look recent.”

Daniels’ attention sharpened.

“Run the address.”

“Already did. There was a report about three months ago.”

“What kind?”

“Neighbor called in a suspicious person. Said he saw someone he didn’t recognize standing at Calver’s door. A short time later, he saw the same person come out of the apartment and leave the property.”

“Description?”

“Not useful enough to identify. No plate. No arrest.”

“Was anything taken?”

“Calver told responding officers he didn’t keep money there and nothing appeared to be missing. He played it down. Did not submit a later property list.”

“Did he say how the person got inside?”

“The latch area had been forced. Door was repaired afterward.”

“Did Calver see the person?”

“No. Neighbor did.”

“Send us the full report.”

“Already on the way.”

“Preserve the apartment exactly as found. Do not run the Amiga, the laptop, or any removable media.”

“Understood.”

Daniels ended the call.

Navarro watched the landscape pass.

“Someone entered his current home three months ago,” she said. “Nothing was reported missing.”

“That is what Calver said.”

“And now his old room looks searched.”

“That is what it looks like.”

She glanced at him.

“You think the first search told them they were looking in the wrong house?”

“I think we have two separate scenes and no evidence yet that the same person touched both.”

“You didn’t answer me.”

“I did.”

Navarro turned back toward the windshield.

After a moment, she said, “You’re becoming difficult.”

“Henry says that happens when you spend too much time with Michael.”

That drew the beginning of a smile from her, but it disappeared before it fully formed.

Daniels’ phone vibrated with a secure message.

The voluntary recovery of Frank Kessler’s attic file had been completed. Officers had entered only the described area after confirming consent again by phone. The document box had been photographed in place, sealed, and transported without opening it at the house.

Daniels read the message twice.

“Frank’s file is secured.”

Navarro nodded.

“One surviving person gives us a complete file.”

“One dead person gives us a searched bedroom.”

“And Michael gives us neither.”

Daniels did not answer.

The road continued west.


***

The house in Fort Myers looked like the kind of place no one noticed unless they had been invited there.

Single story. Pale exterior. A short driveway. Trimmed shrubs along the front wall. A small decorative flag beside the porch had wrapped itself around the pole in the damp air.

Two marked vehicles stood at the curb. Another occupied part of the driveway without blocking the garage.

The garage door was closed.

An officer met Daniels and Navarro beneath the porch roof. He gave them protective coverings and a brief scene status before they entered.

“No sign of forced entry at the exterior doors,” he said. “The side door was unlocked when responders arrived. Calver mentioned it during the call.”

“Anyone else inside?” Daniels asked.

“No.”

“Parents?”

“Still in Hazlehurst, Georgia. Local officers have reached the relative they are staying with. They have been told there has been a death, but no detailed interview has been conducted yet.”

“Good.”

“We held notification until someone could be physically present with them.”

The officer opened the front door.

The house smelled faintly of furniture polish, laundry detergent, and the air-conditioning system that had continued running through everything.

Family photographs covered one section of the hallway wall.

Elias Calver appeared at several ages.

A boy in a school portrait with hair combed too neatly.

A teenager standing beside a bicycle.

A young man in a graduation gown.

Later photographs showed him with his parents at holidays, outside restaurants, beside a lake, in front of the same house.

Nothing about the images suggested the person Frank had described.

No recovery system.

No anger.

No old access roles.

Only a son who had kept returning long enough to appear in the next frame.

Daniels stopped briefly at one of the more recent photographs. Calver stood between his parents outside the house, one arm behind his mother and the other resting against his father’s shoulder. His face was clear. The image was no more than a few years old.

“Document that one,” Daniels said.

The scene officer photographed the framed image in place, then the wall around it.

Navarro looked toward the hallway.

“The bedrooms?”

The officer led them past a bathroom and two open doors.

The first room was unmistakably prepared for guests.

Neutral bedding.

An empty luggage rack.

A lamp on each side of the bed.

Two folded towels placed on top of the comforter.

The dresser drawers were closed. Nothing personal sat on the nightstand.

The room beside it was different before they crossed the threshold.

A *Jurassic Park* poster occupied most of one wall. A *Ghostbusters* poster hung above an old desk, its corners flattened beneath yellowed plastic strips. The furniture belonged to another decade even where the bedding did not.

The bed had been used.

One side of the blanket had been pushed down and left twisted near the foot. The pillow still held a shallow depression. A charging cable remained connected to the wall beside the nightstand, its loose end resting where a phone might have been.

Daniels stayed at the doorway.

On the closet floor, three old cardboard boxes stood open.

Another had collapsed slightly at one corner. Papers, cables, plastic cases, school binders, and small objects remained inside them, but each box looked as though someone had worked through it rather than emptied it in anger.

The upper closet shelf showed lines in the dust where the boxes had stood.

At the desk, two drawers remained partly open. A third had been pushed inward at an angle and stopped before closing.

No modern computer sat there.

A small television occupied a low stand in the corner. Its curved glass screen reflected the room in gray. No remote lay nearby. An original Nintendo Entertainment System sat beneath it, one cable running toward the television and another disappearing behind the stand.

A gold cartridge sat inside the open cartridge bay.

Daniels looked at it for less than a second.

“Link.”

Navarro turned.

“What?”

He pointed toward the NES.

“The kid. Link.”

Her eyes followed his hand.

“I know who Link is.”

Daniels looked at the gold cartridge again.

“I used to love the music.”

Navarro studied him for a moment.

Then both of them returned to the room.

The brief warmth of recognition vanished as quickly as it had appeared.

Near the desk lay a large old game box with its top open.

Navarro read the title.

*The Secret of Monkey Island.*

Beside it remained the manual and the circular Dial-A-Pirate copy-protection wheel.

She did not touch any of it.

“They left that,” she said.

Daniels looked down.

“What is it?”

“Old copy protection.” She indicated the wheel. “Collectors pay ridiculous money for complete ones.”

“Then this wasn’t ordinary theft.”

“Or whoever searched the room did not know what was valuable.”

Daniels looked from the open game box to the open storage boxes.

“They opened containers. They checked drawers. They left the television, the console, the cartridge, and that.”

Navarro’s gaze moved slowly around the room.

“No Amiga here.”

“The officers found one at his apartment.”

“And the expensive piece stayed here.”

She lowered herself slightly to examine the nearest box from outside its boundary.

“Someone was looking for information. Or something small enough to hide with it.”

“Possible.”

“Everything is possible with you.”

“Not everything.”

Navarro looked toward the unmade bed.

“Calver could have done this himself.”

“Yes.”

“He slept here.”

“Appears that way.”

“He may have come here because he needed something he left behind.”

“Or someone else searched before he arrived.”

“Or after.”

“Or years ago.”

She glanced at him.

“You really know how to improve a conversation.”

The scene officer remained near the hallway, wisely silent.

Daniels pointed toward the boxes.

“Photograph every layer before removal. Separate anything bearing Calver’s name, project identifiers, old address labels, storage dates, or references to Chesapeake. Do not assume the disorder is recent until the parents tell us what the room looked like before they left.”

The officer nodded.

Navarro stepped closer to the desk without entering the marked working area.

“No dust pattern showing a missing computer,” she said. “No open space large enough for one.”

“So whatever he used at the apartment stayed at the apartment.”

“His current technology stayed with his current life.”

“And this room held the old one.”

She looked toward the posters, then the boxes.

“Maybe that is why someone came here.”

Navarro looked once more at the open *Monkey Island* box.

“The packaging is complete enough to be valuable,” she said. “And it stayed.”

Daniels looked toward the open closet boxes.

“So whoever did this was not collecting.”

“Looks that way.”

The garage waited at the other end of the house.


***

The door between the kitchen and garage stood open.

Beyond it, the scene was ordinary enough to resist what had happened there.

Metal shelves held cleaning products, paint cans, extension cords, and folded tarps. A lawn spreader stood beside a stack of plastic plant pots. Two fishing rods leaned against the far wall. A folding chair hung from a hook beneath a row of hand tools.

The parents’ vehicle space was empty.

Calver’s own vehicle remained outside.

Medical personnel had already withdrawn. The body remained under the control of the scene and medical examiner, screened from unnecessary view.

Daniels did not need to look long.

There was no visible struggle.

No overturned shelving.

No broken object suggesting another person had been forced through the space.

Nothing in the immediate scene contradicted the initial assessment.

Nothing made it final either.

Calver’s wallet and keys had been documented.

His mobile phone remained in his trouser pocket.

The cordless handset lay on the concrete several feet away, where it had come to rest after the call. The handset display showed the completed emergency call. Its battery indicator was low but still active.

“No note?” Daniels asked.

“None in the garage,” the scene officer said. “Nothing obvious in the bedroom or kitchen. We have not begun a full document search.”

“The call recording?”

“Available.”

Navarro looked toward the open connection door.

“Play it here.”

The officer used a secured device.

The recording began with the dispatcher requesting the address.

Calver’s voice was calm.

Not relaxed.

Not emotionless.

Controlled in the way of someone who had already decided which parts of the next minutes mattered.

“The address on your screen is correct.”

The dispatcher asked him to confirm it anyway.

He did.

“My name is Elias Nathan Calver. This is my parents’ house.”

“What is the emergency, Mr. Calver?”

“Send police and medical. I’m in the garage.”

“Is anyone else in the house?”

“No. I’m alone.”

“Are you injured?”

There was a pause.

“I will be by the time they arrive.”

The dispatcher’s voice changed immediately. She began giving instructions, asking where he was, what he had access to, whether he could move away from anything dangerous.

Calver answered only some of the questions.

“The side door is unlocked.”

“Elias, stay on the line with me.”

“My parents are in Hazlehurst, Georgia.”

“We will make sure someone contacts them. I need you to stay with me.”

“Please don’t call them until someone is there.”

The dispatcher continued talking. She asked him to move into the house. She asked him to put distance between himself and anything he might use to harm himself.

For several seconds, Calver said nothing.

Then the dispatcher asked:

“Can you tell me why you’re doing this?”

His breathing was audible.

“I thought I could fix it before anyone else got hurt.”

The dispatcher responded immediately.

“Fix what, Elias?”

No answer.

“Who else could get hurt?”

Calver’s voice returned more quietly.

“Tell them I’m sorry.”

“Tell who?”

The connection remained open.

The dispatcher kept speaking to him.

There were no more intelligible words.

The recording ended when the line disconnected.

No one in the garage spoke.

Navarro looked at the secured playback device.

“Before anyone else got hurt.”

Daniels nodded.

“That could mean Michael.”

“It could mean several people.”

“He said *anyone else*.”

“Yes.”

“Meaning he believed someone had already been hurt.”

“Or believed he had.”

Navarro looked toward Calver’s covered form.

“That is not the same thing.”

“No.”

The scene officer lowered the volume on the device.

Daniels looked at the cordless handset.

“He made sure the address attached to the call was this house.”

“He also made sure his own phone stayed untouched,” Navarro said.

“Maybe.”

She looked at him.

“His mobile was in his pocket. He walked into the house, took the cordless handset from its base, brought it into the garage, and called from the line registered to the location.”

“That is a sequence.”

“It is a choice.”

“It does not tell us why.”

“No.”

Navarro turned toward the phone still secured in Calver’s pocket.

“But it may tell us what he wanted left intact.”

The phone had been photographed in place before removal. The medical examiner approved its recovery from the clothing while maintaining the body’s evidentiary condition. Navarro wore fresh gloves and accepted the sealed device from the scene officer.

It remained powered on.

The lock screen displayed no useful notification text.

A fingerprint icon appeared near the lower edge.

Navarro looked at Daniels.

“His thumb may still unlock it.”

Daniels glanced toward the scene supervisor.

The authorization was given for the limited purpose of preserving the device’s accessible state in a time-sensitive missing-person investigation. No one would begin a broad search in the garage. Navarro would document what appeared, isolate the phone from networks, and secure it for full examination.

She stood beside Calver.

For the first time since entering the garage, her professional distance seemed to narrow.

“Sorry,” she said.

She guided his thumb to the sensor.

The phone unlocked.

The screen returned to the last active application.

A photograph filled it.

A man stepped out of a compact car in the Chesapeake Signal Tech parking lot. He wore ordinary work clothes and carried something in one hand. His face was partly turned away, but not enough to prevent recognition.

Steve Harper.

Navarro went still.

“That’s Harper.”

Daniels stepped closer but did not touch the phone.

“When?”

The media viewer showed a date several days before the package handoff.

Monday, July 27, 2026.

The value was preliminary until extraction confirmed it, but it belonged to the received item, not to anything created that morning.

“Three days before the parking-lot delivery,” Navarro said.

“Did Calver take it?”

She backed out only far enough to preserve and identify the application.

Signal.

The image had been opened from a conversation thread. The visible contact no longer had a saved name. No caption accompanied the photograph. No surviving message appeared directly above or below it in the current view.

Navarro did not scroll farther.

“He received it.”

“You’re sure?”

“This is the Signal media viewer. The local record marks the item as inbound.”

“Sender?”

“Not from what is visible here.”

She enabled airplane mode, then placed the phone inside a shielded evidence enclosure while leaving it powered.

“The contact was removed from his address book. The thread remains, but the profile does not currently give me a person.”

“Phone number?”

“Not necessarily visible. Signal supports usernames. Full extraction may recover account identifiers or remnants, but right now I have an inbound photograph and an unresolved contact.”

“Text?”

“None attached.”

Daniels looked at Steve’s image before the screen disappeared behind the enclosure.

“He didn’t need a caption.”

“Or the rest was deleted.”

“Did Calver send it anywhere?”

“Nothing in the visible thread indicates forwarding.”

“That is not proof he didn’t.”

“No.”

Navarro reviewed the limited state photographs made during the unlock. A locally stored copy appeared in the recent-files index.

Its name was:

```text
steve.jpg
```

“He renamed it,” she said.

Daniels looked at her.

“Steve.”

“Yes.”

“So he knew who the photograph showed.”

“Or the sender told him before the rest of the context disappeared.”

Daniels considered that.

Steve had described the unknown man approaching him in the Chesapeake parking lot and using his name immediately.

Not *sir*.

Not *hey*.

Steve.

Someone had selected him before the package arrived.

Someone had watched him leave his car.

Someone had sent that image to Elias Calver.

Navarro looked toward the open door into the house.

“We know Harper uses Signal.”

“For what?”

“Gadget groups. Hardware discussions. Private trading conversations. He told us that during the device review.”

“You think Calver contacted him there?”

“I think Calver received a photograph of him there. We still have no direct chat between their accounts.”

Daniels nodded.

“Calver had the image.”

“He saved it.”

“He named it.”

“And apparently did not forward the saved copy.”

The distinction mattered.

Calver might have been directing the operation.

He might have been following instructions.

He might have saved the photograph because he needed it.

Or because he feared he would someday need to prove that someone had sent it.

Daniels looked toward the covered form again.

“I thought I could fix it.”

Navarro followed his gaze.

“Before anyone else got hurt.”

Neither of them said Michael’s name.

They did not need to.


***

The first forensic review of the mobile phone began before they left Fort Myers.

Only preliminary preservation findings were available.

The Signal photograph had arrived on July 27.

Calver had saved a local copy afterward and renamed it `steve.jpg`.

No retained metadata proved that the image had been captured by Calver’s phone. Its characteristics were more consistent with a received file that had been processed by the messaging application.

The contact associated with the thread remained unresolved.

No surviving caption was present.

No visible forwarding action followed the image.

The phone would require a complete examination before any stronger conclusion could be made.

The 911 call remained what it was: a self-initiated call from the parents’ landline, with no explicit reference to Michael, Chesapeake, `CM-047`, or the recovery system.

The old bedroom remained a searched-looking room whose condition could not be dated until the parents were interviewed.

The apartment in Lake Placid remained orderly.

The break-in report from three months earlier remained an unsolved unlawful entry that Calver had minimized.

Each fact pointed toward another.

None completed it.

Before leaving the house, Daniels returned to the hallway.

The recent family photograph still hung among the others.

Calver stood clearly between his parents.

Daniels looked at it for several seconds.

Then he asked for a high-resolution evidentiary photograph suitable for a neutral identification procedure.

The frame remained on the wall.

Only the image traveled with them.

***

It was fully dark by the time Daniels and Navarro returned east.

The rain had passed.

Water reflected the headlights from shallow depressions in the road, then disappeared beneath the vehicle.

Navarro worked from the passenger seat now, reading the initial reports in silence.

Daniels kept both hands on the wheel.

After several miles, she said, “Calver was not alone in this.”

Daniels did not answer immediately.

“We have an unknown Signal contact,” he said.

“Who sent him a surveillance photograph of Harper.”

“A photograph taken without Harper’s awareness.”

“Three days before the delivery.”

“Yes.”

“And Calver saved it under Harper’s first name.”

“Yes.”

“That is not passive.”

“No.”

“It also is not command.”

“No.”

Navarro closed the report.

“If Calver was the man who delivered the package, we still do not know who prepared it.”

“We do not know Calver delivered it.”

“Not yet.”

“We do not know who photographed Steve.”

“No.”

“We do not know who sent the image.”

“No.”

“We do not know whether the same person drove the Yukon.”

“No.”

Navarro watched the road.

“And we still do not know where Michael is.”

Daniels’ grip tightened slightly on the wheel, then relaxed.

He placed a call to Chesapeake.

Steve Harper was instructed to return to the building for a follow-up identification procedure. He was told only that investigators needed clarification about the parking-lot contact. He was not given Calver’s name, photograph, or status.

Frank had been allowed to leave after the attic file was secured and after confirming that he would remain reachable. Brenda had gone home. Henry had not returned.

The building would be opened for Daniels, Navarro, and Steve only.

“What about Sarah?” Navarro asked after the call.

“Not tonight.”

“She’ll know something happened if we wait too long.”

“I will call her when we have something that concerns Michael rather than only Calver.”

Navarro considered that.

“We may be very close to having both.”

“Close is not the same thing.”

“No,” she said. “It isn’t.”

They continued east.

***

Steve arrived at Chesapeake before they did.

He waited in the reception area with his hands in the pockets of a lightweight jacket. The building’s overhead lights made him look younger and more tired than he had during the earlier interviews.

He stood when Daniels and Navarro entered.

“Did you find Michael?”

“No,” Daniels said.

Steve’s shoulders dropped slightly.

“Then what is this?”

“We need you to look at a photograph.”

“Of the guy?”

Daniels did not answer.

They moved into the small conference room.

The coffee pot was gone. The old personnel ledger and Frank’s papers had been removed. Only the clean tabletop remained beneath the lights.

Navarro placed a tablet in front of Steve.

The image showed the family photograph from the hallway in Fort Myers.

Calver stood between his parents.

No face had been circled.

No name appeared on the screen.

Daniels sat across from Steve.

“I am going to ask an open question. Take as much time as you need.”

Steve looked uneasy.

“Okay.”

“Do you recognize anyone in this photograph?”

Navarro turned the tablet toward him.

Steve glanced at it.

His eyes moved across the three people.

Then they returned to Calver.

The change in him happened before he spoke.

His hands came out of his pockets.

His mouth opened slightly.

He leaned closer to the screen without touching it.

“That’s him.”

Daniels watched his face.

“Who?”

Steve lifted one hand and pointed directly at Elias Calver.

“The man from the parking lot.”
