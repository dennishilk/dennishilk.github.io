# Chapter 15 — Ground Truth

**Saturday, August 8, 2026**

The morning had not driven away the night’s humidity.

It hung over the land in a warm, heavy layer and gathered beneath Daniels’s collar before he had even reached the damaged inner gate. Brown water stood in the shallow ditches beside the old access road. Thin skins of pollen and insects rested on the surface. Every step through the higher grass produced a brief rustle that stopped almost as soon as it began.

Deputy Lewis Grant walked ahead. Henry followed several paces behind Daniels, carrying nothing except a clear document sleeve containing copies of the old site plans and the mapped antenna corridor.

The orange marker beside the collapsed fence still stood where Grant had placed it three days earlier. The access tape had been replaced. Beyond it, C.M.T.A. looked almost unchanged: weathered concrete, faded structures, rusting mounts, and vegetation slowly reclaiming everything people had left behind.

Only the woman at the edge of the clearing was new.

She was not standing near the low concrete building.

She was not looking at the panel antenna.

She was almost fifty yards away, where the old paved surface gave way to low vegetation and a shallow drainage depression. One boot rested on the concrete, the other in the grass. She held a small field notebook in one hand and used the end of a folded measuring stick to move dead stems aside.

Grant raised a hand.

“Katharina.”

The woman straightened and came toward them. She did not cross the center of the old concrete pad. Instead, she followed its edge, choosing the firmer ground without appearing to think about it.

Grant waited until she reached them.

“Officer Andrew Daniels,” he said. “He’s leading the investigation into Michael Weber’s disappearance.”

She offered Daniels her hand.

“Katharina Brandt.”

She pronounced it carefully, without exaggeration.

Then one corner of her mouth moved.

“Katrin is fine. Most people here find the rest of it unnecessarily ambitious.”

Grant pointed toward Henry.

“Henry Sullivan, Chesapeake Signal Tech. He knows more about the old equipment than anyone we could get out here on a Saturday.”

Henry lifted his eyebrows slightly.

“That sounds more official than it is.”

“You knew what not to touch,” Grant said. “That already puts you ahead of most people.”

He turned back to Daniels.

“Katrin works land management for the South Florida Water Management District. Wetlands, drainage routes, damaged access roads, old service corridors. The property west of the C.M.T.A. boundary is District land.”

Daniels looked beyond the low vegetation behind her.

“That’s why we weren’t supposed to continue along the antenna corridor without someone from the District.”

“That,” Grant said, “and because I didn’t want Henry wandering into water he thought was grass.”

Henry looked toward the drainage depression.

“I feel increasingly valued.”

Katharina glanced at him.

“You’ll feel less valued after the mosquitoes find you.”

Daniels looked back toward the place where she had been standing.

“What were you checking?”

“Nothing I’d call evidence.” She tucked the measuring stick beneath her arm. “I wanted to see where the water goes.”

Grant gestured toward the old plans in Henry’s hand.

“They’ve been thinking about the antenna.”

“I saw the map you sent.”

Henry opened the document sleeve and removed the corridor plot.

“The panel at C.M.T.A. doesn’t transmit equally in every direction,” he said. “Its main coverage is roughly inside this area. We don’t know where the other radio was, only that it should have been somewhere within the corridor.”

Katharina studied the page for only a few seconds.

“How precise is that?”

“Not very. The beam widens with distance. We don’t know the exact antenna model, the original adjustment, or what the vegetation looked like when it was installed.”

“But it points west.”

“Yes.”

She handed the map back.

“There’s an old structure a little over a mile that way.”

Grant leaned closer to the map.

“What kind of structure?”

“Small concrete-block building. Four heavy foundations around it. There used to be a fire observation tower there.”

“The tower still standing?” Daniels asked.

“Only part of one support. The steel structure has been gone for years.”

“What was the building used for?”

Katharina shrugged once.

“Maps. Equipment. Telephone. Radio, probably. Maybe someone sat inside when the tower was staffed. It predates most of the District records I use.”

Henry looked from her to the corridor.

The location she had indicated lay close to its center.

“Can we get there?” Daniels asked.

“On foot.”

“Safely?”

Katharina looked toward a dark pool between the grasses.

“Safe is a large word out here. But I know a drier route.”

She pointed toward a barely visible rise running west from the property line.

“The direct line crosses low ground. After the rain, some of it will look firm until your second step. The old service route stays higher.”

“Alligators?” Henry asked.

“Possible near the standing water.”

“And snakes?”

“Also possible.”

Henry waited.

Katharina slipped the notebook into a pocket.

“The snakes are usually the smaller problem. They’re only easier to step on.”

Grant checked his radio and reported their direction and the number of people moving off the C.M.T.A. property. The signal was weak, but the message went through.

Katharina started first.

Daniels followed, then Grant and Henry.

For the first hundred yards, the route ran along a low embankment that was almost invisible beneath the grass. The ground underfoot remained firm. On either side, shallow water spread between reeds and dark islands of vegetation.

The air barely moved.

Within minutes, Henry slapped the side of his neck.

Katharina looked back.

“One?”

“At least three.”

“Then the others haven’t realized we’re here yet.”

Henry looked at Daniels.

“She has a reassuring profession.”

Grant removed a small bottle from a side pocket and handed it to him.

“Insect repellent.”

Henry accepted it.

“You could have mentioned this before we started walking.”

“I wanted to see how long you lasted.”

Katharina continued ahead, but Daniels caught the briefest suggestion of a smile.

The service path passed through lower terrain, then rose so gradually that the change was easier to see in the vegetation than to feel underfoot. In places, remnants of an old gravel layer showed between roots and grass. Pale stones lay scattered across the route as if the soil had been pushing them back toward the surface for years.

Katharina stopped at a narrow washout.

“One at a time.”

She placed one boot on a firm root and crossed the dark depression. Daniels followed. Water stood beneath the leaves even though the surface had looked dry.

Henry stopped at the edge.

“What happens if I miss the root?”

“You get wet.”

“Nothing else?”

“We’ll find out if you miss it.”

Henry placed his foot exactly where hers had been.

Grant waited until he was across.

“Very graceful.”

“I’m a technician,” Henry said. “Not a swamp dancer.”

“That explains some things.”

Beyond the washout, branches reached across the path from both sides. Katharina pushed them away with her forearm, never reaching blindly into the thicker leaves.

Then she stopped without warning.

Daniels stopped behind her.

A long, dark shape lay across the path, curved and partly hidden beneath the grass.

Henry did not move.

“Is this one of those moments when nobody should say what they’re thinking?”

Katharina studied it.

“You’re thinking snake.”

“No.”

Grant looked at him.

“Yes.”

Katharina touched the shape with the end of the measuring stick. A piece of bark broke free and fell into the grass.

“Branch.”

Henry exhaled.

“I knew that.”

“Of course.”

She stepped over it.

Several minutes later, the embankment broadened. The vegetation opened in places, revealing straight lines that did not belong to the natural landscape: an old ditch, two low concrete markers, and a leaning post with traces of faded yellow paint.

Katharina pointed to it.

“Old boundary marker. Most of this is older than the current land-management plan.”

“How old is the route?” Daniels asked.

“Hard to say. Probably older than the District’s ownership. Service corridors survive because people keep using them as long as they still lead somewhere.”

Henry looked back.

Nothing of C.M.T.A. remained visible through the trees.

“How far?”

Katharina checked the GPS receiver at her belt.

“A little over a mile.”

“It felt longer.”

“That was the mosquitoes.”

The route bent slightly north.

Katharina slowed.

“We’re close.”

At first, Daniels saw only a pale straight edge between the trees. Then another. As they moved closer, the shapes became low walls built from gray concrete blocks.

The building stood on a small raised area, barely larger than an equipment shed. Dirt, leaves, and plants covered much of its flat roof. A metal door faced the old service route.

Four heavy concrete foundations surrounded it.

Three ended just above the grass.

The fourth rose higher. Its upper section had broken away, leaving an uneven dark edge with rusted steel protruding from the concrete.

Katharina stopped at the edge of the clearing.

“That was the tower site.”

Daniels studied the foundations.

“When was it removed?”

“Before my time. As far as I know, the tower had already been gone for years.”

Grant looked toward the building.

“And that?”

“Never demolished.”

“Still used?”

“Not officially.”

Henry had opened the map again.

He looked first at the antenna corridor.

Then at the concrete-block building and the four foundations.

The location sat almost exactly inside it.

Katharina moved two steps to her left to see past a cluster of branches.

Then she stopped.

“That’s newer.”

Daniels followed her gaze.

A small solar panel sat along the front edge of the roof. It had been mounted low and slightly back, almost hidden from the service route by branches. Dust covered its surface, but unlike the rest of the roof, it was not buried beneath moss or vegetation.

“How new?” Daniels asked.

“Not weeks. But much newer than the building.”

Grant studied the mounting brackets.

“District installation?”

“Not ours. District equipment is marked, and there would be no reason to put one small panel on an officially abandoned structure.”

Henry was no longer looking at the roof.

“Daniels.”

The tone was enough.

Daniels turned.

On the side of the broken tower support facing away from the path, a flat rectangular housing had been fixed to the concrete. From the clearing, only its pale edge was visible.

Henry moved one step closer, then stopped outside the remains of a short rusted fence.

“That looks like a panel antenna.”

Katharina looked from the solar panel to the tower support.

“Like the one at C.M.T.A.?”

“Very much like it.”

“LoRa?” Daniels asked.

“Possible. Probably. I’d need to see the housing and connections more closely.”

Grant looked back toward the direction from which they had come. C.M.T.A. was more than a mile away, hidden behind vegetation.

“It points back.”

Henry checked the map.

He did not need long.

“Almost exactly.”

Katharina did not approach the support. Instead, she moved sideways until she could see behind it.

“There’s a cable.”

A black line ran down the concrete beneath the antenna. It disappeared beneath grass and dead leaves, then emerged several yards later and continued toward the block building.

Henry followed it with his eyes.

“That could be the connection.”

Daniels looked between the antenna, the cable, and the solar panel.

“The other end of the C.M.T.A. link?”

“It sits inside the corridor. The antenna points back. It has independent power and a cable running into the building.” Henry lowered the map. “Yes. Very likely.”

Katharina kept looking at the solar panel.

“Then the building may have been abandoned.”

She pointed toward the roofline.

“But it wasn’t unused.”

She walked several paces along the edge of the clearing without moving directly toward the door.

Then she raised one hand.

“Wait.”

Daniels stopped.

Katharina indicated the ground beneath the narrow roof overhang. Less debris had collected there than on the open ground. The rain had darkened the sand but had not completely washed it smooth.

Two elongated depressions remained between small branches and plant matter.

Daniels crouched at a distance.

“Footprints?”

“Possibly. At least one probably is.”

The front depression ended in an indistinct curve. Behind it, a flatter mark suggested that someone had turned a foot or shifted weight while standing near the door.

No tread pattern remained.

Grant looked toward the sky.

“After Thursday night’s rain?”

“Could be. Could be earlier. The roof keeps some of the water off this spot.”

“How old?” Daniels asked.

“A few days is possible. More than that would be dishonest.”

She indicated the grass beside the marks rather than the depressions themselves. Several stems had been bent toward the door. Some had begun to rise again. Others remained trapped beneath damp leaves.

“Someone may have stood here,” she said. “Or set something heavy down. It isn’t enough to turn into a person.”

Daniels photographed the area from several angles and placed a small marker beside the edge without touching the possible impression.

Then he looked at the building.

The metal door contained a small rectangular window clouded by dirt and webs. A narrow vent sat to one side. The concrete-block walls showed old cracks but no obvious bulging. The roofline did not visibly sag.

“Stay here,” Daniels said.

Grant moved beside him.

“I’m coming.”

Daniels nodded.

Henry remained with Katharina near the marked ground.

Daniels and Grant approached from the side, avoiding the area directly in front of the door. Daniels first moved around the right wall. There was no second entrance, only another vent and the black cable entering a weathered metal conduit.

A small window sat high on the rear wall. One pane was cracked but remained in the frame.

Daniels shone his light through it.

He saw a wall, the end of a metal shelf, and part of the floor.

No movement.

No visible person.

Grant checked the opposite side.

“No other access.”

They returned to the front.

Grant stood clear of the door.

“Sheriff’s Office,” he called. “Anyone inside?”

No answer.

Only insects and the faint metallic tapping of something moving near the broken tower support.

Grant called again.

Nothing.

Daniels positioned himself beside the frame and looked through the small window. His view reached only a few feet into the dark room.

He placed one hand on the handle and moved it slightly.

The door was not locked.

He pulled it open only a few inches.

The hinges produced a dry groan.

Daniels stopped and waited.

The interior remained silent.

He opened it farther until daylight crossed the floor. His flashlight moved slowly from left to right: narrow room, metal shelving, a small worktable, old boxes, newer cable channels on the wall.

No person.

No immediate visible collapse hazard.

He waited several more seconds before looking back.

“You can come closer. Stay clear of the ground in front of the door and don’t touch anything.”

Katharina led Henry around the marked impressions along the firmer side.

Before anyone entered, Daniels photographed the overall arrangement: the building, the four tower foundations, the broken support, the panel antenna, the solar module, and the cable route between them.

He took closer photographs of the antenna mount, its orientation, the wiring along the concrete, the roof-mounted panel, and the lines entering the building.

It took only a few minutes.

“That’s enough for now,” he said.

Daniels entered first.

The concrete floor felt solid. Dark water marks stained the ceiling, but no panels hung loose and no section visibly sagged. He checked the two corners behind the door and shone his light beneath the narrow table.

“Clear.”

Grant entered next.

Henry and Katharina followed only after Daniels indicated a route along the left wall.

The room was scarcely larger than a single-car garage. Rusted mounts, coiled wire, and several flat map cases filled the shelves. A yellowed land-use chart still hung on one wall, though water damage had dissolved portions of its lines.

Katharina stopped in front of it.

“This is the old fire-observation area.”

She indicated a faded symbol.

“The tower is marked here. So is the service route.”

Daniels looked at a thin black line running west from the building. Its label was almost illegible.

“And that?”

Katharina shook her head.

“Not my equipment.”

Henry was already studying the gray housing beneath the worktable.

He did not crouch or touch it. He only followed the cables with his light.

The black line from the panel antenna entered the left side of the housing. The solar module fed a small charge controller and a battery on the lower shelf.

A third cable left the right side of the modern housing.

It ran for only several feet before reaching a much older terminal block.

Henry followed the thick cloth-covered wires from that block into a metal pipe that disappeared through the floor.

“Telephone,” he said.

“A normal telephone line?” Daniels asked.

“Originally, probably. For the tower or the forestry office.” Henry pointed toward two terminals. “But this pair was reconnected later.”

Grant examined the insulation.

“How much later?”

“Not with the original installation. This cable is much newer.” Henry looked back toward the modern radio housing. “Someone deliberately tied the new wireless link into the old line.”

Daniels looked toward the conduit in the wall.

“So the C.M.T.A. signal reached this building over the antenna.”

“Yes.”

“And from here it could continue over the telephone pair.”

Henry waited before answering.

“That appears to be the design.”

Katharina looked at the metal pipe vanishing into the floor.

“Where does it go?”

“We’ll have to trace it.”

Beside the terminal block sat a small beige machine Daniels had initially mistaken for an old cash register. Its plastic had yellowed at the edges. Two mechanical buttons and a narrow paper slot occupied the front.

A strip of continuous paper hung almost to the floor.

The upper lines had faded until only isolated letters remained.

Farther down, the printing was darker.

Daniels raised one hand.

“No closer.”

He approached from the side and directed his flashlight at the paper.

The first clearly readable line was a date.

```text
07/31/26
```

Below it:

```text
RELAY SESSION RECEIVED
LOCAL PROFILE: CM-047
SEQUENCE: 00417
```

Henry moved half a step closer.

“The same event.”

Daniels studied the number.

At C.M.T.A., it had appeared beneath the accepted remote access. Inside the Chesapeake controller, it had been the last outbound sequence.

Now it existed a third time.

“The transaction reached this relay,” Grant said.

“At least the same transaction did,” Daniels replied.

A larger blank space followed the system lines.

Then more text appeared.

The characters were still machine-printed, but the lines were arranged as a stored message rather than a status report.

Daniels read silently.

```text
AC-21 IS USING THE OLD RECOVERY PATH.

THE KEY WAS NEVER STORED.

THE SYSTEM CANNOT COMPLETE IT WITHOUT ALPHA.

THAT IS WHY THEY NEED ME.

M.W.
```

No one spoke.

Outside, insects hummed in the heat. A branch moved against metal near the broken tower support and produced a quiet, irregular tap.

Henry read the message a second time.

“Michael knew what the link was being used for.”

Daniels kept his attention on the paper.

“The initials alone don’t prove he wrote it.”

“No.”

“But AC-21, Alpha, CM-047, and 00417 weren’t public information.”

“No.”

Katharina looked at the old printer.

“When was the message entered?”

Henry turned his light toward the modern gray housing.

“Not necessarily on Friday. It may have been stored earlier and set to release when a specific transaction arrived.”

“His test,” Grant said.

“Possibly.”

Daniels read the final line again.

```text
THAT IS WHY THEY NEED ME.
```

Until that moment, they had assumed Michael disappeared because he discovered a connection, recognized an invalid contact, or examined the wrong device.

The message described something else.

Someone had not taken him only to silence him.

Someone needed him alive.

Daniels stepped away from the printer.

“The entire installation stays untouched. We need Navarro, radio specialists, and someone who can trace that line.”

Grant reached for his radio.

Henry continued looking at the paper.

“If AC-21 was the external authority and Michael was Alpha on the local side…”

“Then we aren’t only looking for Calver,” Daniels said.

He opened his notebook.

“We’re looking for everyone who understood how this project worked.”

He did not write any names yet.

Only a heading.

```text
FORMER PROJECT PERSONNEL
```
