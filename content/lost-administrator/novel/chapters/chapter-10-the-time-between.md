# Chapter 10 — The Time Between

*Wednesday, August 5, 2026*

For several seconds, nobody spoke.

The low hum from the battery cabinet was barely more than an electrical presence. Beside it, the charge controller continued to show the battery and solar-panel symbols.

**13.2 V**

An ordinary number in a room that should have been dead.

Below the radio bridge, the narrow strip of paper still projected from the event recorder.

The last line was darker than the others.

**07/31/26   REMOTE ACCESS   CM-047   ACCEPTED**

Daniels raised one hand.

"Nobody touches anything."

Grant stood partly in the doorway to the narrow service compartment. Mercer remained behind him, close enough to see inside without leaving the route he had assessed. Henry had not moved.

"Not the recorder. Not the switches. Not the wiring. Not the covers."

"Wasn't planning to," Henry said.

Daniels removed the compact service camera from his equipment and remained exactly where he was.

The first photograph showed the full compartment from the doorway.

Battery cabinet.

Charge controller.

Fuse holders.

Radio bridge.

Recorder.

A second documented the floor between the threshold and the equipment.

"Mercer. How much of this area remains acceptable?"

Mercer studied the ceiling connections, wall penetrations and low cabinets again. Then he illuminated the narrow path they had used.

"The route to your position remains walkable. No movement in the cracks. Nothing loose above us. Air readings remain normal. I still would not put anyone closer to the rear wall without a reason."

Daniels nodded.

"Then everyone stays behind me."

He photographed the charge controller, its wiring, the fuse holders and the position of every visible switch.

Then the radio enclosure.

**AUXILIARY REMOTE LINK**

**LORA P2P — 902–928 MHz**

**LOW-POWER TELEMETRY BRIDGE**

He removed no cover.

Anything that could not be seen without moving the equipment remained unseen.

Grant looked back toward the second metal door.

"I'll move back for the radio."

"Only over the assessed route."

Grant withdrew through the equipment room. A few seconds later, his voice came faintly through Daniels' radio.

Daniels turned to the recorder.

He photographed the complete housing, then the paper outlet, then the visible lines.

The strip was narrow. Older entries had faded unevenly, some apparently separated by years. Only the final line remained dark against the discolored paper.

"Could there be more than we can see?" Daniels asked.

Henry examined the recorder without leaning closer.

"Possibly. The entry may begin farther inside. Or this may be everything the model prints."

"Time?"

"Not visible."

"Internal memory?"

"Impossible to determine from here. Some only print. Some buffer a few events. With others, the paper is the only useful record."

"And we do not find out by pressing a button."

"Exactly."

Grant's voice came from the vestibule.

"County confirms. Evidence processing is responding, along with digital and radio support."

Daniels answered through his own unit.

"Tell them independent direct-current circuit, active solar charge, legacy telemetry equipment and a visible paper event recorder. Nobody assumes it can be shut down safely."

Grant acknowledged.

Daniels looked toward Mercer.

"Until they arrive, access remains limited."

"I'll keep the vestibule clear."

Daniels documented the visible cable routes. One pair ran to the battery cabinet. Another disappeared into an older duct. A thinner cable left the radio enclosure and passed through the concrete wall.

"Antenna cable?" he asked.

"Probably," Henry said.

"Certain?"

"Certain only that it leaves the radio enclosure and the room."

Daniels nodded.

Henry looked again at the paper.

"The line says the system accepted something."

"Not that Michael was here."

"No."

"Not that Michael transmitted it."

"No."

"And not that this place knew which person was behind CM-047."

Henry remained silent for a moment.

"The system knew an identifier. That is all we can say."

Daniels looked at the dark line.

A date.

An event.

An identifier that had once belonged to Michael.

And one word that meant more than reception but far less than identity.

**ACCEPTED.**

"Then that is how we treat it," Daniels said. "As a technical event. Not identification of a person."

Until the specialists arrived, the paper would remain where it had been since July 31.

Untouched.

And, for the first time, fully guarded.
---
Mercer marked the entrance to the service compartment with red barrier tape from his equipment.

It did not form a complete perimeter. The vestibule was too narrow and the surrounding site too unstable. But it established a line nobody would cross without approval.

Outside, the afternoon heat remained heavy over C.M.T.A.

Mercer stopped beside the low building and looked toward the point where the cable left the wall.

"Nobody stands beneath the roof edge. Nobody leans against the structure."

Henry stepped several yards back.

From there, the cable route became visible.

It did not continue toward the damaged mast. Instead, it ran along the wall and ended a few feet below the roofline at a flat, nearly square antenna on a short metal bracket.

No rod.

No exposed elements.

Only a weathered panel aimed away from the building.

Mercer raised his camera.

"That is the antenna?"

"Very likely," Henry said.

"Still not certain?"

"The cable comes from the radio section and ends there. That is as far as I would go without inspecting it."

Daniels photographed the wall, panel, cable route and roofline. Then he captured the bracket and its fasteners from several angles.

Mercer moved to one side, staying clear of the roof edge.

"Not an omnidirectional antenna."

"It does not look like one," Henry replied. "A panel normally has a preferred direction."

Grant looked along the invisible axis leading away from its face.

"Then the signal came from there."

Henry shook his head.

"No. Then the antenna was probably most sensitive in that direction."

"What changes it?"

"Beam width. Side lobes. Reflections. Terrain. The signal may have arrived near the main direction or from one side."

Daniels looked up at the panel.

"But it gives us an area."

"A rough one. Assuming the bracket still points where it did on Friday."

Mercer examined the fasteners through the camera zoom.

"No obvious damage from here."

"That does not prove it was never adjusted," Daniels said.

Mercer returned to his ATV for a handheld GPS receiver and compass. Then he walked to the entrance in the outer fence.

At the gate, he waited for a stable position.

"Why here?" Grant asked.

"Reproducible point. Open view of the sky. The gate appears on the site plan, and another team can return to it."

He saved the coordinates. Daniels photographed the display and Mercer's position. Grant recorded the estimated accuracy.

Mercer turned toward the concrete building.

From there, the panel was partly visible from the side. He compared its face with the wall, took a compass bearing along the apparent orientation and repeated the measurement from a second position.

"Not perfect," he said. "The distance and bracket introduce an error."

"Record the observed bearing and the possible deviation," Daniels said.

Mercer wrote both in his field notebook. Daniels photographed the page without reading the numbers aloud.

Henry looked from the gate into the indicated landscape.

No visible road followed the direction.

No building or tower identified itself as the other end.

Only distance.

"How wide is the line?" Grant asked.

"It is not a line," Henry said.

He held his hands apart and opened the angle between them.

"A corridor. The farther it extends, the wider it becomes. Without the antenna specifications, we do not know its proper width."

Daniels looked at Mercer's notes.

"But we can place it on a map."

"Yes."

"Not to claim the transmitter stood inside it."

"No."

"Only to preserve the direction in which the system was intended to communicate."

Henry nodded.

Daniels turned to Grant.

"Forward the GPS position, bearing and photographs. No search along the corridor and no contact with property owners yet. Maps first."

Mercer photographed the panel once more from a distance, including the antenna, the edge of the building and part of the horizon.

A rough orientation.

Not a destination.

But the event no longer had only a date and an identifier.

It had a direction.
---

They returned to the low building.

Mercer checked the air in the vestibule again before allowing Daniels and Henry to approach the marked boundary in the equipment room. Grant remained near the inner door where he could watch both spaces.

The paper had not moved.

**07/31/26   REMOTE ACCESS   CM-047   ACCEPTED**

Daniels studied the line.

"We know something was received."

"Probably," Henry said.

Daniels looked at him.

"Why only probably?"

Henry indicated the recorder without moving closer.

"Because the recorder may not be connected directly to the radio receiver. The bridge may receive something, evaluate it and then report an event to another controller. Or the recorder may only log an action triggered somewhere else."

"But no reception, no remote access."

"That would be the reasonable assumption."

Daniels opened his notebook.

"Then separate the meanings."

Henry folded his arms.

"All right."

"First: a signal reaches the system."

"Or a packet the system recognizes as usable."

"Second: the event contains CM-047."

"Yes."

"Third: the system prints ACCEPTED."

"Yes."

"What separates accepted from received?"

Henry looked toward the radio enclosure.

"Received would only say something arrived. Accepted suggests the system did not reject it after whatever checks it performed."

"Whatever checks."

"We do not know more than that."

Daniels wrote a few words.

"What could those checks have been?"

Henry considered the question.

"An assigned device identifier. A specific packet structure. A site-dependent verification value. A shared key. Configuration that existed only on an authorized maintenance unit."

Grant looked through the doorway.

"A password over radio?"

"Possibly. But not necessarily a readable password. It could have been a value both ends knew."

"And CM-047 was that value?"

"Possible," Henry said. "I would not assume it."

Daniels raised his eyes.

"Why not?"

"Because CM-047 is printed openly. It looks more like the name under which the system recorded the transaction. A user, device or site identifier."

"The username."

"Maybe."

"And something else was the password."

Henry made a slight face.

"That is the simple analogy. The actual design may have been different."

Daniels continued writing.

"If I transmit the characters CM-047 today with any radio, the system does not necessarily accept me."

"Very probably not."

"Weak systems exist."

"They do."

Henry looked at the label on the enclosure.

"But this calls itself a telemetry bridge. That suggests structured data, not a radio waiting for somebody to type a line of text."

"Structured data can still be poorly secured."

"Yes."

"A serial number can be copied."

"Yes."

"A shared value can be leaked."

"Yes."

"An old authorized device can still exist."

"Yes."

"And somebody can use the correct equipment without being Michael."

Henry did not answer immediately.

"Yes."

The low compartment became quiet again.

Daniels turned to a clean page.

"Then we do not say the system recognized Michael."

"No."

"We do not say Michael's password was used."

"We do not know that there was a password."

"We say a transaction identified as CM-047 passed the system's existing technical check."

Henry nodded.

"That is the narrowest statement supported by the paper."

Grant moved half a step closer to the doorway.

"What did the system do after accepting it?"

Henry looked past the compartment toward the silent cabinets in the equipment room.

**PRIMARY TELEMETRY UPLINK**

**LEGACY INTERFACE ADAPTER**

**LOCAL CONFIGURATION REQUIRED**

"Unknown."

"Opened something?"

"Possible."

"Turned something on?"

"Possible."

"Transferred data?"

"Possible."

"That is not very helpful."

"It prevents us from choosing the wrong answer."

Daniels closed the notebook.

"Could accepted mean only that the identifier was properly formatted?"

"Yes."

"Not that an action completed."

"Yes."

"Not even that the intended target still worked."

"Correct."

Daniels looked toward the breaker that remained in its middle position.

"Then this small system could have accepted a command while the main installation was unable to act on it."

"Possible."

"Or the command was intended only for this independent section."

"Also possible."

"Or accepted means something we will understand only if we identify the manufacturer and protocol."

"Exactly."

Mercer reported from the vestibule that the requested personnel were approaching the property.

Daniels stepped away from the boundary.

"Then that is where we leave it for now."

He looked at the paper once more.

"Someone or something initiated a transaction under CM-047 on July 31. The system did not reject it."

Henry studied the word at the end of the line.

It was small enough to appear harmless.

But it had not been printed because the receiver heard random noise in the band.

Something about the event had fit.

An identifier.

A device.

A structure.

Perhaps something nobody should still have possessed.

The arriving vehicles became audible outside.

Daniels turned toward the door.

"Now we find out how much this machine still knows."

Behind them, the recorder remained powered.

And beneath Michael's old identifier, the same word waited.

**ACCEPTED.**

---
The responding vehicles stopped outside the inner grounds.

Mercer met them and led the evidence personnel over the same assessed route. Nobody crossed between the damaged buildings or approached the Equipment Shelter.

One evidence technician carried two camera bags and a narrow hard case. Behind her came a specialist in radio and legacy digital systems with test equipment and sealed containers.

Mercer explained the structural restrictions first.

Daniels explained the discovery second.

The technician compared Daniels' photographs with the unchanged scene.

"Nobody has touched it?"

"No."

"Power?"

"Unchanged. The main installation remains off. The small direct-current system is operating from battery and solar."

The radio specialist remained behind the marked line.

"Controls operated?"

"No."

"Paper moved?"

"No."

"Then it stays that way for now."

The evidence technician began with overview, mid-range and detail photographs. Every visible screw, cable penetration and label was documented from more than one angle.

Only after the condition had been preserved a second time did the specialist approach the recorder.

He wore gloves and inspected the front with a small light.

The unit was older than the radio module above it.

Two mechanical buttons.

A paper outlet.

A window showing part of the ribbon mechanism.

No display.

No visible network connector.

No memory-card slot.

"Not a modern logger," Grant said.

"We do not know that yet," the specialist replied. "But it was not built for convenient extraction."

He followed the power lead to a separate fuse holder.

"Independent low-voltage supply. Possibly continuous."

"Enough for a clock?" Daniels asked.

"Easily. The questions are whether the clock survived an outage and whether it was correctly set."

The evidence technician positioned a second camera directly in front of the recorder.

Only then did the specialist loosen the two captive screws on the small front cover. The cover folded forward with the screws still held in place.

Behind it were the ribbon mechanism, two transport rollers and a narrow metal guide.

The specialist illuminated the interior.

Then stopped.

"There is more printing."

Daniels did not come closer.

"Where?"

"Behind the guide."

Beneath the metal, the upper edges of additional characters were visible.

The technician changed lenses.

"Could it belong to an older event?" Daniels asked.

"The spacing is too small."

Henry studied the paper path from the doorway.

"Two-line record?"

"It appears so."

"Then the visible line is the summary."

"And the second may contain supplemental data."

Daniels looked at the buttons.

"Do you need to advance it electrically?"

"No."

A small manual transport wheel sat behind the cover. The specialist photographed it and marked its original position. The technician began continuous video.

"Manual feed only. No button. No new print command."

Daniels approved.

The specialist turned the wheel a few millimeters.

The paper moved.

Below the known line, another row began to appear.

Then a complete word.

**TIME**

He stopped immediately.

More photographs.

A second controlled movement.

The hidden line passed beneath the guide.

**TIME   13:42:18   SEQ 00417**

Nobody spoke.

Daniels looked first at the upper line.

**07/31/26   REMOTE ACCESS   CM-047   ACCEPTED**

Then at the second.

**TIME   13:42:18   SEQ 00417**

"One forty-two and eighteen seconds," Grant said quietly.

Daniels kept his eyes on the paper.

"Is that the time of the access?"

The specialist raised one hand.

"It is the time the recorder associated with the event."

"That was not my question."

"It is the most reliable answer I can give now. We still need to identify the source of the time and determine whether the clock was accurate, reset during an outage or could be set manually."

"But the second line belongs to the event above it."

The specialist compared the spacing with an older faded record. The same pattern was barely visible there.

A primary line.

A smaller line beneath it.

"Yes. The format repeats."

Daniels looked again at the digits.

1:42:18 PM.

The Yukon had left the shared rear access road at 2:03:17 PM.

The janitor had seen Michael walking toward the old archive and backup room at approximately 1:47 PM.

Michael's workstation had recorded a local login at 1:41:26 PM.

Daniels did not say any of it aloud.

The technician photographed both lines together, then separately, then the paper path and rollers.

The specialist examined the exposed electronics without removing anything.

"Small controller. Serial connection to the rest of the assembly. A configuration memory is possible. I do not see evidence of a large event store."

"Then the paper may be all we have."

"For the history, possibly."

"And the clock?"

"The assembly has a crystal. I cannot yet confirm separate backup power."

The charge controller still displayed **13.2 V**.

Stable voltage today did not prove uninterrupted power since July 31.

Daniels opened his notebook and wrote:

**1:41:26 PM — LOCAL LOGIN**

**1:42:18 PM — CM-047 ACCEPTED**

**2:03:17 PM — YUKON**

Fifty-two seconds separated the first two lines.

Twenty minutes and fifty-nine seconds separated the second and third.

Until now, they had followed the Yukon because it had carried Michael away from Chesapeake.

But the system before them had accepted Michael's old identifier before the vehicle appeared on the first camera.

The specialist placed a clear protective cover over the opened recorder.

"The paper stays in the unit until the power, paper path and time circuit are fully documented."

Daniels nodded.

They had already known the date.

Now the event had a time.

And that time fell within Michael's last confirmed minutes at Chesapeake Signal Tech.
---

Daniels looked at the three entries in his notebook.

The radio specialist remained beside the recorder, now protected beneath the transparent cover.

"When do you take the unit?" Daniels asked.

The specialist did not answer immediately.

"Not yet."

Grant looked over from the doorway.

"You are leaving it here?"

"For the moment."

Daniels did not close the notebook.

"Reason."

The specialist pointed in turn toward the recorder, the bridge and the charge controller.

"This is not one isolated item. It is a connected system. If we remove the recorder now, we may destroy the relationship between the input, time source, power state and printed event."

"And if someone transmits again?"

"That could also matter."

Henry looked up at the bridge.

"Assuming it responds again."

"Exactly."

"Could somebody remotely erase the existing line?" Daniels asked.

The specialist looked at the mechanical paper output.

"Not the printout."

"The internal state?"

"Unknown. We have no current evidence that the system supports remote administration or deletion."

"No evidence is not the same as impossible."

"No."

Daniels looked at the cable leading toward the panel antenna.

"Then we have two risks. Disconnect it and alter the original state. Leave it connected and permit another interaction."

"Correct."

"Your recommendation?"

The specialist indicated the equipment room behind them.

"The main installation remains de-energized. The breaker is untouched. This small independent circuit appears to power only the bridge, recorder and associated controller."

"Appears."

"That is the limit of what we know today."

Daniels waited.

"I recommend leaving it connected under constant control. No test transmission. No query. No attempt to communicate with it."

"And if another signal arrives?"

"We record it passively."

Grant looked toward the vestibule.

"How?"

"Independent equipment outside this system. A receiver can capture activity in the relevant band. It transmits nothing and connects to nothing here. It only listens."

Henry nodded slowly.

"Raw radio data."

"If possible. Frequency, bandwidth, timing and signal characteristics. Understanding the payload would be a separate problem."

Daniels looked at the paper.

"The panel antenna remains in place."

"Yes."

"Battery connected."

"Yes."

"Charge controller connected."

"Yes."

"Recorder connected."

"Yes."

Mercer stepped into the vestibule doorway.

"I can control the outer access and establish a fixed post. If this continues overnight, we need lighting and a relief schedule."

Daniels nodded.

"Arrange it."

Grant reached for his radio.

The specialist turned to the evidence technician.

"Set the passive receiver outside the service compartment. Independent battery. No connection to the installation. Position the antenna without disturbing the panel or its bracket."

The technician opened the narrow hard case.

Daniels looked toward Henry.

"Now the times."

Henry glanced at the notebook.

"What do you need?"

"Whether we can already say the access occurred after the login."

"If the recorder clock was correct."

"And we do not know that yet."

"No."

The radio specialist listened while connecting his own coaxial cable.

"The recorder clock is still running," he said. "We can compare its current time with a reference."

Daniels turned toward him.

"Without changing it?"

"If there is a documented read-only interface. If there is not, we do not trigger a test print. Then we examine the time circuit and continue observing the device."

"If it is three minutes wrong today?"

"Then we know a deviation exists today."

"Not whether the same deviation existed Friday."

"Correct."

Henry looked at the charge controller.

"The battery may have powered the clock continuously."

"May have," the specialist said. "We will examine battery age, present condition, any available controller history and whether the clock has separate backup power."

Grant returned from his radio call.

"Do you want Chesapeake contacted?"

Daniels looked at the three times.

"Yes."

"Who?"

"The forensic unit handling Michael's workstation. I do not want a summary. I want the original time sources."

Grant held his pen over the notebook.

Daniels counted them.

"Exact local login. Time zone and system clock status. The USB event with its full timestamp. Any known clock deviation on the workstation."

Henry raised his head slightly.

"The USB time may become important."

"It already is."

"I mean the order."

Daniels looked at him.

Henry continued carefully.

"If the device was connected after the login and the remote event followed that, we would have three events within less than a minute."

"Login. USB. Radio access."

"Yes."

"That does not connect them."

"No."

"It does not mean the workstation transmitted anything."

"No."

"And it does not mean the USB device contained a radio."

"No."

Daniels turned to Grant.

"Include that in the request. Nobody turns proximity into causation."

Grant nodded and returned to the vestibule.

The passive receiver came online outside the service compartment. Its small display showed a wide section of the band. Weak, irregular lines rose and disappeared above the noise floor.

None meant anything yet.

"If a matching signal appears," Daniels asked, "can that tell us where it came from?"

"Not from one receiving position," the specialist said. "We may compare time and strength. Reliable direction finding would require additional positions or other data."

Again, no exact line.

Only another corridor.

Grant's voice carried from the vestibule as he transmitted the request for the original workstation records.

Daniels looked at his notebook.

Between 1:41:26 and 2:03:17 lay twenty-one minutes and fifty-one seconds.

Until that afternoon, they had filled the interval with witness statements, camera images and incomplete system evidence.

Now it contained another fixed point.

Not yet a confirmed connection to Michael's computer.

But no longer empty.

The small radio system remained powered.

The panel remained aimed toward the same unknown part of the landscape.

And the recorder remained ready to print another line.

If somebody was still on the other side.

---

Grant returned with the radio in his hand.

"The Chesapeake forensic unit is calling back over the secure line."

He passed Daniels the telephone relayed through the vehicle at the outer gate.

"Daniels."

The analyst on the other end had already worked with the forensic image of Michael's workstation.

"You requested the original time sources."

"Not the previous summary."

"I have the source records in front of me."

Daniels opened his notebook.

"Begin with the login."

"The record is from the local login database. Friday, July 31, 2026, 17:41:26 UTC on tty1."

"Converted?"

"1:41:26 PM Eastern Daylight Time."

"System time zone correct?"

"Yes."

"Clock deviation?"

"None currently identified that would matter to this sequence. We are still comparing other sources."

Daniels looked toward the recorder.

"What does the entry prove?"

The analyst paused briefly.

"That the account m.weber was used at the local console."

"Not who entered the password."

"Correct."

"Not who was seated at the machine."

"The access was physical. Identity does not come from the record alone."

Henry listened from near the doorway.

Daniels made a small mark beside the first time.

"Now the USB device."

Papers moved at the other end of the call.

"Multiple automatic events confirm a newly connected USB device, followed by USB mass storage and initialization of the related storage controller."

"Exact time?"

"Shortly after the local login."

"I already knew shortly after."

"The original sources do not all present the time in the same format. Some are local. Some were normalized to UTC during examination. We are reconciling sequence numbers and timestamps before giving you one second as final."

Daniels looked at Henry.

Henry said nothing.

"Can you confirm the order?"

"Login first. USB connection second."

"Before 1:47 PM?"

"Clearly."

"Before 1:42:18?"

The answer did not come immediately.

"That depends on the final normalization of the USB events and on the reliability of the recorder clock."

Daniels looked at the hidden second line.

**TIME   13:42:18   SEQ 00417**

"Then do not decide it yet."

"I will not."

"Send the source excerpts. Include origin, time zone and checksums."

"You will receive them."

Daniels wrote below the login:

**SHORTLY AFTER — UNKNOWN USB DEVICE**

No seconds.

No arrow toward C.M.T.A.

"Individual serial number?" he asked.

"Present and preserved. It does not appear in the workstation's earlier device history."

"Connected again after Friday?"

"No."

"The same as the nearly unused flash drive in Michael's drawer?"

"No. Different devices."

"Identified elsewhere at Chesapeake?"

"The broader search is still in progress. No confirmed match yet."

Daniels looked toward the radio bridge.

"And nothing in the workstation image identifies the connected object as a transmitter."

"Not at this point. The system definitely recognized at least a USB mass-storage interface."

Daniels waited a beat.

"Now explain that so someone who works in WordPad and considers Solitaire demanding software can understand it."

Grant lowered his eyes, but not quickly enough to hide the beginning of a smile.

Henry cleared his throat.

"The computer saw something that behaved like a storage device."

"Good."

"That does not prove storage was its only function."

Daniels looked at him.

"A USB stick with a second job."

"Technically, a multifunction device or composite device. Possibly a dongle."

"My version was clearer."

The analyst spoke through the telephone.

"One USB device can expose more than one interface. Storage, serial communication, networking or manufacturer-specific functions. We are checking whether the machine registered anything besides the mass-storage interface."

Daniels looked toward the service compartment.

"If there was a serial interface?"

Henry answered carefully.

"Then software on the workstation could have communicated with it locally."

"Could it have transmitted something?"

"If the device itself contained radio hardware, or if it controlled something connected elsewhere."

"LoRa?"

"Possible."

Henry added immediately:

"Possible is not a finding."

Daniels held the pen above the page.

"Then what do we know?"

"Something was connected. The computer treated at least part of it as storage. We do not yet know whether it stored, received, transmitted or merely provided another interface."

Daniels wrote one word beside the USB entry.

**FUNCTIONS?**

"No confirmed connection to C.M.T.A.," he said into the telephone.

"Understood."

"Now the janitor's statement," Daniels said. "Approximately 1:47 PM. Michael walked toward the old archive and backup room. He was not seen entering it. The corridor was not continuously observed."

He wrote:

**ABOUT 1:47 PM — TOWARD ARCHIVE AREA**

Then the final time.

**2:03:17 PM — YUKON LEAVES REAR ACCESS ROAD**

"Camera clock?" he asked.

"Already compared with the other traffic sources. 2:03:17 PM remains the working time. No uncertainty that changes the order."

Daniels thanked the analyst and ended the call.

He studied the entries.

The first came from Michael's workstation.

The second came from the same machine, though not yet with an approved second.

The third came from the paper recorder many miles away.

The fourth came from a human witness.

The fifth came from a camera.

"Five entries," Grant said.

"Four independent types of source," Daniels replied. "Workstation, recorder, witness and camera."

The radio specialist stepped out of the service compartment.

"And none automatically shares the same clock."

Daniels nodded.

"That is why we compare them."

He placed a small **L** beside the login.

A **U** beside the USB event.

An **R** beside the recorder.

A **W** beside the witness.

A **C** beside the camera.

He did not draw a line between any of them.

Instead, he wrote across the top of the page:

**1:41:26 PM — 2:03:17 PM**

Then beneath it:

**What happened in the time between?**

They did not leave the site immediately.

The paper remained visible beneath the clear protective cover. The passive receiver continued to record the noise floor. A controlled access point was established outside the concrete building.

Mercer joined Daniels near the door.

"Before you leave, I want to complete another perimeter assessment of the remaining structures."

Daniels looked toward the Operations Building.

"Nobody enters an unsafe building."

"That was not my proposal."

Grant collected another flashlight from the vehicle. Henry stayed with them while Mercer led the route.

They began at the low concrete building and moved back across areas already assessed. Mercer marked and photographed loose concrete, washed-out ground and overgrown service openings.

The Operations Building appeared less stable from close range than it had from the main clearing.

Part of the roof edge had lowered. A crack ran from an upper window frame toward the foundation. Through two broken windows, Mercer could see rooms with hanging ceiling material and debris across the floor.

He illuminated the interior from outside.

Overturned furniture.

Empty racks.

Dust.

Plant material carried in through the broken glass.

No light.

No movement.

No visible sign of recent occupation.

"No farther," Mercer said.

Daniels photographed the openings and the areas that could not be seen from outside.

"A complete search requires structural clearance."

"Or a technical rescue team," Mercer said. "Nobody enters today."

They continued along the rear wall.

A side door hung crooked in its frame, but the floor beyond it had partially collapsed. Mercer approached only far enough to document the condition.

What was visible was empty.

What was not visible was too dangerous to enter without preparation.

They remained farther from the Equipment Shelter.

One corner had settled. Sections of the outer covering bowed away from the wall. The damaged roof made even a close approach unnecessary.

Mercer observed it through binoculars.

"Still excluded."

Daniels recorded the decision.

Nobody pulled at a door.

Nobody climbed through an opening.

Nobody stood beneath the damaged roof.

The tower remained untouched. Mercer checked only from a safe distance for any visible change in the base or guying since their arrival.

None was apparent.

A small open shelter contained corroded brackets and sections of old conduit. Another low room could be viewed entirely from outside and was empty.

Between the structures, they found no fresh tire impressions and nothing that could be identified as Michael's.

No phone.

No bag.

No clothing.

No indication the Yukon had stopped there.

When they returned to the low concrete building, nearly an hour had passed.

The site had given them an accepted access event.

It had not given them the person they were searching for.

Mercer looked at Daniels.

"Everything we could safely enter or fully inspect from outside has been searched."

"And the rest?"

"Documented and restricted. Another team and different equipment will be required."

Daniels looked back toward the Operations Building.

Michael could not be declared present merely because his old identifier had reached the site.

But they could no longer treat the property as finished.

"Grant stays," Daniels said. "Access control, radio monitoring and handoff to the night post."

Grant nodded.

"Mercer?"

"I remain until the fixed post is established."

Henry looked toward Daniels' vehicle.

"And me?"

"You come with me."

Daniels unlocked the passenger side.

"Sarah and Emma hear this from us before they hear it through anybody else."

Henry remained still for a moment.

"Should I be there?"

"You can explain what the system does not prove."

Daniels looked at him.

"And they know you."

Henry looked back once at the open door of the concrete building.

A system from Michael's technical past continued to wait inside.

Then he got into the car.

Sarah opened the door before Daniels could knock a second time.

She looked first at him and then at Henry standing behind him.

"You found something."

It was not a question.

Daniels removed his hat.

"We found new information."

Emma sat at the dining table. Her ThinkPad rested in front of her with the lid partly open, but the screen was dark. Byte moved quietly in his cage at the other end of the room.

When Emma saw Daniels, she sat straighter.

"Is he there?"

"No."

The word was gentle.

It still made the room smaller.

Sarah pulled out a chair but did not sit.

"What did you find?"

Daniels placed no photographs on the table.

"There is a small radio system on the property. It was added later than most of the equipment, has independent power and was still operating."

Sarah waited.

"It recorded a remote-access event on Friday, July 31. The event used the identifier CM-047."

Her expression changed.

"Michael's identifier."

"An identifier assigned to Michael in the past."

"That is the same thing."

"Not completely."

Sarah sat down.

"Then explain the difference."

Daniels took the chair across from her. Henry sat beside him.

"The system accepted a transaction under CM-047. It could not determine which human being initiated it."

"You are saying it could have been Michael."

"Yes."

"Or somebody else."

"Yes."

"Using his access."

Henry answered.

"We do not yet know what the system required. CM-047 may only have been the visible name. A particular device, stored configuration or another technical value may have been involved."

"But somebody had whatever it needed."

"Probably."

Emma's fingers rested on the edge of the ThinkPad.

"Did it recognize him?"

Daniels turned toward her.

"No. Not as a person."

"It only recognized that something was right."

"Something about the event passed its check."

Emma looked at the dark screen.

"Then it recognized his identifier."

"Yes."

"But it did not recognize him."

Sarah looked at her daughter.

Emma did not continue.

Daniels opened his notebook but kept it turned toward himself.

"We also found a time associated with the event."

"When?" Sarah asked.

"1:42:18 PM. The recorder's clock still has to be verified, but the event appears to have occurred shortly after the local login at Michael's workstation and well before the Yukon left."

"How shortly?"

"Fifty-two seconds according to the two records."

Emma looked up.

"And the unknown USB stick?"

"The unknown device was also connected shortly after the login."

"Before the signal?"

"We cannot confirm that yet."

"But all of it was close together."

"Very close in time."

Sarah stood and moved away from the table.

"Then he was still there."

"We consider it very likely Michael was still at Chesapeake when the event occurred."

"Because the janitor saw him later."

"At approximately 1:47 PM."

"Then he was at his desk when this happened."

"We cannot say that."

Sarah turned.

"His account is used. An unknown device is connected to his computer. His old identifier appears at that place. Five minutes later he walks toward the archive room."

"That sequence is possible."

"Not until evidence connects the points."

"Would he have noticed?"

"We do not know."

"Yes."

"What did the system do after it accepted the access?"

"It could have turned something on."

"Possible."

"Or accepted a message."

"Possible."

"Or tried to use something that no longer worked."

"Exactly."

Emma looked down.

"That is a lot of possible."

"Yes."

"And not much certain."

Daniels closed the notebook.

"What is certain is that a technical event under Michael's former identifier was accepted on Friday, before the Yukon left Chesapeake."

Sarah remained beside the window.

"We thought the Yukon was the beginning."

"So did we."

"Yes."

"Then we look for something that was never part of the current systems."

"I will contact you as soon as we know more."

"He was probably still there," she said.

Sarah placed both hands on her shoulders.

"Yes."

"And something had already started."

Sarah closed her eyes for a moment.

"Maybe."

Emma looked at the dark laptop.

The system at the abandoned site had recognized Michael's identifier.

It had not known whether Michael stood behind it.

And neither did they.

Daniels drove Henry back to Chesapeake Signal Tech.

The parking lot was fuller than it had been on Day Zero but quieter. Two forensic vehicles stood near the entrance.

Michael's car remained in the same space.

Unchanged between the lines, as though its owner had left for only a few minutes.

Henry looked toward it while getting out.

Daniels noticed and said nothing.

Inside, Brenda led them to a small ground-floor meeting room. The forensic team had restricted Michael's office again after Daniels requested the original workstation records.

Several building plans lay across the table.

They were not one consistent set.

One showed the building shortly after construction. Another contained modifications from the early 2000s. A third documented newer network runs, electrical circuits and climate zones while older systems remained as pale background lines.

Henry pulled out a chair.

"What are we looking for exactly?"

"A route our current logs could not see."

"That does not narrow it much."

"Start at Michael's workstation."

Henry found the technical office area on a newer plan. Network ports, power, telephone and routes to the server rooms were clearly marked.

"This is current. Ethernet. Building phones. Normal electrical service."

Daniels indicated a lighter line beneath the modern wiring.

"And that?"

The line ran from the wall behind the technical workstations into a service shaft. From there it continued downward and disappeared beneath later crosshatching in the basement.

"Old telephone wiring," Henry said.

"Where does it go?"

Henry pulled the oldest drawing closer.

Multiple copper pairs ran from the original offices to a distribution point below. Some ended at telephone and fax outlets.

Others were only numbered.

One bundle continued toward the old archive and backup area.

Daniels looked up.

"The direction Michael walked at about 1:47."

"The direction," Henry corrected.

"The direction."

Henry followed the lines with one finger.

"This predates me."

"Purpose?"

"I do not know."

"You have worked here for decades."

"That does not mean I know every cable installed before I arrived."

"Was it active?"

"Not to my knowledge."

"That was not the question."

Henry leaned back.

"The old telephone system was replaced. Modems were removed. Leased lines were canceled. Anything no longer needed stayed in the shafts or was disconnected at the frames."

"Disconnected or removed?"

"Usually disconnected. Nobody opens walls to remove miles of copper that are not causing a problem."

Daniels looked at the plan.

"And if something remained connected at the other end?"

"That would not make it functional by itself."

"But possible."

"Theoretically."

"Could the unknown USB device reach such a line from Michael's workstation?"

"If it had a serial interface and a matching connection still existed nearby."

"Without the network."

"Yes."

"Without a server."

"Yes."

"Without an ordinary outgoing-connection log."

Henry looked toward the door.

"Yes."

He pulled another drawing from the pile. It showed the basement before a later renovation.

Behind the archive area, a narrow service section appeared where newer plans showed only a utility void. A row of small boxes carried handwritten numbers.

Beside them:

**LOW-VOLTAGE DISTRIBUTION**

Below that, another notation had faded through repeated copying.

Henry leaned closer.

"Could say remote. Or route."

"Which would you prefer?"

"Neither."

Footsteps passed the open door.

Then stopped.

The janitor stepped backward and looked into the room. He wore the same gray work clothes Daniels remembered from the first interviews. A bucket of tools hung from one hand.

"If you're going down to those old boxes," he said, "be careful."

Daniels raised his head.

"Why?"

The janitor pointed at the plan.

"Some of them still have a little power on them."

Henry looked at him.

"What?"

"Not much."

"Those lines are abandoned."

"The telephones might be."

Daniels pulled out the empty chair.

"Please come in."

The janitor set down the bucket.

"When did you measure voltage there?" Daniels asked.

"I didn't measure anything."

"Then how do you know?"

The man moved the fingers of one hand.

"It tingled."

Henry stared at him.

"Where?"

"One of the old connection boxes downstairs."

Daniels pushed the plan toward him.

"Show me."

The janitor placed one finger on the narrow service section behind the old archive room.

"About here."

"What were you doing?"

"Repairing the wall a few months ago. The plaster beside the box was loose."

"You worked on the wiring?"

"No. I knocked off the loose material and patched it."

"And then?"

"The edge of my putty knife touched one of the old contacts."

"A metal putty knife?" Henry asked.

"What else would I use?"

"And it shocked you?"

"Not a real shock. Just a small bite in my fingers. Like an old telephone line. I pulled the knife away and worked around the box."

Daniels looked at the marked position.

"Did you report it?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"It was barely anything. I figured an old adapter or battery was still connected somewhere."

Henry looked at him.

"That section should have been without power for years."

"Then nobody told the box."

For a moment, nobody spoke.

Daniels pulled the oldest plan toward him.

"Which box exactly?"

The janitor tapped the drawing again.

Not the central telephone frame.

Not the modern equipment room.

A smaller section immediately behind the archive and backup area.

"The rear one. Where the lines go upward."

Daniels looked at Henry.

Henry stared at the copper routes leading down from the technical offices.

"You said the equipment was no longer active," Daniels said.

"That is what I believed."

The janitor picked up his bucket.

At the door, he stopped.

"One more thing. The box clicked that day."

Daniels looked at him.

"What kind of click?"

The janitor set the bucket down again.

"A relay, maybe. A dry little clack."

"While you were working there?"

"Right after the putty knife touched the contact."

"Once?"

"Once for certain. Maybe twice. A few seconds apart."

"Anything light up?"

"Not that I saw."

"A fan? A motor? Any other sound?"

"No. Then it was quiet."

Henry leaned over the plan.

"Why did you not report it?"

"Because old telephone boxes click. At least I thought they did. There is equipment down there older than half the people working here."

He picked up the bucket.

"I only mention it because you should not make the same mistake."

Then he left.

His footsteps moved away along the corridor.

Daniels waited until they could no longer hear them.

"A relay," he said.

Henry did not answer.

He pulled the oldest plan closer and followed the line from the technical work area into the service shaft. From there it descended past the former telephone distribution and continued toward the low-voltage section behind the archive room.

A route older than the current servers.

Older than the switches.

Older than the monitoring that had found nothing unusual.

"Nobody touches that equipment," Daniels said. "Not until the area is secured and evidence processing has an electrician."

Henry nodded, but his attention remained on the point where the line disappeared.

The equipment belonged to no current server.

No active switch.

No monitored network.

Perhaps that was why nobody had noticed it.

Not because it had been completely dead.

Because it had only woken for a moment.
