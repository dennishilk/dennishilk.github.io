# Chapter 17 — Without a Word

The afternoon sky over Port St. Lucie had gone the color of old steel.

Heat still shimmered above the road, even beneath the gathering clouds, and the palms along the median stood almost perfectly still. Farther west, beyond the low roofs and commercial strips, a dark line of rain hung beneath the horizon without moving any closer.

Sarah drove with one hand resting lightly on the wheel.

Emma had been talking when they left Tradition.

Not about Michael. Not directly.

She had wondered whether Grissom would still come to the glass when he heard her voice, whether the filter had made that clicking sound again, and whether the fish food in the cabinet was the regular kind or the container Michael called the emergency backup even though, according to Emma, it looked exactly the same.

Sarah had answered when answers were required.

The closer they came to Palmetto Ridge, the shorter Emma’s sentences became.

Eventually they stopped altogether.

Sarah did not ask if she was all right.

She reached for her phone at the next red light, opened the playlist labeled EMMA, and tapped the first song she knew Emma would not skip.

Billie Eilish began softly through the car speakers.

Sarah turned it up a little.

Not loud enough to demand attention.

Only loud enough that the silence no longer needed explaining.

Emma watched the houses pass through the passenger window. Her knees were angled toward the door, one hand tucked beneath her thigh. She did not sing along. She did not look at Sarah.

When Michael’s street came into view, Sarah slowed.

Emma saw the house through the windshield before they reached the driveway.

For two or three seconds, she simply stared at it.

The closed garage.

The front windows.

The short strip of lawn.

Nothing had changed.

That seemed to make it worse.

Then Emma tilted her head.

“Dad would already be shaking his head at the grass.”

Sarah glanced toward the lawn.

It did not look especially high. Their neighbor Tom had probably taken care of the small section when he did his own, just as he had once or twice before.

“Tom probably did it again,” Sarah said.

Emma kept looking out through the windshield.

“Dad would still find something wrong with it.”

Sarah smiled.

She turned into the driveway and stopped the car.

Normally Emma would have been out before Sarah had fully shifted into park. She would have reached the front door while Sarah was still gathering her keys and telling her not to pull on the handle before it was unlocked.

This time, Emma waited.

She got out when Sarah did and walked beside her, close enough that her shoulder touched Sarah’s arm once, then again. By the time they reached the porch, Emma was almost leaning against her.

Sarah said nothing about it.

She unlocked the door.

Emma let out a small breath when the cool, still air of the house reached them.

Then she pulled away and ran.

“Grissom!”

Her shoes struck the floor in quick, uneven beats as she disappeared toward the living room.

Sarah closed the door behind them and locked it.

By the time she reached the aquarium, Grissom had already come forward. He hovered near the glass as Emma crouched in front of him, both hands pressed to her knees.

“Hi,” she told him. “Yes, I know. We’re late. That wasn’t my fault.”

The angelfish turned slightly, one eye toward her.

Emma stood and leaned closer to the tank.

“Dad says you pretend you’re starving even when you’re not.”

She did not feed him yet.

First she watched the surface.

The return flow made a steady ripple beneath the light. She lowered her head toward the side of the tank and listened to the filter, then touched two fingers to the cabinet beneath it, feeling for the faint vibration.

Don’t trust the light. Check the water.

Michael had said it enough times that Emma no longer needed to repeat the rule aloud.

She checked anyway.

Water movement.

Filter sound.

Temperature display.

Then the fish.

Grissom followed her along the glass.

“You look normal,” she said. “Which is good. Not normal-normal. Fish normal.”

Sarah heard only pieces of the conversation as she crossed toward the kitchen telephone.

There were no new messages that mattered. One automated call about an insurance offer, one silent hang-up, and a recorded sales pitch that had begun speaking before the machine finished its greeting.

She deleted nothing.

Then she went outside to check the mailbox.

Most of it was ordinary.

A utility notice.

A grocery circular.

An envelope addressed to Michael from a company Sarah did not recognize.

A Pizza Hut flyer folded around the rest.

She almost dropped it into the pile without looking, then saw the large photograph of the buffet and the words beneath it.

THE LUNCH BUFFET IS BACK.

PIZZA, PASTA, BREADSTICKS & MORE.

PARTICIPATING LOCATIONS ONLY.

Sarah slid the flyer free and folded it once.

When she returned, Emma was still talking to Grissom.

“Dad says the light lies,” she was saying. “Not lies like on purpose. More like it can look right when everything else is wrong.”

Sarah set the mail on the counter.

Emma glanced over.

“He’s fine.”

“I knew you’d check properly.”

Emma opened the food container.

Sarah held up the flyer.

“I found something.”

Emma looked at the red logo, then at the buffet photograph.

Her expression changed almost immediately.

“Mmm.” She looked at it another second. “Yes. Very much.”

Sarah smiled.

“We can stop on the way to the Vances.”

“The one where you can sit inside?”

“If they’re serving it today.”

“The buffet one?”

“That is what the flyer says.”

Emma put the measured portion of food into the tank and watched Grissom rise toward it.

“Then yes.”

She pointed toward the flyer.

“Because I don’t want to choose one whole pizza.”

“I remember.”

“You say that like it’s unreasonable.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You made the face.”

Sarah folded the flyer and put it into her bag.

“What face?”

“The deciding-face.”

“I have a deciding-face?”

“Yes.”

Emma looked back at the aquarium.

“Dad has one too, but his is worse.”

For the first time since they had entered the house, Sarah laughed.

It was brief, but it was real.

They stayed long enough to confirm the filter remained steady, gathered the ordinary mail, and checked the doors before leaving. Emma walked beside Sarah on the way back to the car. She did not hang on to her this time.

The dark line of rain was still waiting in the west.

By the time they reached Pizza Hut, the first drops had begun to strike the windshield.

They were large and widely spaced, leaving dark circles on the pavement that vanished almost as quickly as they appeared.

Emma saw the buffet sign through the window and opened her door before Sarah had fully parked.

“Wait until I stop moving.”

“I did.”

“You absolutely did not.”

Emma was already halfway across the first parking space.

Sarah shut off the engine and reached for her bag.

Her phone began to ring.

Emma had reached the entrance. She pulled the door open, looked back, and saw Sarah still beside the car.

The name on the screen was DANIELS.

Sarah answered.

“Hello?”

“Sarah. It’s Officer Daniels.”

Emma waited at the door.

Sarah turned slightly away from the restaurant.

“Did something happen?”

“We’re still working through the material from Chesapeake.”

His voice was careful.

Too careful.

“We’re looking more closely now at people connected to an older project. Former employees. Earlier records.”

Sarah watched Emma through the glass.

Emma pushed the door open again, then let it close. She took one step back toward the parking lot.

“Did you find something?” Sarah asked.

“We found documents that raise questions. There’s a point I still need to verify before I can tell you anything concrete.”

“Is that good or bad?”

A pause.

“I don’t know yet.”

Sarah lowered her eyes.

Daniels continued.

“I didn’t want you to think the case had stopped moving. It hasn’t. I’ll call you again as soon as I have something solid.”

Emma started back toward the door, then turned toward Sarah once more.

Sarah saw it.

“I understand,” she said. “Thank you.”

“I’ll be in touch.”

Sarah ended the call before Emma could come back to the car.

She put the phone into her bag and raised one hand.

Emma waited for her at the entrance.

“Who was that?”

“Work.”

Sarah opened the door for her.

Emma accepted the answer without another question.

Inside, the restaurant smelled of baked dough, tomato sauce, and the sharp, familiar warmth of industrial cheese. The buffet was not large, but it did not need to be. Trays of cheese pizza, pepperoni, breadsticks, pasta, and salad sat beneath heat lamps while a teenager in a black cap replaced an almost-empty pan.

Emma approached it with complete seriousness.

Sarah took a plate and followed her.

A few minutes later, Sarah returned from the buffet and found Emma already seated in the booth.

Three slices of cheese pizza were arranged on her plate.

Sarah stopped beside the table.

“You know you can take whatever you want.”

Emma had a bite of pizza in her mouth and a thin strand of cheese stuck to one finger.

She chewed, swallowed, and held up the finger.

“The next three are pepperoni.”

Sarah laughed.

Emma did too.

No one in the small conference room was laughing.

The old personnel ledger was closed now.

Daniels had shut it after reading the line a second time. The empty RK-03 divider had been placed among the other papers, neither hidden nor left on display.

Brenda sat with her hands folded around nothing.

Henry stood beside the table.

Navarro remained near the laptop.

Daniels looked at each of them.

“Who knows?”

“Only the four of us,” Brenda said.

“Did anyone call Frank?”

No one had.

“Message him?”

Again, no.

Daniels looked toward the hallway.

“Where’s Harper?”

“I sent him home after the interview,” Navarro said. “He’s been told to remain available and not delete, reset, or alter anything on his phone or computers.”

Daniels nodded.

“Then this stays in this room for now.”

A vehicle turned into the company lot.

The sound was ordinary.

Tires over rough pavement. A brief change in engine pitch. The low thump of a door closing somewhere beyond the front wall.

Brenda rose and went to the window.

She looked down.

Frank’s car moved into his usual space.

He parked the way he always parked, straight between the lines, close enough to the curb to leave room beside him. He got out, locked the vehicle, and began walking toward the building at his normal pace.

Brenda remained at the window for another moment.

“Frank’s coming,” she said.

Then, faster:

“Frank’s coming.”

Daniels looked at Henry, then at Brenda.

“Act normal.”

No one did.

They heard the front door open.

Frank’s heavier footsteps crossed the reception area. A cabinet door opened. Then his office door, or perhaps the storage room beside it. A moment later came the click and low mechanical hum of the coffee maker.

One minute passed.

Then another.

No one found anything to say.

Frank appeared in the doorway with a mug of coffee in one hand.

He stopped.

All four of them were looking at him.

“What happened?”

Daniels let the silence stand for two or three seconds.

He watched Frank’s eyes.

The table.

The papers.

Brenda.

Henry.

Then back to Daniels.

“Frank,” Daniels said. “Please sit down.”

Frank pulled out a chair.

“What is it?” he asked as he sat.

He placed the coffee in front of him.

Daniels leaned back slightly.

“Tell me again, from the beginning, how long you’ve known Michael and which projects you worked on together.”

Frank had just lifted the mug.

He stopped with it near his mouth, then took a swallow at the wrong moment. He coughed once, sharply, and set the mug down too quickly. Coffee jumped over the rim and landed in two small drops on the table.

“From the beginning?” he asked.

Daniels did not move.

“From the beginning.”

Frank glanced at the others.

“How far back do you want me to go?”

“From the first time you met Michael.”

For a moment, Frank seemed almost relieved by the question.

He reached for a napkin and wiped the coffee from the table.

“He was younger than I expected,” he began. “Not young. Just younger than the person I had built in my head from what people had said about him.”

No one interrupted.

“He didn’t talk much at first. He listened. He had this way of waiting until everyone else had finished explaining something, then asking one question that made it obvious we had all been solving the wrong problem.”

A faint, unwilling smile touched Frank’s face.

“He could be difficult. Stubborn. If he believed a system was being built badly, he didn’t care who had approved it. He would tell you.”

Henry looked down.

“But he understood infrastructure in a way I had never seen before,” Frank continued. “Not just servers. Not just software. He saw dependencies. Small ones. The thing no one else thought mattered until six months later, when it became the one thing holding everything together.”

Daniels let him continue until Frank began drifting into another story.

Then he interrupted.

“What does CST-0017 mean to you?”

Frank’s smile disappeared.

He thought for a moment.

Then he lifted his shoulders slightly and opened both hands.

“Mmm. I’m not sure. Should it mean something?”

Daniels did not answer.

He opened the ledger to the marked page and slid it across the table.

Frank looked down.

His eyes moved to the number first.

Then to the name beside it.

He looked at Brenda.

Then Henry.

Neither helped him.

Frank exhaled.

“Okay.”

Daniels rested one hand beside the ledger.

“What does RK-03 mean?”

Frank wiped his palm once against his trousers.

“Organizational approvals. Internal acceptance. I confirmed that required responsibilities and procedures had been met.”

A line of moisture had appeared along his forehead.

“What else?” Daniels asked.

“I wasn’t part of the technical implementation.”

“I didn’t ask what you weren’t.”

Frank looked at him.

Then back at the page.

“I handled final internal acceptance. Accountability. Whether the project could be formally accepted under the conditions that had been set.”

Daniels said nothing.

Frank continued because the silence gave him no place to stop.

“There were separate responsibilities. Technical design, validation, recovery authority, internal acceptance. My role was not equivalent to Michael’s. Or Dr. Brinkmann’s.”

Still Daniels did not interrupt.

“It was a controlled recovery architecture,” Frank said. “Built around separated authorization domains, formal validation boundaries, and staged restoration conditions that prevented unilateral activation.”

The words came more quickly now.

“Operational continuity depended on independent role assurance, physical fallback, procedural redundancy, and a defined acceptance layer that—”

“Was it designed to restore the system after a complete failure?” Daniels asked.

Frank stopped.

Both hands were resting on the edge of the table.

For one second he seemed to hang from them.

“Yes.”

He sat back properly after saying it, pulling his chair a little closer and straightening his shoulders as though posture might restore some of what the answer had taken from him.

Daniels nodded once.

“Why were there four roles?”

“So no one person could control the entire recovery process.”

“Explain.”

“The technical work was separated from validation. Validation was separated from final internal acceptance. No one person could initiate, verify, and authorize the whole sequence alone.”

“You understood the sequence.”

“The structure of it.”

“Enough to sign off on it.”

“Yes.”

“Enough to know what the other roles could and could not do.”

Frank hesitated.

“Yes.”

He took a breath.

“ALPHA?” Daniels said.

Frank looked at him.

Then at the ledger.

“Michael.”

“I know who ALPHA was. What did it mean?”

Frank’s throat moved.

“It meant he was the central recovery authority. Not a title. Not the project lead in the ordinary sense. The other roles could verify, authorize, or prepare parts of the process. They could not simply replace him.”

“Could the procedure be completed without Michael?”

“Not in the intended way.”

“And in another way?”

Frank looked toward Navarro.

She did not help him either.

“The safeguards existed to prevent that.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

“No,” Frank said. “Not without bypassing the system it was designed to protect.”

Henry pushed away from the table.

The chair leg struck the floor behind him.

“Where is Michael?” he shouted.

The volume shocked everyone, including Henry.

“What did you do, Frank?”

Daniels turned immediately.

“Henry. Sit down.”

Henry stood breathing hard.

Navarro shifted one step closer but did not touch him.

Daniels kept his voice level.

“We do not know that Frank did anything to Michael.”

Henry’s jaw tightened.

“But we do know he hasn’t told us everything.”

The anger in Henry’s face did not disappear. It only went inward.

He lowered himself into the chair.

“Sorry,” he said to Daniels, back in the quiet, almost gentle voice they knew.

Daniels acknowledged it with a small nod.

Then he turned to Frank.

“When did you last think about CM-047 before Michael disappeared?”

Frank leaned back.

“That was all a very long time ago. The project was closed. The systems were decommissioned. There was no reason for anyone to—”

“Frank.”

The name stopped him.

Frank looked at his hands.

“A few weeks ago,” he said.

Navarro’s attention sharpened.

“What happened?” Daniels asked.

“Something in the company network looked wrong.”

Navarro lifted her head fully now.

“What looked wrong?”

“An old area was being touched. Something that should not have been active.”

“Which area?”

“The old CM-047 recovery path.”

Navarro leaned forward.

“From which system?”

“Not from what I saw.”

“That doesn’t answer me.”

“I couldn’t determine the source from what I saw.”

“What did you see?”

Frank shifted in his chair.

“A sequence of requests. A few attempts over a short period.”

“Automated?”

“No.”

He answered that one immediately.

“How do you know?”

“Because it wasn’t random. It wasn’t a scanner trying common paths. The requests were too specific, too slow, and too closely related.”

He looked at Navarro.

“Someone knew what they were looking for. They just didn’t know exactly where it still existed.”

“What were they looking for?”

Frank closed his eyes for a second.

“I didn’t have to guess.”

He opened them again.

“They were looking for the recovery path.”

“And Michael?”

“Michael was working in another system at the time. I could see what he was doing.”

“That proves he couldn’t have done anything else?”

“No.”

Frank swallowed.

“But I believed it was someone else.”

Navarro’s voice remained calm.

“When?”

“Around July eighteenth. Maybe a day or two before.”

“How many attempts?”

“A few. Enough that I noticed the pattern.”

“Did you preserve the logs?”

Frank’s eyes moved toward the closed papers.

“I printed them.”

Navarro waited.

“Where are the printouts now?”

Frank’s breathing changed.

He moved the coffee farther away even though he had not touched it again.

“I shredded them.”

Henry stared at him.

The look was colder than the shout had been.

Frank met his eyes once and looked away immediately.

Navarro did not react the way Frank seemed to expect.

“The shredded pages were recovered,” she said.

Frank looked up.

“They were reconstructed and scanned. Most of it appeared to be ordinary business records and routine network output.”

She reached for her phone.

“What lines are we looking for?”

Frank described the approximate date range, the old project identifier, the sequence of path requests, and the format he remembered from the printout.

Navarro asked him to repeat two of the terms.

Then she made the call.

She spoke quietly but with clipped precision, requesting an immediate targeted review of the reconstructed shred material using the identifiers Frank had provided. She asked for visual inspection, not only text search, because portions of the printouts had been split across strips and some characters might not have been read correctly.

Henry did not stop looking at Frank.

When Navarro ended the call, Daniels asked the question she had not.

“If you knew it wasn’t Michael, why didn’t you warn him?”

Frank opened his mouth.

Closed it.

“Well, I—”

He looked at Daniels.

“You have to understand—”

“Why didn’t you warn him?”

Frank’s voice dropped.

“I did.”

Daniels did not blink.

“How?”

Frank’s hands tightened together.

“His old project badge.”

Daniels interrupted.

“In his mailbox?”

Frank nodded.

Whatever remained of his defensive posture collapsed.

He looked smaller in the chair now.

“What was Michael supposed to understand from that?” Daniels asked.

“That someone was looking for CM-047.”

Frank’s voice was barely above the room’s air-conditioning.

“That the old recovery path had been touched. That the project wasn’t as dead as it was supposed to be.”

“Why not tell him directly?”

Frank looked toward the window.

Then toward Brenda.

“I was afraid.”

No one spoke.

“Of what?”

“Of whoever was searching. Of what it meant. Of what it could do to Michael. To the company.”

His eyes came back to Daniels.

“To me.”

He stopped and rubbed one hand across his forehead.

“I didn’t know who I could trust. I didn’t know how to walk into someone’s office and say that a project buried for years was moving again.”

“So you put the badge in his mailbox.”

“I knew he would recognize it.”

“And after that?”

“I waited.”

“For what?”

“For him to come to me. To say something. To show me he understood.”

“Did he?”

“I don’t know.”

Frank gave a weak, humorless breath.

“Michael was difficult to read as a person. I watched him. In the building. In the systems. I looked for any sign that he was searching the old project areas or reacting differently.”

“You monitored him.”

“I paid attention.”

“That is not the same answer.”

“No,” Frank said. “It isn’t.”

He looked down.

“I watched what I could see. I did not read his private files. I watched system activity. I watched his behavior.”

“And?”

“Nothing I could prove was connected.”

“Did you believe he understood the warning?”

“I hoped he did.”

“Were you being watched?”

Frank shook his head before Daniels finished the question.

“I don’t know.”

“Was there evidence?”

“No. Nothing concrete.”

“Then why did you think you were?”

“Because once you know someone is looking for something like that, every car outside your house feels wrong.”

His words came faster.

“You notice people in the parking lot. You wonder who stays in the hallway too long. You jump when the doorbell rings.”

He pressed both palms together.

“I felt watched. That doesn’t mean I was.”

“Was that why you destroyed the printouts?”

“Yes.”

Frank stared at the table.

“I panicked.”

He took a breath.

“It was stupid.”

“Why destroy them?”

“I thought if they were gone, no one could prove I had seen anything.”

The room went quiet.

“And after Michael disappeared?” Daniels asked. “Why did you still say nothing?”

Frank’s eyes filled, though no tears fell.

“I was more afraid.”

“Of being next?”

“Yes.”

“And?”

Frank looked at Daniels.

“And I knew what it would look like.”

“The logs. The badge. The missing information.”

“Yes.”

“You thought you would be the first suspect.”

“Yes.”

“So you kept quiet.”

“Yes.”

He looked briefly toward Steve’s empty place in the room, as though the young man might somehow still be standing there.

“For one moment, I even thought about making it look like Harper.”

Brenda’s chair moved.

“Frank!”

He flinched.

“I’m sorry.”

Daniels’ voice hardened.

“Did you do anything to direct suspicion toward Steve Harper?”

“No.”

Frank answered quickly.

“No. I did nothing.”

He looked at Brenda.

“I felt sick just thinking it.”

Daniels let that sit for one second.

Then he touched the closed ledger.

“Did you remove your RK-03 file from Brenda’s archive?”

Frank did not try to deny it.

“Yes.”

“Where is it?”

“At my house.”

“Where?”

“In the attic.”

“Is it complete?”

“Yes.”

“You intended to destroy it?”

Frank nodded.

“I was going to burn it with the yard waste. Dry grass. Branches.”

He swallowed.

“But I couldn’t.”

Daniels leaned forward.

“Is anyone at your house now?”

“No.”

“You live alone?”

“Yes.”

“We can apply for a warrant. Or you can give us voluntary, limited consent to enter your home and recover that file from the location you describe. Only the file and only that location.”

Frank nodded immediately.

“That’s fine.”

“I need you to say it clearly.”

“You have my permission to go into the house and take the file.”

“You will sign a written consent.”

“Yes.”

“I need your key.”

Frank removed a key ring from his pocket and separated one brass key from the others.

Daniels did not take it yet.

“Where exactly is the file?”

“In the attic. Rear side of the house. There’s a row of plastic storage bins beneath the roof slope. The file is in a cardboard document box behind the second bin from the left.”

“Anyone else with a key?”

“My sister has one, but she’s not there.”

Daniels took the key.

“A patrol unit will call you when they are at the house. You can confirm entry again before they go inside.”

“All right.”

Navarro prepared the written consent while Daniels made the request.

Frank signed it.

The call was placed.

Then Daniels returned to the question.

“When did you remove the file?”

“Monday, July twentieth.”

“How?”

Frank’s eyes went to Brenda.

“I asked her to make another pot of coffee.”

Brenda stared at him.

“I waited until she left the archive area. Then I took it.”

“Pff.”

The sound came from Brenda before she could stop it.

Frank looked at her.

“I know.”

Daniels continued.

“Did Michael ever speak to you about the badge?”

“No.”

“Did he try?”

Frank hesitated.

“Maybe once.”

Henry looked up.

“What happened?”

“He looked at me from across the room. Longer than normal. Like he wanted to say something.”

Frank almost smiled at the memory, but could not.

“I was about to go over to him. Then Steve came between us talking about something he’d seen on GitHub. Someone had connected a Wii Balance Board so you could control DOOM by shifting your weight.”

Navarro blinked once.

Henry looked away.

“Steve explained the whole thing,” Frank said. “Michael smiled at him. Then he went back to work.”

“And you?” Daniels asked.

“A part of me was relieved.”

Frank’s voice dropped.

“And the other part knew I might have just lost my only chance.”

“You did not create another one.”

“No.”

“When did you last see Michael alive?”

Frank did not answer.

Daniels waited.

“When did you last see him alive?”

Frank stared at the coffee he no longer wanted.

“When he got into the white car.”

No one moved.

Daniels’ voice changed.

Not louder.

Sharper.

“What white car?”

Frank looked toward the window, as though the sight still existed there.

“I was in my office. I saw Michael come out the side exit.”

“The emergency exit?”

“Yes.”

“He crossed the side of the property and went through the pedestrian gate.”

“How?”

“He held something to the RFID reader. I assumed it was his Chesapeake badge.”

“Did that system record entries?”

“No. It was local. It only knew which card IDs were authorized. If users changed, the controller had to be reprogrammed directly.”

“So there is no log.”

“No.”

“What happened next?”

“He closed the gate behind him.”

Frank’s voice was steadier now because he was describing something outside himself.

“Then he pressed the handle once to make sure it had latched.”

Henry closed his eyes briefly.

That was Michael.

“He walked toward a white vehicle outside the fence,” Frank continued. “Large. American. I couldn’t identify the model from that angle.”

“Driver?”

“I couldn’t see one.”

“License plate?”

“No.”

“Was Michael carrying anything?”

“I couldn’t tell.”

“Did he open the passenger door?”

“No.”

Frank looked at Daniels.

“It opened from the inside just before he reached it.”

Navarro wrote that down.

“Did he hesitate?”

“No.”

“Did he appear forced?”

“No.”

“Normal pace?”

“A little faster than normal.”

“How much faster?”

“I don’t know. Enough that I noticed.”

“Anything else?”

“He looked back at the building twice.”

“At your window?”

“I’m not sure.”

“Did he look directly at you?”

“No.”

“So you do not know whether he saw you.”

“No.”

“What time?”

“Shortly before two. Ten minutes either way.”

Navarro glanced at Daniels.

The camera time sat unspoken between them.

2:03:17 PM.

“Did you watch the vehicle leave?” she asked.

“Not fully. It passed out of my sight behind the fence line and the building.”

“Then what did you do?”

Frank’s face changed again.

“I went back to my desk.”

“And began shredding the logs.”

“Yes.”

“Before Henry came in.”

“Yes.”

Daniels’ eyes remained on him.

“Henry told you the archive room was open. He told you about the laptop and Michael’s Leatherman.”

Frank nodded.

“You stopped the shredder while he spoke.”

“Yes.”

“Then you turned it back on.”

Frank’s mouth opened.

No sound came.

He tried again.

“I—”

His voice failed.

The room remained still.

Frank pressed one hand against his mouth, lowered it, and tried once more.

“Because when Henry told me about the room and the Leatherman, I knew.”

His breathing became uneven.

“I knew it could be connected.”

He looked as though the next word might break him.

“And I still thought about myself first.”

Silence filled the room.

Daniels did not rescue him from it.

After several seconds, he asked:

“Did you help Michael leave the building?”

“No.”

The answer came quickly.

“No. But he knew the emergency-exit alarm was broken.”

“What alarm?”

“The green exit-alarm box under the panic bar. The door itself worked. The alarm module didn’t. It looked damaged, already tripped or mechanically broken.”

“When did Michael tell you?”

“About three days earlier.”

“And what did you do?”

“I wrote a maintenance request.”

“For the custodian?”

“Yes.”

“Was it completed?”

“No.”

“Where was the request on Friday?”

“Still on my desk.”

“So Michael knew he could open that door without triggering the alarm.”

“Yes.”

“Who else knew?”

“No one else mentioned it to me.”

“That was not my question.”

“I don’t know who else knew.”

Frank rubbed his forehead again.

“It didn’t seem important at the time. The door only opened from inside. Everyone parked in front. No one normally used that side.”

Henry rose.

Slowly this time.

He drew in a breath.

Daniels saw it before the words came.

“Brenda,” he said. “Please make another pot of coffee. Now. Take Henry with you.”

Brenda understood immediately.

She stood.

“Come on, Henry.”

Henry remained where he was for one second, looking at Frank.

Frank’s voice was barely audible.

“I’m sorry, Henry.”

Henry did not turn.

He walked out with his jaw clenched and his steps hard enough to be heard after he and Brenda had entered the hallway.

The door closed.

Frank stared at it.

Daniels and Navarro remained with him.

Navarro reviewed the timeline again.

The side exit.

The damaged alarm box.

The RFID gate.

The white vehicle.

The door opening from inside.

Frank’s estimate of the time.

She asked each question without accusation and repeated two of them in different forms. Frank’s answers did not materially change.

Several minutes later, voices became audible in the hallway.

Brenda spoke softly.

Henry answered once, still angry.

Then again, quieter.

By the time the door opened, his breathing had slowed.

Brenda carried the coffee pot.

Henry followed her.

She poured coffee for Navarro, then Daniels. At Henry’s small nod, she filled a cup for him as well.

She did not ask Frank whether he wanted any.

She placed the pot beside his mug.

Wordlessly.

Then she sat down next to Henry.

Frank looked at the pot.

He did not touch it.

Daniels folded his hands.

“Is there anything else you have not told us?”

Frank remained still for a long moment.

Then something in him gave way.

Not dramatically.

He simply stopped measuring every sentence.

“CMTA was never just a remote site,” he said.

Daniels watched him.

“What was it?”

“The physical fallback point for CM-047.”

Henry’s cup stopped halfway to his mouth.

“The annex housed the recovery hardware that was not supposed to depend on Chesapeake’s normal infrastructure. Separate communications. Local access. Independent power options. It was where the system could be reached if everything else failed.”

“The printer record we found,” Daniels said.

Frank nodded.

“CM-047.”

“And Dr. Brinkmann?”

“She handled validation. She understood the architecture at a technical level I did not. Michael trusted her judgment.”

“Did you?”

“Yes.”

Frank took a breath.

“There was another person.”

“Elias Calver.”

Frank’s eyes moved to Daniels.

“So you know.”

“We know he is missing.”

Frank nodded slowly.

“He never accepted that the project was over.”

“What does that mean?”

“After it ended, he came to me once. Soon after closure. He wanted help reviving it. He thought the work had been wasted.”

“And you refused.”

“Yes.”

“That was the last contact?”

“No.”

The room tightened again.

“He came back about a year ago.”

“How?”

“He scheduled a job interview under another name.”

Brenda turned toward him.

Frank looked at her.

“You told me my appointment had arrived.”

Brenda’s expression changed as memory found the moment.

“You came out of your office,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You saw him.”

“Yes.”

“And you didn’t say his name.”

“No.”

She looked toward Daniels.

“He didn’t even introduce them. Frank just looked at the man and took him into his office.”

“What name did Calver use?” Daniels asked.

Frank told him.

Navarro wrote it down.

“What did he want?” Daniels asked.

“He said we needed control back.”

“We?”

“I believed he meant himself and me.”

“You are not sure.”

“No.”

“What did he say?”

“That he had spent years studying the problem. That he was close to finding a way to bring CM-047 back without all four original roles.”

Henry set his coffee down.

“What did you tell him?”

“That I didn’t believe him. And that I wanted nothing more to do with it.”

“How did he react?”

“He became furious.”

“Threatening?”

“Angry. Aggressive. He accused me of abandoning the project. Of being afraid of what we had built.”

“Did he mention Michael?”

“No. Only the project.”

“And after that?”

“I never heard from him again.”

“Did you tell Michael?”

“Yes.”

Frank looked toward Henry.

“It was shortly after. I had stayed late on a backup day. Michael was alone in the server room.”

“What did you tell him?”

“Everything Calver had said.”

“And Michael’s response?”

Frank remembered it exactly.

“It was an interesting project,” he said. “But it’s over.”

The room stayed quiet.

“He said one person could not rebuild it alone. He was completely certain.”

Frank looked at Daniels.

“He said it was technically impossible.”

Daniels’ service phone vibrated against the table.

The sound was small.

Every person in the room heard it.

Daniels glanced at the screen.

The phone nearly slipped from his hand.

He caught it against the edge of the table and stood.

Then he called the number displayed in the message.

“Daniels.”

He listened.

“Okay.”

His eyes went to Navarro.

“How?”

A longer pause.

“Where?”

He turned slightly away from the table.

“Okay.”

Another silence.

“Yes. Keep the scene locked down. I’m on my way.”

He ended the call.

No one asked.

They were all staring at him.

Daniels remained standing with the phone in his hand.

“They found Calver.”

For one brief second, the words sounded like progress.

Then Daniels continued.

“At his parents’ house in Fort Myers. His parents are in Hazlehurst, Georgia, visiting a relative.”

The faces around the table changed.

Brenda’s hand rose toward her mouth.

Henry went completely still.

Frank stared at Daniels as though he had not understood the sentence.

Daniels looked at Navarro.

“Calver called 911 himself. It appears he did not want his parents to find him when they came home.”

No one moved.

Every face at the table was shocked now.

Daniels lowered the phone.

“He was found dead in the garage. At the moment, it appears to be suicide.”
