# Chapter 21 — No Wake

The map covered almost the entire table.

It was not one map, exactly. It was a printed road overview laid over two county waterway sheets, several satellite images, and a series of photographs taken at the ramp where the Yukon had been found. Clear plastic sleeves kept the pages from sliding apart, but the edges still curled where too many hands had moved them.

Daniels stood on one side of the table. Navarro stood on the other.

The local officer who had coordinated the Yukon scene leaned over the northern edge of the map with a capped marker in his hand.

The room belonged to the small local station. It contained four chairs, a wall-mounted television, a coffee machine that sounded as though it needed repair, and a narrow window overlooking the rear parking lot. Nothing about it suggested a command center. That was useful. No one in the room was pretending that a single map had turned the water into a manageable search area.

The officer placed the marker on the Yukon ramp.

Then he began marking other public launches.

One.

Two.

Three.

A fourth farther along the corridor.

Then two more on the opposite side of a wider branch.

Navarro watched the marks accumulate.

“How many of these are there?”

“Depends how strict you want to be about what counts as a ramp.”

“A place where someone could put in or take out a small boat.”

“In that case?” The officer looked back at the map. “A lot.”

He added another mark.

Several were larger public facilities with parking, restrooms, or more than one concrete lane. Others were little more than a sloped slab, a short dock, and enough space for tow vehicles to turn around. Some had signs. Some had faded signs. A few appeared on county maps under one name and on local fishing pages under another.

The Yukon ramp was not isolated.

It was one ordinary opening among many.

Daniels turned one of the scene photographs toward himself.

The image showed the short dock and calm water beyond it. Near the end of the dock, a white regulatory sign faced the channel.

```text
IDLE SPEED
NO WAKE
```

The words had been there while the Yukon sat in the parking space.

No warning light.

No gate.

No record of who passed beneath them.

Daniels looked at the marks again.

“So if someone used the water, the first problem is not finding an exit.”

The officer shook his head.

“The problem is deciding which one mattered.”

Navarro traced the broad route away from the Yukon ramp without touching the paper.

“This goes to the lake?”

“Eventually.”

“And these?”

“Some reconnect. Some narrow. Some bring you back toward roads, drainage works, other launches. A few are not worth much in dry months. A shallow boat can use more of them than a deep one.”

“Does anyone track what launches where?” Daniels asked.

“Not at places like this.”

“FWC?”

“They patrol. Deputies respond when someone calls. Fire Rescue goes out when somebody needs help. But putting a fishing boat in the water isn’t a check-in.”

Daniels nodded once.

“Anything known on the water overnight?”

The officer understood that Daniels was not asking only about one agency or one log.

“Nothing I’ve heard about.”

“Can you have it checked generally?”

“Yes.”

“No special request. Just anything unusual in the area. Calls, contacts, stranded boats, complaints.”

“I’ll take care of it.”

The officer left the room with his phone.

Daniels and Navarro continued studying the map.

The water did not form a road in the way a road appeared on a screen. It widened, narrowed, split, rejoined, disappeared behind names, and crossed boundaries that meant something to property records but nothing to a boat already afloat.

Navarro pointed toward the first group of public ramps.

“Even if the Yukon arrived at three twenty-four—”

“We do not know that it did.”

“Even if it arrived after the phone disappeared from the network, we still do not know how long anyone remained there.”

“No.”

“A boat could already have been waiting.”

“Yes.”

“Or the driver could have left in another vehicle.”

“Yes.”

“Or Michael could have been out of the Yukon before it ever reached the ramp.”

Daniels looked at her.

“Yes.”

The map gave them options.

It did not choose among them.

The local officer returned approximately fifteen minutes later carrying a yellow note page.

“Nothing overnight.”

Daniels waited.

“There was one contact earlier in the evening. Local man came back from fishing and had probably had one drink more than he needed. He was already on land. Cooperative. Known around here. No arrest, no medical call, no boat problem. He was gone well before the time you care about.”

“Did he report seeing anything?”

“No.”

“Anything after that?”

“No distress calls. No unusual lights reported. No floating or abandoned boat. No nighttime FWC contact in this section. No deputy call to the water.”

“Nothing reported,” Daniels said.

“That is all it means.”

“What was the weather like?”

“Nothing unusual. No storm. No hard rain. Fairly calm in the canals and close to shore.”

The officer moved one finger toward the open water.

“Farther out would have been rougher. There was enough wind across the lake to build some chop. Not dangerous by itself. Less comfortable in something small.”

“Enough to keep people off?”

“Some people.”

“Not everyone.”

“Not fishermen who know the lake.”

Navarro looked from the broad open-water area to the clustered public ramps.

“So the weather does not give us a route.”

“No,” the officer said. “It only tells you which route would have been easier.”

Daniels thanked him.

Then he looked at the map again.

“Would it be possible to get a boat out here?”

The officer glanced toward the photographs.

“For a search?”

“For orientation. We stay on public water. No private docks. No private cuts. We need to see the approaches from the water instead of guessing from paper.”

The officer considered it.

“The nearby Fire Rescue station has a shallow boat. Flat enough for the side water. I can ask.”

Daniels looked at Navarro.

“You good with that?”

“As long as nobody expects me to identify tire tracks underwater.”

The officer smiled.

“I’ll make the call.”

***

The Fire Rescue boat arrived without lights, sirens, or a second vehicle following behind it.

It was a low, flat aluminum boat on a simple trailer, built for flooded roads, shallow canals, and places where a deeper rescue craft would become part of the emergency. An outboard sat at the stern. Life jackets, a throw bag, a small anchor, and ordinary rescue equipment were secured along the sides.

The man driving the tow vehicle backed it toward the water with the easy concentration of someone who had done so many times.

The local officer raised one hand as the driver stepped out.

“Morning, Madsona.”

“Morning.”

“Didn’t expect to see you until bowling.”

Evan Madsona looked at him over the roof of the vehicle.

“You still owe me for the last game.”

“I owe you nothing. The lane was bad.”

“The lane was straight.”

“That was the problem.”

Madsona laughed once and came around the vehicle.

He was introduced to Daniels and Navarro without ceremony. The local officer gave him only what he needed.

“The white Yukon was found at this ramp. They’re considering whether somebody might have gone from road to water. They need to see the public approaches. That’s all.”

Madsona nodded.

“Public water only?”

“Yes.”

“No private docks, no private canals, no going ashore anywhere that isn’t clearly public or accessible.”

“Understood.”

He looked at Daniels.

“Anything specific you want me to find?”

“Not yet. We need to understand what someone could have done.”

Madsona launched the boat and tied it to the short dock while the tow vehicle was moved into a marked space. He gave them life jackets, showed them where to step, and waited until both were seated before releasing the line.

He did not head immediately away from the ramp.

Instead, he kept the outboard barely above idle and moved along the edge of the concrete slab.

The water there was shallow enough to show changes in color beneath the surface. Sand faded into darker mud. Thin weeds bent with the movement of the boat. Sunlight flashed from small pieces of shell and stone.

Madsona leaned slightly to look toward the submerged edge.

“What are we checking?” Navarro asked.

“A trailer.”

Daniels turned toward him.

“Underwater?”

“If somebody wanted one gone fast, they might back it off the edge or push it sideways. In water this shallow, part of it would usually show. A tire. Fender. Straight metal where nothing straight belongs.”

He moved slowly along the adjacent shoreline.

They saw no wheel.

No metal frame.

No disturbed line of reeds.

Nothing rested beneath the surface except natural debris and the shadow of the boat.

Madsona turned them back toward the channel.

“Doesn’t mean there wasn’t a trailer,” he said. “Only means one isn’t lying right here where we can see it.”

Daniels looked once more at the parking area.

From the water, the Yukon space was only one long painted rectangle among others.

Then Madsona eased the throttle forward.

The boat moved away from the dock.

Within minutes, the ramp began to disappear.

The water curved behind reeds and low vegetation. A protected indentation opened along one side, calm enough that the surface held a broken reflection of the sky.

Madsona reduced speed again.

“From the parking lot, you’d lose sight of a boat about here.”

Daniels looked back.

The dock was gone.

Not distant.

Gone.

A person standing beside the Yukon could have watched a boat leave and lost it before learning which way it continued.

The protected corner contained nothing remarkable. No structure. No hidden vessel. No sign that anyone had waited there. It was simply an ordinary place where the shape of the water broke the line of sight.

Madsona moved on.

The next public ramp appeared much sooner than the map had made it feel.

It was smaller than the facility where they had begun. The concrete slope was visible between low banks, and one narrow parking area ran beside it. An angler stood near the edge with a bucket and a rod.

When he saw the Fire Rescue boat, he raised one hand.

Madsona raised his in return.

“You know him?” Navarro asked.

“Know of him. Lives a few streets over.”

They did not stop.

Nothing at the ramp called for attention. No abandoned vehicle. No empty trailer. No damaged dock. The angler returned to his line before they passed.

Daniels watched the access road through the trees.

“How long from the first parking lot to this one by road?”

Madsona considered it.

“Fifteen minutes. Maybe twenty. Depends which way you go and whether you know the smaller roads.”

“And by boat?”

“At this speed? Less than ten.”

Navarro looked back along the water.

“Faster if someone pushed it?”

“Yes. But there’d be no reason to run hard here at night unless they wanted attention.”

“So the water shortens the transfer,” Daniels said.

“If there was a transfer,” Navarro replied.

“Yes.”

Madsona continued past the ramp.

Farther on, the route divided.

He pointed forward.

“Stay on that line and you reach the open lake.”

Then he indicated the other direction.

“That way starts branching. Wider public channels first. Narrower water after that. Some of it comes back toward roads and other ramps. Some does not help unless your boat sits shallow.”

“How easy is it to lose someone?” Daniels asked.

“If you don’t know which turn they took?” Madsona looked into the branches. “Very.”

They entered one of the larger channels.

For the next forty minutes, Madsona showed them what the map could not.

A branch that appeared substantial on paper narrowed between vegetation until a deeper boat would have turned back.

Another widened unexpectedly and offered three possible directions.

A public maintenance crossing looked close enough to reach from the water but provided no place to remove a boat.

A distant dock belonged to a private property and remained only a shape across the channel. Madsona kept well away from it.

They saw nothing unusual.

No abandoned bag.

No damaged boat.

No sign scratched into a post.

No route that announced itself as the correct one.

The water remained useful precisely because nothing about it needed to be special.

Eventually Madsona turned toward the open lake.

The change began before the channel fully ended.

The protected surface lost its smoothness. Short waves reached beneath the hull from an angle. The flat-bottomed boat, steady in the channels, began to lift and slap against the water.

Madsona lowered the speed.

The aluminum floor vibrated under Daniels’ shoes.

Navarro placed one hand against the side rail.

“This was the calm night?” she asked.

“This is calm enough,” Madsona said. “Wind has room to work out here.”

They moved only a short distance into the broader water.

The lake did not appear empty.

A small motorboat crossed far ahead of them. Another sat nearly still, one man visible inside with his attention on a fishing line. Farther away, something white rose and fell between the wave tops before resolving into another boat.

None of the people looked toward them for more than a moment.

Madsona kept the bow at an angle that softened the impact.

“In the protected water, almost anybody can move around if they stay slow,” he said. “Out here, you should know how your boat behaves.”

“Could a small boat cross at night?” Daniels asked.

“Yes.”

“Comfortably?”

“Depends on the boat. Depends on the driver. Depends how far.”

Navarro looked back toward the channels.

“The easier route stays near protection.”

“Usually.”

“Not proof of what happened.”

“No.”

Madsona took them far enough for the difference to become unmistakable.

Then he turned back.

The waves changed from impact to pressure at the stern, and the boat settled again as the channel walls drew closer.

Daniels waited until the ride had become quiet.

“Anything built out here?”

Madsona looked toward the shoreline.

“Built how?”

“Small buildings. Maintenance sheds. Pump structures. Anything reachable from public water.”

Madsona thought for several seconds.

“Closest thing that comes to mind is a small pump station.”

“Can we see it without entering private property?”

“Yes.”

“Take us there.”

The pump station sat beside a narrow controlled channel behind a low concrete edge.

It was not a station in the sense of occupied rooms or permanent staff. It was a block-built structure with a weathered roof, one locked metal door, and a dirty window above a pump housing. A short service platform met the water. Behind a protective grate, a large screw-shaped mechanism disappeared into shadow.

Madsona brought the boat carefully against the concrete.

“This is public service access,” he said. “Door stays closed unless you have authorization.”

Daniels stepped out alone.

He remained on the open platform.

The door was secured and showed no visible damage. Dust and old water marks covered the lower wall. Through the window he could see a control cabinet, part of the pump mechanism, a hanging work light, and open floor.

No bedding.

No clothing.

No food containers.

No person.

He checked the accessible side of the building and the area immediately around the platform. Nothing appeared recently forced or abandoned.

He returned to the boat.

“Anything?” Navarro asked.

“A pump station.”

Madsona pushed them away from the concrete.

They continued back toward the original ramp.

Approximately ten minutes later, an aluminum fishing boat approached from the opposite direction with two men inside. Madsona knew both from his town. He slowed and exchanged greetings across the water.

One of the men looked at Daniels and Navarro.

“You giving tours now, Evan?”

“Only to people who ask better questions than you.”

The men laughed.

Daniels identified himself and kept the question broad.

“Did either of you hear about anything unusual on the water late Friday night or early Saturday morning? Boat trouble, people calling for help, anything left where it should not have been?”

Both men considered it.

Neither had been on the water during the relevant hours.

Neither had heard of an accident, nighttime recovery, or unusual boat.

Among the regular fishermen, no story had circulated about anything exceptional.

One of the men rested an arm on the side of his boat.

“A small boat came through quiet and the driver knew the water, nobody had to notice.”

The other nodded.

“And if nobody had a reason to remember it, they wouldn’t.”

Daniels took their names and contact details without turning the exchange into a full interview between moving boats.

Madsona thanked them.

The two boats separated.

When the original ramp came back into view, the parking area looked almost exactly as it had before they left. The Yukon was no longer there, but the long space where it had stood remained empty.

Madsona tied up at the short dock.

Navarro stepped onto the concrete and removed her life jacket.

“Now we know how it could have happened.”

Daniels stepped out behind her.

“We still don’t know that it did.”

The local officer was walking toward them before Madsona had finished securing the boat.

He held his department phone in one hand.

“We just got another unattended-property call.”

Daniels looked at him.

“Vehicle?”

“Boat.”

The officer glanced toward the water behind them.

“Six or seven public accesses down. Small aluminum boat tied at the edge. Empty trailer in the lot. No tow vehicle.”

Navarro stopped folding the life jacket.

“How long?”

“Caller says at least since yesterday. Maybe longer.”

“Registration?” Daniels asked.

“Not much of one.”

The officer looked at the note on his phone.

“They can read `FL 37`. Rest looks scraped off.”

Daniels turned toward Madsona.

The firefighter had heard enough.

“I’ll leave the boat in the water.”

***

The second ramp was farther by road than it had appeared from the water.

By the time they arrived, one patrol unit had restricted the immediate parking area and another officer was keeping the reporting fisherman away from the boat.

The aluminum hull floated beside the outer edge of the short dock.

A common line held it in place.

No one had attempted to start it or pull it ashore.

The registration on the forward side had been scraped hard enough to leave a pale irregular patch against the older finish.

Only four characters remained reliably visible.

```text
FL 37
```

Everything after them had been cut, peeled, and abraded into meaningless fragments.

The trailer was not in a marked space.

It stood near the edge of the lot where tall grass and brush pushed against the pavement. Its tongue had been rolled between vegetation and a low cluster of branches. The frame remained visible if someone looked directly toward it, but a driver entering only to launch a boat might not notice it immediately.

The plate bracket was empty.

Between the trailer and the water, the ramp told a different story.

Dark rubber marks crossed the concrete in uneven bands. They began lower down near the wet portion and continued upward in smeared arcs. At the transition to sand and gravel, the surface had been disturbed. Two areas showed where powered wheels had spun without clean forward movement.

The marks did not preserve a readable tread.

They preserved effort.

Madsona crouched at the edge of the restricted area without touching the surface.

“Rear wheels were doing the work.”

“Two-wheel drive?” Daniels asked.

“Maybe. Or a four-wheel-drive truck left in two-high.”

“What class of vehicle?”

Madsona looked at the width and the space between the marks.

“Could be a half-ton pickup. F-150. Silverado. Could be a big SUV. Expedition. Tahoe. Something in that class.”

“Can you narrow it down?”

“Not from this.”

A crime-scene technician photographed the ramp from above and from both sides.

“Recent enough to matter,” she said. “Too damaged to identify the tire.”

“How recent?” Navarro asked.

“Compatible with the period you’re asking about. I cannot give you an hour. The spinning destroyed most of the pattern, and the vehicle moved laterally.”

Madsona followed the broad swing of the marks.

“They lost traction. Corrected. Tried again.”

The trailer’s winch sat above the tongue with its cable wound heavily toward one side of the drum.

Madsona looked at it without touching.

“That would explain part of it.”

“The winch?” Daniels asked.

“Cable stacked wrong. If it bound under load, they may have backed farther into the water to float the boat onto the trailer instead of pulling it.”

Navarro looked down the slope.

“Putting the drive wheels on the slick section.”

“Yes.”

“And when they couldn’t climb out?”

“They could back down again, float the boat off, and pull the trailer out empty.”

Daniels looked at the hidden trailer and then at the boat still tied to the dock.

“Somebody intended to take the boat out here.”

Madsona stood.

“That is what it looks like.”

At the lot exit, Navarro noticed another disturbance.

The right corner of the grass showed two roughly parallel impressions. One passed over the edge where a front tire could have cut across. The second sat farther back and pressed deeper into the softer ground.

Daniels photographed the entire exit first.

Then he asked Madsona, “Have anything about this long?”

Madsona reached into a pocket and produced a pack of cigarettes.

Daniels placed it on clean asphalt beside the impressions, never inside them, and took several closer photographs.

Madsona watched him.

“First useful thing they’ve done all week.”

Daniels gave him a brief look.

The distance between the impressions and their arc were compatible with a longer SUV or pickup turning right too tightly. They were not precise enough to establish wheelbase or model.

Navarro studied the corner.

“Left in a hurry?”

“Maybe,” the technician said. “Or someone forgot how much vehicle was still behind them.”

Madsona nodded.

“Happens with pickups. Big SUVs too.”

“Can it be tied to the ramp marks?” Daniels asked.

“No. Similar apparent freshness. That is all.”

Daniels made sure the two areas were logged separately.

The boat itself was documented where it floated.

Its exterior was ordinary except for the identity damage.

At the stern, the Hull Identification Number had been attacked with a pointed metal tool. Deep crossed scratches cut through the original stamping area. Some underlying impressions remained, but not enough could be read at the scene.

Navarro looked at the damaged panel.

“That isn’t wear.”

“No,” the technician said. “Somebody worked on it.”

The outboard motor remained attached.

Its manufacturer plate was visible near the mounting bracket.

The serial number had not been scratched, removed, or covered.

Navarro looked from the ruined hull number to the intact motor plate.

“They missed one.”

“It identifies the motor,” the technician said. “Not necessarily this boat.”

The number was photographed, read twice, and sent to the local station for immediate checking.

No one started the motor at the ramp.

The fuel tank remained connected and held enough fuel that the proposed trip between the ramps would have been plausible, even with detours. It did not tell them how much fuel had been present earlier or how far the boat had actually traveled.

The initial interior examination produced almost nothing.

No blood.

No wallet.

No clothing.

No restraints.

No usable fingerprints on the most obvious surfaces.

No indication that the interior had been recently washed or chemically cleaned.

The aluminum floor showed ordinary scratches, sand, water, and old dirt.

One partial shoe impression remained in a thin layer of residue near a seat support. Its length and width were compatible with an adult male and could fit Michael’s shoe size.

It could also fit many other men.

The tread was too incomplete to identify a manufacturer, model, or individual shoe.

“Consistent,” the technician said. “Not identifiable.”

Navarro looked at Daniels.

“Everything about the outside says somebody prepared it.”

“Nothing inside says they used it for Michael.”

“Not yet.”

They waited until the boat, motor, trailer, winch, line, and separate tire-mark areas had been entered into the evidence and scene records.

While the technicians worked, Daniels and Navarro stood with Madsona near the Fire Rescue tow vehicle.

The firefighter looked toward the water.

“Before this morning, that first ramp looked like the end of something.”

Navarro folded her arms.

“Road ends there. Water doesn’t.”

Madsona nodded.

Daniels watched the boat being prepared for controlled recovery.

“And we still do not know whether Weber reached either ramp.”

“No,” Madsona said.

Daniels thanked him.

“So do I,” Navarro said. “For the boat and for the geography.”

“Evan is fine.”

Daniels returned the cigarette pack.

“Your evidence scale.”

Madsona checked it.

“Still mostly full.”

The local officer told him he would see him at bowling.

Madsona pointed at him.

“Bring money this time.”

Then he began securing the Fire Rescue boat for the road.

Daniels and Navarro drove to the local station.

***

They had been inside less than ten minutes when the officer from the map room came toward them carrying a printed result.

“We got the outboard.”

Daniels stopped.

“Owner?”

“Original owner. Reported stolen about six weeks ago.”

“From this hull?”

“No. Different boat.”

Navarro took the page.

The stolen motor had been attached to a boat left temporarily in the water at a larger public facility. That location had multiple ramp lanes, a restroom building, more parking, and exterior cameras.

The boat itself had disappeared with the motor.

Its original hull had never been recovered.

The officer continued.

“The old report has video.”

“How good?”

“Good enough for the event. Not good enough for a person.”

The recording was transferred to the room’s wall screen.

The camera viewed the dock area and part of the public grounds. The road outside the facility sat near the edge of the frame.

A large white vehicle stopped beyond the entrance.

It did not enter the parking lot.

Its side profile appeared only partially through trees and the edge of the camera view. It could have been a Yukon, Tahoe, Expedition, or another full-size white SUV. No plate was visible.

One person exited.

The figure appeared to be an adult man of average height and ordinary build. The distance erased his face and reduced his clothing to light and dark areas without useful detail.

He walked directly to one particular boat.

He did not inspect the other vessels.

He released the line and entered.

Then he remained at the control area for several minutes.

The recording did not show a key.

It did not show exactly what his hands did.

Three minutes passed.

Then four.

The white SUV began to roll very slowly along the road outside the grounds.

It did not leave the camera view completely.

The man in the boat continued working.

A moment later, the outboard started.

The hull moved away from the dock.

At that instant, the white vehicle accelerated and disappeared from the edge of the frame.

The man guided the stolen boat into open water and left in the opposite direction.

Navarro replayed the final thirty seconds.

“The driver waited to see if the boat would start.”

“That is what the timing suggests,” Daniels said.

“One person in the boat. One person driving the SUV.”

“Yes.”

“At least two people.”

“Yes.”

The officer paused the image on the white vehicle.

“Could be a Yukon.”

“Could be several vehicles,” Daniels said.

Navarro enlarged the man near the boat until the pixels lost all useful shape.

“Adult male. Average height. Average build.”

“Which describes half the men in the case.”

The report established the theft.

It did not establish the thieves.

The trailer result took longer.

Its visible plate was missing, but a secondary number on the frame survived in a less obvious position. That number led to a separate theft report from approximately seven weeks before Day Zero.

The trailer had disappeared from a private property where it had stood without a boat.

The owner had already been contacted by the time Daniels and Navarro reviewed the file.

He confirmed the problem with the winch.

“It still worked,” he said over speakerphone. “But you had to watch the cable every second. It would climb to one side of the drum and jam.”

“What did you do when it started?” Daniels asked.

“Stopped. Took the load off a little. I kept a short metal bar in my truck. Used it to guide the cable back toward the middle.”

“The bar stayed with you?”

“In my truck. Not with the trailer.”

“Could someone use the winch without knowing that?”

“In daylight, maybe they’d figure it out. At night, with a boat pulling against it? They wouldn’t have much chance.”

The owner had ordered a replacement winch before the theft.

The new one arrived by mail after the trailer was gone.

It remained uninstalled at his home.

The old defect had traveled with the stolen trailer.

Daniels ended the call and looked again at the ramp photographs.

The likely sequence was not complicated.

Someone backed the trailer into the water.

The winch cable stacked to one side and bound under load.

Without knowing the owner’s method, the person backed farther down the ramp to float the boat almost completely onto the trailer.

The driven rear wheels reached the wet, slick portion of the concrete.

When the driver attempted to pull the loaded trailer out, the wheels spun and the vehicle shifted sideways.

After more than one attempt, the person backed down again, floated the boat off, pulled the empty trailer out, and pushed it into the brush.

The boat remained tied to the dock.

Navarro looked at the tire photographs.

“They knew where they wanted to recover it.”

“They did not know the trailer.”

The later controlled test of the outboard removed another possibility.

The motor started cleanly and ran without an obvious fault. The boat had not been abandoned because its engine failed. The available fuel was more than sufficient for the short route between the ramps.

Someone had arrived with a working boat.

Someone had intended to leave with it on the stolen trailer.

Something as ordinary as a bad winch and wet concrete had interrupted the plan.

The components had come from different places.

The trailer had been stolen first.

The other boat and outboard had been stolen approximately one week later.

At some point, the outboard was removed from that missing hull and installed on the unidentified aluminum boat now in evidence.

The present hull’s origin remained unknown.

Its registration could not be reconstructed from `FL 37`.

Its damaged identification number did not produce a complete result.

The boat that had originally carried the motor was still missing.

Daniels wrote three lines on a yellow sheet.

```text
TRAILER — STOLEN SEPARATELY
OUTBOARD — STOLEN WITH DIFFERENT BOAT
CURRENT HULL — UNKNOWN
```

Navarro read them.

“Somebody assembled this.”

“Yes.”

“Weeks before Michael disappeared.”

“They assembled a boat weeks before Michael disappeared.”

“You don’t think it was for him?”

“I think we have not proved what it was for.”

The white SUV in the older recording remained their only possible visual link.

Daniels called his station and asked for one limited comparison: Calver’s known vehicle records on the day of the boat theft.

He did not ask anyone to build a theory around it.

Approximately one hour later, the answer returned.

Calver’s car had been captured by a traffic camera roughly twenty-five kilometers from the larger ramp on the same day.

The date fit.

The broader timing did not exclude movement through the region.

The image identified Calver’s vehicle and plate. It did not show the driver. It did not show a passenger. It did not show a trailer. It was not the white SUV.

Navarro read the note.

“The day fits.”

“The day,” Daniels said. “Not the place.”

“It could be coincidence.”

“Yes.”

“Or his car could have been nearby while the others took the boat.”

“Yes.”

“Keep it?”

“With the file. Nothing more.”

By late afternoon, the investigation had gained a prepared watercraft assembled from stolen and unidentified parts, a likely failed recovery attempt, and an older video showing two people working together with a large white SUV.

It still had no direct evidence that Michael had occupied the boat.

Daniels did not call Sarah.

Not yet.

The partial shoe impression was not enough.

The route was not enough.

The white SUV from six weeks earlier was not enough.

A prepared boat was not automatically Michael’s boat.

The first deeper evidence examination began after the hull had been moved into a controlled facility.

Seats, mounts, rails, fasteners, and closed spaces were documented before anything was removed. The technicians worked from clean to more intrusive steps, preserving the possibility that a small trace mattered even when the larger surfaces had given them nothing.

One of the seat assemblies did not move smoothly along its rail.

At first, the obstruction appeared to be ordinary debris pressed into the narrow channel.

The technician stopped.

A strip of thin printed plastic was visible between the seat rail and the aluminum side support.

The position was photographed.

Then photographed again with scale and angled light.

The object was not pulled free until the entire area had been documented.

What came out was a crushed, empty tissue packet.

It had been folded around something harder.

The packet was placed on clean evidence paper and opened under controlled conditions.

Inside lay a small black device.

Navarro recognized its shape before anyone read the label.

The missing specialized USB device had never been an ordinary flash drive. It had presented mass storage and a manufacturer-specific serial interface to Michael’s workstation. Its individual serial number had survived in the forensic logs.

The device in the tissue packet carried a number.

Navarro opened the workstation report.

A technician read the serial aloud in groups.

She followed each group with one finger.

The first matched.

The second matched.

The third.

The fourth.

“Last four again,” she said.

The technician repeated them.

Navarro checked the report one final time.

Then she looked at Daniels.

“We got it.”

“The device?”

“The one from Michael’s workstation.”

The empty tissue packet had been wrapped tightly around the device and forced deep into the seat rail.

It had not fallen there.

It had been hidden.
