For roughly twenty years, I went by Nebukatneza in Azeroth. Then, in November 2026, I walked into the world with a different name above my head for the first time. It said Dennis Hilk. Two decades to come up with a new character name, and I went with the one I already had. The fellow wearing it is an undead paladin.

A morning in Azeroth and on Worldnode · 10 November 2026

“So, what level are you?”

“14.” — “Wait, weren't you level 14 this morning?” — “Yes! But now my Debian server can track my undead paladin through Blizzard's API. Even his professions!”

I was only about 20 percent away from level 15. Other people might have knocked out one more quest. Instead, I spent the entire morning exploring API endpoints, setting up OAuth credentials and building a Python collector for my blog.

While everyone else plays WoW and levels their characters, I sit in my Fish shell on Arch Linux asking my Debian 13 server, Worldnode, whether it knows how my undead paladin in Azeroth is doing. It actually answered: Dennis Hilk, Undead, Paladin, Horde, level 14. Apparently I just keep finding new excuses to make Linux servers do things. 😄

How Nebukatneza became nebu

My first World of Warcraft home was Destromath, a German EU PvP realm. Horde, undead warlock, guild Klinge der Macht. At some point Nebukatneza became nebu, and the name followed me well beyond WoW. I’ve written separately about how I ended up being called nebu.

My warlock’s story carried on after level 60. I played every expansion from The Burning Crusade through Dragonflight, and Nebukatneza stayed my main. Sometimes I took a break and came back halfway through an expansion. The continents, talent trees and rules kept changing. Whenever I came back, I still had my warlock.

From Vanilla to Dragonflight, one expansion at a time

At first, what I noticed was how much bigger the world got. There was Outland on the other side of the Dark Portal, and then there was Northrend. Over time, WoW also changed how I spent an evening. Dungeon Finder took care of finding a group. Garrisons, artifact weapons and Covenants gave their respective expansions their own progression systems. Mythic+ gave dungeon groups a challenge that kept scaling with higher keystones. Some parts of the game got easier to get into while others gave players more to master.

WoW and its first nine expansions by European release date. Features include pre-patch and later additions from the relevant era.

That timeline also shows how often the direction changed. Cataclysm rebuilt the old world around a new leveling experience. Mists simplified talent selection. Dragonflight brought back branching talent trees and gave professions more depth through specializations and crafting orders. Every few years, the developers made a fresh decision about where to ask for more of a player’s time and where to give some back.

Why Dragonflight was the last expansion I bought

“Candy Crush meets World of Warcraft — too much bling-bling everywhere.” That’s how I jokingly put it. Retail had started to feel too bright and too loud for me. Effects, achievement banners and rewards called for my attention so often that I ended up noticing them less. When the next progress bar was already filling before I’d enjoyed the last one, even a proper achievement started to feel a bit ordinary.

In the parts of the game I played, I often felt I needed less patience or skill to earn a reward than I remembered. High Mythic+ keys and Mythic raids still demand practice and coordination. Plenty of players enjoy the more accessible starting points, the bold look and the modern systems. I missed a quieter atmosphere, slower leveling and the satisfaction of getting something I’d spent a while working toward.

Dragonflight was where my own retail journey ended. I didn’t buy the retail expansions that followed it.

Back to Classic Era and eight level-60 characters

Classic Era felt right again. A trip across the world could take up an evening, and if I skipped a week, the dungeon set I’d worked for was still good enough for whatever I planned to do next. Over time I built up eight level-60 characters on Noggenfogger, plus a level-one auction-house orc. He’s the one with the loudest axe in the picture. You have to have priorities.

The Nebukatneza in this list carries on the name of my old Destromath warlock. I can’t find that very first character anymore. I look at this roster and still know exactly where “nebu” came from.

WoW Forever and a paladin named Dennis Hilk

World of Warcraft: Forever launched on 5 November 2026 here in Germany, on the same global launch that fell on 4 November in North America. Blizzard is building it as a permanent version alongside retail and Classic. The original continents are once again the setting for levels 1 through 60, with new quests and reworked zones. The two changes that mattered most to my fresh start were first names and surnames, and the chance to play a Forsaken paladin.

In Vanilla, paladins were Alliance-only. Blood elves brought the class to the Horde in The Burning Crusade. I played a Horde paladin back then and kept the warlock as my main. In Forever I wanted to see how the world felt if I started with the paladin this time. I did at least stick with the undead and the Horde.

I play in the EU in German on the Normal (PvE) ruleset. The public profile also showed level 14 when I checked it on 10 November. That number stays in the screenshot as the beginning of this story, even as the profile eventually moves on.

WoW Forever after 22 levels: Classic Era with a Redux filter?

Update: 11 November 2026. Yesterday I set up a Debian server to keep track of my undead paladin. Today Dennis Hilk is level 22, has Consecration, has tanked four dungeons and belongs to a guild called M O I N. I'm from East Frisia. The guild recruitment interview was effectively over after “Moin.”

The old world with fresh paint — perhaps a little too much

WoW Forever feels astonishingly close to Vanilla, and there's an incredible amount happening in the world. It reminds me of “WoW Classic Remastered” — or Classic Era with one of those GTA V Redux-style graphics filters. The water looks excellent. It also rains rather often, although living in Wiesmoor, East Frisia, I probably shouldn't complain about rain. Sometimes the colours are a bit too saturated for me, so I adjust them myself.

There are new quests and stories worth discovering. Seeing the new interpretation of Dalaran near Silverpine particularly fascinates me: in Vanilla and Classic Era, I used to wonder what it would look like. Not every addition wins me over, though. The enormous merchant camp beside the Crossroads — Wegekreuz, thank you very much! — is too much for my taste. And why is everyone setting up tents around campfires? I miss the special feeling of finding the last five people still awake by a campfire at five in the morning, chatting nonsense. Now it can feel more like grabbing a buff and leaving.

The Retail-style interface is another adjustment. I've arranged it so I can work with it, and learning a fresh paladin makes that easier. In Classic Era, when changes broke half my addons, I couldn't face setting up things like Clique on my priest all over again. On Forever I also don't yet bump into the same familiar player names as often as I did on Noggenfogger. Perhaps that will change after the initial rush.

Four dungeons as a tank: Ragefire, Undercity, Wailing Caverns and Shadowfang Keep

I've already tanked Ragefire Chasm, the new Undercity dungeon, Wailing Caverns and Shadowfang Keep. Every run went smoothly. I know the old routes by heart, even the twists of Wailing Caverns. The Undercity dungeon looks beautiful, but after just one run I found it pretty confusing. Then again, new players must wonder what on earth is happening when they first enter Razorfen Kraul or Wailing Caverns.

In Wailing Caverns someone needed an escort quest. “We'll clear the route first, then do that.” Somebody else asked where the fellow with the port wine was. “Outside the instance portal. I'll show you on the way out.” “Super, thanks!” “np.” Small conversations like that are part of what I love about these games.

In Shadowfang Keep one player said they were completely new. I told everyone not to worry: I'd pull the enemies back to us. So I worked with the corners and line of sight, brought casters into the group and delivered manageable packs. The damage dealers waited for the pull. We flew through the castle; I forgot to record it; and I came home with the Baron's Scepter as my new main-hand weapon. Arugal has been standing around in that castle for twenty years. He can wait.

Years of tanking as Bearforceone the druid and Arganosh the warrior still help. I watch enemy positioning and the healer's mana. A healer in Wailing Caverns even said, “Dennis, you don't have to keep looking at my mana. I'll tell you.” Fair enough. Old habit: I always keep the healer as a focus target. Having healed extensively myself on Desponia, I know both sides of the group.

And I genuinely enjoy tanking as a paladin. This was the missing Vanilla class for a Horde player; I only played one once The Burning Crusade arrived. Consecration, Retribution Aura, shield spikes, a cloak that damages attackers — the plan is to make every mob regret hitting me. Best of all, we can taunt. That's worth gold. Levels 1 to 20 on a warrior used to be miserable; on this paladin they were pleasant. There's also no shortage of undead to blast.

Relaxed groups, newcomers welcome — and instant tank invitations

I set the group finder to “Entspannt” (relaxed), welcome newcomers and let people know I can lead the run. That combination gets my tank invited almost instantly. The tool makes it easier to meet people, but it doesn't automatically teleport me into a randomly assembled dungeon party like Retail. I like that distinction.

So far every group has been friendly, patient and curious. Many players openly say this is their first time playing WoW at all, not simply their first time outside Retail. At the end people say, “Thanks, have fun!” or “See you next time!” In Retail I was used to the opposite: queue, rush, next queue. Here we're actually playing together. A good group isn't necessarily the fastest; it's the one where everyone can have fun and the newcomers get to learn.

Difficulty, music and being poor again

At level 22 the difficulty feels quite close to Classic Era — not Retail. A couple of fights have been tight, but I'm not going to pronounce judgment before I've tackled things like Onyxia, Zul'Gurub, Upper Blackrock Spire and Ahn'Qiraj. I do not want achievements and glittering rewards just for walking a few steps, though.

Recently I put on Subway to Sally and went questing through Silverpine. That felt like a little time machine: the same old forest, but this time with a class I couldn't have played as undead Horde in Vanilla. And I have to think about gold again. In Classic Era, new alts got 250 gold and four large bags from my established level-60 characters. Now every purchase counts. Baron Gorthak has work to do.

My verdict: Classic Era in Redux, not another Retail

After 22 levels I'm having a wonderful time. My biggest surprise is the friendliness of the community. I hope the developers don't make everything progressively easier. No automatic dungeon queues, no solo groups filled with bots, no achievement pop-ups for every trivial action, and nothing beyond cosmetic upgrades in a shop. New quests and good-looking water? Lovely. I just want the core of Classic to stay intact.

For someone completely new to WoW, I'd actually suggest playing Classic Era first, perhaps up to level 20, then trying Forever and comparing. If an old Vanilla veteran asks me whether to switch, my first question would be: “Have you played Classic Era?” It is still lively: I logged in today for an hour and was immediately asked to heal Molten Core on Desponia. I couldn't — there was chili con carne to cook.

My plan is one paladin to level 60, earn Quel'Serrar myself, and raid with him. When he's on a raid cooldown, I'll take a break or play Classic Era instead. Nebukatneza, Desponia, Bearforceone, Arganosh, Onenightstab and the rest stay there. Including Kurtcowbain, my usually friendly healing shaman, who sometimes gets angry, grabs his two-handed Nightfall and hopes for that priceless Windfury proc in a battleground. I'm not taking another eight characters to level 60. And I'm never farming the rogue T0.5 set again. Thirty-nine Stratholme runs and about seventeen UBRS runs were plenty for one lifetime.

WoW on Linux with Battle.net through Lutris

On Cthulhu I play on Arch Linux with dwm. For World of Warcraft I launch Battle.net through Lutris and Wine, then install and start the relevant WoW version there. My Steam games go through Steam and Proton. I cover both setups in my Linux gaming guide to Steam, Proton and Lutris.

How the WoW Forever Observer works

The independent Armory describes two delays. Characters appear from level 10 after logging out, and the Armory can still serve an older saved snapshot. A future sync can turn higher levels and newly reported achievements into dated milestones. The moment it happened in-game remains unknown. If a character gains several levels between checks, the history can record only the level actually observed.

Blizzard’s official API returns my Forever character’s level, experience, equipment and professions. On 10 November 2026, I successfully ran the Python collector on my Debian 13 server, Worldnode, and published its first real JSON snapshot. It contains only selected display data: no credentials and no permanent gameplay history. The website shows the last successful sync time; the hourly automated run is configured separately. An older snapshot is not live telemetry.

I first entered Azeroth as Nebukatneza and somewhere along the way came out as nebu. This time it was simpler. After about twenty years, I finally walked in as Dennis Hilk.
