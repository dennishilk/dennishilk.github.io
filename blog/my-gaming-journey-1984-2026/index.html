A personal gaming journey · 1984–2026

My Favourite Games Through the Years – NES, Amiga, DOS, LAN and Linux

By Dennis Hilk6 November 2026A long personal story

I was born in 1984. My first game was Super Mario on the NES. My first real “I LOVE this game” moment came with a golden cartridge: The Legend of Zelda. More than three decades later, I sit in front of Cthulhu, my Arch Linux machine, and sometimes I would still rather play Monkey Island than the newest blockbuster.

I thought this would be a list of favourite games. Then the memories came back: Dad’s pipe during a submarine patrol in the Atlantic, enormous component catalogues, Sven carrying his PC over for the weekend, a Nintendo 64 travelling to Paraguay, a foot that could disconnect DSL, and Mum’s immortal warning: “Don’t turn it into a chip shop!” Her actual German line was “Mach keine Pommfritzbude draus!”

A note on memory: This is neither a ranking nor a complete list of every game I have owned. I recognised many of them again through old menus, screenshots and gameplay videos. Where a hardware model or date remains uncertain, I say so. A game’s release date and the year I played it are not necessarily the same.

I first met Sven at my parents’ silver wedding anniversary celebration. He is the son of a former work colleague of my father. That was where our friendship began, long before the crossover cables, Pringles and entire LAN weekends.

Chapter 01 · The late 1980s and early 1990s

Super Mario, golden Zelda and a Game Boy with accessories

My first videogame machine was the NES, and Mario was the first game. But the one that truly hooked me was The Legend of Zelda. That gold cartridge! It was not just a game, it was a world I wanted to return to. I also loved Castlevania’s extraordinary music, DuckTales and Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles. My affection for game soundtracks started early.

Then came the original grey Game Boy: first Tetris and Super Mario Land. Later I owned a clip-on light and magnifier, the Game Boy Camera and the Game Boy Printer. I was less interested in creating fine photography than in the technology actually working. Eventually I traded the collection away to afford something newer. Perfectly sensible at the time; a small regret today.

From Game Boy photography to MMS: My first Nokia was a 3210, and my first camera phone was the wonderfully circular-keyboard 3650. Sending Sven a picture by MMS seemed to take forever. The quality was, shall we say, not quite Bob Ross.
Chapter 02 · The Amiga 500

Monkey Island, Turrican and more adventures than disks could hold

The Amiga 500 made me fall properly in love with adventure games and soundtracks. The Secret of Monkey Island has stayed with me forever. The two Fettuccini brothers at the circus, Guybrush, and that rubber chicken with a pulley in the middle: I still laugh thinking about them. I played or owned every entry in the series. The original runs on my retro PC again, while the Special Edition lives on Cthulhu. I keep pressing F10: classic graphics, upgraded graphics, classic again. I probably know every scene in both forms.

Of course I played Monkey Island 2 as well. Alongside it were Turrican II (what music!), Cinemaware’s Wings, Lotus Turbo Challenge, both Eye of the Beholder games, Ambermoon, Beneath a Steel Sky, Maniac Mansion, Simon the Sorcerer and F/A-18 Interceptor. Another World fascinated me – and killed me so often I doubt I got beyond two or three stages.

I recognised the original Leisure Suit Larry immediately: that tiny chap in his white suit! After comparing old screenshots and gameplay videos, I finally recognised my long-forgotten billiards game: Archer Maclean’s Pool, not Arcade Pool. As for Wings, I have returned to it on Steam as Wings! Remastered Edition.

Someone asked on Facebook which game character I would want beside me if the world ended. I was apparently the only person who answered Guybrush Threepwood. The laughs and likes were worth it.
Chapter 03 · Friends in front of the television

Super Nintendo, a black PlayStation and an N64 for José

On the Super Nintendo, Mario was my starting point again, but The Legend of Zelda: A Link to the Past was the real love. Donkey Kong Country and Super Mario Kart were favourites when friends visited. I played Street Fighter, though I was always more of a Mortal Kombat person. Super Castlevania IV and Super Turrican delivered another pair of unforgettable soundtracks; the wonderfully absurd humour of The Ren & Stimpy Show: Veediots! was exactly my thing.

The PlayStation 1 was the only PlayStation I ever owned, and it worked as a music-CD player more often than you might expect. I mostly played a Need for Speed, though I am not sure which entry. What I do remember is dismantling the entire console, spraying the shell black and putting it together again. It worked! I later fitted a modchip myself. Our little rental shop in Fintel also belongs to this period. The family chip-shop warning was becoming entirely justified.

Super Mario 64 stunned me with 3D and its analogue controls. I first played GoldenEye 007 during a stopover in Iceland on a flight to or from the United States. But my favourite N64 memory is about a person, not a game. Through school, an exchange student called José from Paraguay stayed with our family. He was completely fascinated by my Nintendo 64. On his last day I simply gave it to him.

My N64 went to Paraguay. We kept writing to each other for some time afterwards. My parents were so touched by the gift that they contributed some money toward the next PC I was already planning. The N64 was my last own console. Friends had PS2s, Xboxes and Dreamcasts; I was happy to play at their homes, but my future was on the PC.
Chapter 04 · My first PC

Schneider Euro PC, Prince of Persia and DOS without Windows

My first PC was a Schneider Euro PC. First game: Prince of Persia. I recognised its graphics immediately. Those infuriating jumps? I still hate them. My first truly beloved PC game was once again The Secret of Monkey Island. Then came LOOM, Zak McKracken, SimCity and its stupid natural disasters, Pirates!, Centurion, Microsoft Flight Simulator 3.0, Test Drive, IK+, Rick Dangerous, Wings of Fury, LHX Attack Chopper and the original 2D Duke Nukem. I played Duke Nukem II later too.

Wings of Fury was especially tied to friendship. After school, a friend and I were allowed two hours on the PC – one hour each. Then it was time to take our bicycles outside. I probably put hundreds of hours into that game, but I remember the afternoons even more.

My next machine was a 486, running DOS only. I never used Windows 3.1 or 3.11; Windows 95 would be my first Windows. Across that 486 and other early PCs I played Wolfenstein 3D (I still remember ILM), Rise of the Triad, Hexen, Raptor, Primal Rage, Day of the Tentacle, Sam & Max, Larry 6, Broken Sword, Mad TV and SimCity 2000.

Command & Conquer: Tiberian Dawn swallowed what felt like a thousand hours, mostly playing alone before our LAN era. I tried GDI, but favoured Nod. Red Alert followed. I also loved Quake, even though it ran miserably on the 486. Somehow impulse 9 is still committed to memory.

A command for long-term memory

Quake: what did impulse 9 do?

The developer console was a text prompt inside the game, opened with the console key – normally ~ on a US keyboard, depending on the layout. It accepted settings and commands. In the original Quake game code, version 1.01, impulse 9 gave the single player all eight weapons and both keys, set ammunition to 100 shells, 200 nails, 100 rockets and 200 cells, and selected the rocket launcher. It did not grant health, armour or invulnerability; in co-op and deathmatch the function returned without applying the cheat. [3]

One tiny line and the entire arsenal appeared. Decades later, I still remember it.

And submarines! Aces of the Deep and later Silent Hunter. As a teenager I sat in front of the PC with my father’s pipe, patrolling the Atlantic. Sometimes an hour passed without contact. No explosions or reward animations – just the patrol. That emptiness was part of the appeal.

Chapter 05 · Windows, 3dfx and the first networks

“Don't turn it into a chip shop!”

A local PC shop owner from a couple of villages away delivered my first Windows 95 PC, set it up, and demonstrated smooth video playback to my parents. The video was Weezer – Buddy Holly. I remember it clearly. I still know that video today, and whenever I watch it, the song immediately gets stuck in my head. It belonged perfectly to the CD-ROM era: the Windows 95 CD included multimedia extras, including that video, to demonstrate the system’s audio and video capabilities. [1] I also remember people parachuting from a plane in a clip, though I cannot reliably identify that one.

The PC had an S3 ViRGE and a huge black Slot 1 processor, most likely a Pentium II. When I first opened the case, I expected a familiar CPU socket. Instead there was a processor cartridge. What on earth?

ISA, PCI and AGP: the slots behind the games

Expansion cards plugged into long connectors on the motherboard. ISA was the older PC bus, used for things such as sound cards. PCI provided a faster general-purpose connection for graphics, networking and other cards; the original Voodoo Graphics used PCI. AGP arrived as a connection specifically for graphics cards. These slots were not interchangeable. Buying a new graphics card therefore started with a look inside the case, before I ever launched a game. The exact combination of slots on my old motherboard is not documented.

A PC magazine later showed a before-and-after comparison with and without a 3D accelerator. I wanted one immediately. Although the machine was less than six months old, I eventually got an original 3dfx Voodoo Graphics (Voodoo 1). I fitted it myself, external VGA pass-through cable and all. My mother used a warning that she still repeats: “Mach keine Pommfritzbude draus!” In other words: do not turn this into a chip shop!

First test: Duke Nukem 3D. It looked exactly the same as before. Disappointment! The normal Duke renderer was not using the card. Then came Turok: Dinosaur Hunter: WOW. And finally, thanks to a magazine, GLQuake. Hardware-accelerated graphics and filtered textures! I kept switching between software Quake and GLQuake just to stare at the difference – exactly as I still do with Monkey Island and F10.

Why the Voodoo needed another graphics card

The S3 ViRGE was my everyday graphics card: it drew the Windows desktop and also offered early 3D functions. The original Voodoo Graphics was an additional 3D accelerator without a 2D desktop of its own. A short VGA cable took the S3’s output into the Voodoo, and the monitor connected to the Voodoo’s output. Normally it passed the S3 signal through; a supported 3D game switched the output to the Voodoo. [4]

With software rendering, the CPU calculates the image. Installing a Voodoo did not give the normal DOS version of Duke Nukem 3D a different renderer, so it initially looked unchanged. Turok’s supported accelerated rendering path used the new hardware for its 3D world. GLQuake used OpenGL; early 3dfx drivers provided the necessary subset through a game-focused MiniGL library. Filtered textures and hardware rendering made the change immediately visible to me. [5]

By Quake II, the PC was starting to feel old again. I did not exactly rush to tell my parents. On a trip to America I received an English copy of Windows 98 as a gift. Back home I saved up for a Celeron 300A at 450 MHz on a 100 MHz front-side bus, a new board and an NVIDIA RIVA TNT. My self-built Athlon came later; I will return to its pencil trick below.

Celeron 300A: turning 300 into 450 MHz

The 300A’s fixed multiplier gave 4.5 × roughly 66.7 MHz bus clock = 300 MHz. A 100 MHz front-side bus produced 450 MHz: a 50 percent increase in core clock. Its integrated 128 KB L2 cache also distinguished the 300A from the earlier cacheless Celeron 300. [6] That combination of an affordable processor and a potentially huge clock increase made it an overclocking legend. My chip working at 450 MHz did not guarantee that every example would do the same.

Sven, crossover cables and entire weekends

Sven introduced me to Unreal Tournament. Soon he would arrive on Friday afternoon with his entire PC and stay until Sunday. We never used a hub – just a crossover cable directly between our machines. First we exchanged files. Then came Counter-Strike, nearly always de_dust, Quake III Arena and whatever else we liked. Team selection? Rock, paper, scissors.

The crossover cable was not a special kind of internet connection. On those 10/100 Mbps Ethernet links it crossed the transmit and receive pairs, connecting one PC’s outputs to the other PC’s inputs. Two suitable network cards could communicate directly without a hub; IP addresses and games still needed configuring. Modern ports often detect the wiring automatically. [8]

Those were not entirely indoor weekends. Using my father’s old video camera, Sven and I filmed our own Jackass-style nonsense outside: ASSHOLE – don’t try this at home, complete with a Windows Movie Maker title sequence. The recording still exists on VHS-C. I still own the adapter and a USB capture device too. I intend to digitise it; perhaps a short extract will join this article later.

DSL with a foot switch

Dial-up once occupied the phone line: “Deeeeeennis, I need to make a call!” Then we skipped ISDN entirely and moved straight to DSL. When a Telekom employee advised my parents, I confidently announced that we could install the cable ourselves. Dad's reply: “Can we?” He drilled through the ceiling from the utility room, and I connected the wires upstairs. I did not even have a proper socket. It worked until my foot knocked the cables during a game – and I went offline. Today I would call it improvised cabling; back then it was an adventure.

With an analogue modem, calls and data occupied the same voice line: while I was online, nobody could make a normal phone call. DSL carried data in other frequency bands on the copper line; a splitter separated telephone and DSL on those older installations. Together with a flat-rate tariff, the carefully timed dial-up visit became a connection I could leave running. Faster internet and the local crossover LAN were still two separate things.

I also had to explain flat-rate internet to Mum. No, the PC would not cost a hundred thousand marks just because it stayed connected all week.

On family holidays to Borkum, Florida, Menorca or Lake Garda, I occasionally left Kazaa downloading. After returning I might find one usable film, one in Korean, one with sound only, heaps of MP3s and suspicious names such as NEWMETALLICAALBUM.mp3.exe. And yes: I did execute one of those disguised files once. Just once. For roughly a second I saw the command prompt racing through something, then the window disappeared. Then: oh shit. I backed up the other data and reinstalled Windows as a precaution. Who knew what had just installed itself – Sub7, some worm or another tool? I do not know what it actually was.

A small filename, a big deception

Why .mp3.exe is not a music file

Many Windows configurations hid extensions for known file types. A file called NEWMETALLICAALBUM.mp3.exe could therefore appear as NEWMETALLICAALBUM.mp3. The part that matters is the final extension: .exe, meaning an executable program. The “.mp3” before it is simply part of the misleading name. Double extensions were a classic social-engineering lure for spreading malware: make somebody expect an album, then get them to launch a program.

The useful lesson survives Kazaa: show filename extensions and be suspicious of unexpected executable downloads. Microsoft explains how to show them [2].

LAN snacks included Pringles flavours we knew from Florida: Fat Free, Sour Cream & Onion and Cream Cheese & Chive, ordered online, plus crates of malt beer. I even tested whether a Fat-Free crisp left a grease mark: put it into a book, close book, reopen, shake crumbs out. Almost no mark. Rigorous science, obviously.

Chapter 06 · 2004 to today

Nebukatneza and the world that does not run away

Sven introduced me to World of Warcraft too. I had never been as excited about Diablo as he was, but I thought I would just have a look at WoW. My first character was Nebukatneza, an undead warlock on the German PvP server Destromath. I still see him hopping through Silverpine Forest, with in-game music or Subway to Sally and Schandmaul MP3s playing. Eventually he reached level 60, wore the T2 Nemesis set and carried Azuresong Mageblade. Succubus seduce, a Soul Fire cast that felt two hours long, Immolate, Fear – PvP chaos. And of course the epic Tarren Mill battles!

I experimented with hunters, mages, rogues and all sorts of tricks, including Blink through doors and solo Scarlet Monastery attempts. Once I received a three-day ban after kiting a giant mob from somewhere around Feralas or Azshara all the way to Orgrimmar. Another time I experimented with a file change I had read about online and reached GM Island. Suddenly I was immobilised outside Orgrimmar and got a serious lecture in GM chat. I was genuinely terrified of losing my character and stopped messing with that kind of thing. On a separate occasion my account was hacked; a GM appeared in-game, advised me to change my passwords and restored my equipment.

I loved Wrath of the Lich King, took a break after Cataclysm, came back partway into Mists of Pandaria (I love pandas!), and returned now and then. Dragonflight was the last expansion I bought. I missed The Burning Crusade and especially the original game. Sadly, I can no longer find that first old Nebukatneza character. But the name lives on.

Classic Era: eight level-60 characters and an auction-house orc

Today, on Noggenfogger (PvP), I have Nebukatneza (warlock), Bearforceone (druid tank), Onenightstab (rogue), Desponia (priest), Crocodile (hunter), Arganosh (warrior), Kurtcowbain (shaman) and Katharina (mage) – all level 60. Plus Onlyzugzug, my level-one auction-house orc, naturally wearing smart clothes and carrying an axe enchanted with Fiery Weapon.

One story means even more to me: The hunter quest demon in Silithus was unbelievably annoying. About four days after beating it, somebody asked me at the campfire in Orgrimmar around 7 a.m. if I could help. So I took the flight to Silithus – it felt an hour long – and showed them what worked for me. I could not interfere in the solo fight. By their third or fourth attempt, they got it. That is the kind of Classic WoW I love.

The mage Katharina is named after my wife. It is the only one of my characters she has ever played. I was about to take a shower; she spotted the character and said something like, “Oh, cute, Katharina!” I explained keys 1–5, the Intellect buff and that she could try fighting anything that was not dark red. When I came back, the mage was still alive, eating and drinking to recover. Her verdict: “Levelling takes a long time.” Mine: “All the others are already 60 – ask me!”

My wife played Katharina for roughly an hour. The mage still carries her name.

Why Classic Era fits my life now: Work, house, garden and endless technical projects take time. If I do not log in for a week, that is fine. No fresh stack of raids or urgent PvP bracket to chase. The gear I worked for remains special. Azeroth will still be there.

More recent favourites, less chasing trends

I have loved plenty of newer games. Battlefield 3 was my favourite Battlefield, followed by 4; I particularly loved Battlefield 1's campaign. South Park: The Stick of Truth had me laughing constantly. Kerbal Space Program, GTA V (and yes, I knew the first top-down GTA), Far Cry 3, Red Dead Redemption 2, The Witcher 3: Blood and Wine, Kingdom Come, Baldur's Gate 3, Starfield and Alan Wake 2 all left their marks. Alan Wake made the GPU sweat again.

Microsoft Flight Simulator 2024, on the other hand, upset me with lag and disconnections after buying early. My rule now is simple: Never preorder again. I am currently enjoying War Thunder for its beautifully modelled vehicles and ships. And Monkey Island, Quake, Duke Nukem 3D and Half-Life? I never stopped enjoying them.

Chapter 07 · From catalogues to Cthulhu

A pencil, a box of components and finally Arch Linux

I used to pore over enormous Conrad catalogues, perhaps Alternate too, planning component combinations. Ready-made PCs in newspapers or Aldi adverts did not interest me: “For that money I could almost build two myself!” After the Voodoo and Celeron came my 850 MHz Athlon (roughly 910 MHz stable), a used GeForce2 Pro, 256 MB RAM, a 30 GB Maxtor drive, Plextor PlexWriter and Toshiba SD-M1402 DVD drive. I still own a PlexWriter in my retro machine today.

850 MHz → roughly 910 MHz

The Athlon pencil trick, explained from scratch

Megahertz means millions of clock cycles per second. At 850 MHz that is 850 million cycles, not necessarily that many completed instructions. On this generation of systems the CPU clock came from the front-side bus base clock multiplied by a multiplier. Overclocking means operating hardware above its specified clock frequency.

On certain early Socket A Athlon and Duron processors, tiny L1 contact bridges on the package were cut. Conductive graphite from a pencil could reconnect the appropriate pairs, allowing a suitable motherboard to change the multiplier. The pencil line did not itself make the processor faster: it enabled a different setting. Later Athlon generations used different locks and packages; this was never a universal trick for every Athlon. [7]

I later used conductive silver paint. It can provide a more durable, lower-resistance connection than a graphite trace. My exact stepping, motherboard, and combination of bus clock and multiplier are not documented; the roughly 910 MHz result is not enough to reconstruct them reliably.

Push the clock too far and electrical signals may miss their timing requirements; temperature and power delivery matter too. Incorrect calculations or memory accesses can crash Windows. A blue screen reveals a failure but does not identify its precise cause on its own. For me, almost 1 GHz and the “blue screen of vengeance” – Bluescreen der Vergeltung – lost to roughly 910 MHz that actually let me play.

Later came an Athlon 64 X2 3800+, GeForce 7800 GTX, 2 GB RAM and my first SATA drive: such tiny cables compared to IDE! Half-Life 2 impressed me, and Crysis was a visual bomb until fast movement brought the frame rate down. So I turned settings down and closed background apps. Even today I close ICQ and Winamp before some Windows 98 retro gaming sessions.

IDE, also called parallel ATA, connected drives using wide ribbon cables. Two devices could share a channel, with the appropriate master/slave settings. SATA replaced that arrangement with slim data cables and a separate connection for each drive. Less cable clutter and more room for airflow and hands: I could appreciate that difference before even switching the machine on.

The path continued through an early Nehalem Core i7 and GeForce 9800 GT, then an i7-4790K and red MSI GTX 970, later an i7-9700K, a GeForce RTX 3060 Ti and ultimately an AMD Radeon. Recurring NVIDIA headaches on Gentoo and other Linux distributions helped persuade me to change. Cthulhu now runs a Ryzen 7 5800X3D and Radeon RX 9060 XT.

Operating systems were a hobby in themselves. Windows ME drove me mad; for reasons even I cannot explain I used Windows 2000 Advanced Server for a while and still have the original disc. Vista, by contrast, worked well for me in its classic theme. I had tried Debian, Slackware, OS/2 Warp 4 and KNOPPIX from a magazine CD early on. Around 2013 Linux Mint with Cinnamon became a serious daily system. Windows for gaming with Sven was available on a physical removable hard-drive caddy. Later I tried Fedora, SUSE, NixOS, Gentoo, Arch, countless desktops and window managers, including Ratpoison. For one month, just for fun, I lived entirely in a shell.

The caddy was a pull-out holder for a real hard drive: power down, exchange the drive, and a different system started on the next boot. That let me switch between Linux and Windows for gaming with Sven. It was a very physical operating-system switch.

Especially in earlier NVIDIA Linux setups, the kernel module, kernel version and graphics driver had to fit together. Changing kernels or missing matching headers could prevent a module building or loading, leaving the graphical session in trouble too. [9] That describes one possible failure class, rather than retrospectively diagnosing every old crash I experienced. My own frustrations were one reason I later changed hardware.

Today I run Arch Linux with dwm on X11, with a 3440 × 1440, 144 Hz main monitor. No clock widget, blinking panels or assistants popping up uninvited.

Arch Linux lets me assemble the system from the components I want. dwm is a small X11 window manager: it arranges windows in tiled, maximised or floating layouts and groups them with tags. A full desktop environment bundles many more applications, settings interfaces and services. With dwm I choose the extras myself; its basic configuration lives in C source that I edit and recompile. [10] The uncluttered screen is a deliberate choice, rather than a consequence of weak hardware.

One final cameo 📎

Clippy, may I just launch the program?

Clippy, known as Karl Klammer in German, was the animated Microsoft Office assistant: a paperclip with eyes that appeared during writing and offered help. For somebody trying to finish a document, those suggestions could feel like interruptions. [11]

I wanted to click Start and launch a program, not get interrupted by an assistant.

Compared with modern unsolicited AI features, the old paperclip almost seems sympathetic to me. On Cthulhu, though, it can stay here in the article.

Original paperclip tribute with gentle CSS motion; not an original Microsoft graphic or animation. It stays still when reduced motion is requested.

The hardware has become incredibly fast. I have become a more selective gamer. I do not buy every trend, and I play less than I once did. Some of my favourites are more than thirty years old. They still work and still make me happy. And when I launch Monkey Island, I will probably press F10 again.
