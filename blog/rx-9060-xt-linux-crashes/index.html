CTHULHU CRASH LAB · MISSION 04

RX 9060 XT on Linux: Hardlocks, GEM Handles and PCI Access Loss

By Dennis Hilk~44 min + lab11 October 2026

A new Radeon that initially worked beautifully on Linux. Then games where both monitors suddenly went black and the GPU fan shot to full speed. No useful final error message. I did not return the card. I wanted to understand why.

Status: 25 October 2026. My current Arch Linux/dwm, kernel 7.2.11, RADV 26.2.5 evidence includes an older GPUVM fault, later rejected Stalker submissions, and two Arma hardlocks with PCI access loss before reset. Validation-layer CPU faults and SATA transport errors remain separate findings. No single culprit or permanent fix is established. I am pausing repeated game starts without a targeted question.↓ Jump to the latest update from 25 October
Latest results: GravityMark & blackbox (25 October) · Heap-release caller · Validation run · Arma control · PCI failure in the black box · Current assessment · Six collectors and evidence

Why I tried AMD at all

I have no particular allegiance to NVIDIA, AMD or Intel. A graphics card should run my software, perform well and, one small aesthetic requirement, preferably be white. But my RTX 3060 Ti sometimes caused trouble on self-configured Linux systems: Gentoo would be running well until a game, driver update or OBS problem started another round of troubleshooting.

I found an old ASUS Strix Radeon R9 380 for €20 on a local classifieds site. I installed another M.2 SSD, put the card in and freshly tried Arch, Debian and Gentoo. OBS, Steam, Lutris and WoW Classic all worked beautifully. Debian was sometimes the awkward one when I wanted newer drivers, but the basic compatibility test passed.

Then I loaded Baldur's Gate 3. The aging R9 380 simply did not have the performance. I had already seen the white PowerColor RX 9060 XT Hellhound Spectral White 16 GB online and liked it. When the next paycheck arrived, the €20 experiment became an actual GPU upgrade.

At first it all worked

With the Hellhound I mostly played Baldur's Gate 3 and WoW Classic. Both were excellent. The RX 9060 XT was not a failure from day one. I discovered the serious issues later, when I wanted to see some eye candy and started S.T.A.L.K.E.R. 2 and other titles that I had previously neglected.

System and graphics updates had happened in the meantime. I cannot prove that those updates caused the crashes: I had not performed comparable tests in those games before. Sequence in time is not proof of causality.

In particular, Quake RTX was not the same as a whole-system hardlock. A game accepting inputs and a computer receiving a network file demonstrate that important parts of the operating system were still functioning. Black displays alone do not identify whether the failure lies in display output, rendering, a driver, PCIe or the entire machine.

My additional observation on 23 October: The problems are not limited to S.T.A.L.K.E.R. 2; Arma 3 was particularly troublesome in my earlier tests too. My impression is that more demanding graphics workloads fail more often. Quake II and Duke Nukem ran without issues. This is my personal test history, not a measured performance threshold. I keep the Quake RTX display failure described above separate; a title appearing in my Steam library does not establish a test or its result.

Cthulhu, at least say OUCH before you die

The frustrating part was how often there was no useful AMDGPU error in the journal immediately before the worst crash. I saw a previous Arch message resembling amdgpu: device lost from bus, but mostly got black monitors, a roaring GPU fan and a forced restart. I was not even demanding an instant fix; I wanted the machine to shout “Ow, my leg!” before going down.

During a violent GPU/PCIe failure, the kernel may not get a chance to generate or persist a meaningful message. Missing logs are not evidence of a healthy driver stack. I watched live journal output and tried to isolate hardware factors too.

EXAMPLE · Observe Linux kernel messages (not an original historic log)
journalctl -kf -o short-monotonic
This command follows new kernel messages. A severe lockup can still happen without a useful final line.

Riser out, NIC out, games moved between drives

I tested the GPU with the PCIe riser and directly in the motherboard. I varied BIOS/PCIe settings, even removed the 10 GbE network card connected to my NAS, and tested the PSU's original PCIe cables instead of the white sleeved extensions. None of those changes eliminated the crashes.

At one stage the observed PCIe topology was: 00:03.1 Root Port → 53:00.0 Switch Upstream → 54:00.0 Switch Downstream → 55:00.0 GPU, with a reported 8 GT/s ×8 link. Worth investigating, yes; proof of root cause, no – especially because direct-slot tests failed too.

My power supply is from Corsair, but I cannot currently verify its exact model or capacity. I remember one 6+2-pin and one 6-pin PCIe power connection. That already ruled out GPU choices requiring two full 8-pin leads. Toggling the Hellhound's OC/Silent dual BIOS produced no useful change. I considered firmware flashing strictly as a last resort; I never did it.

A second suspect: SATA CRC errors

Alongside game stalls, the kernel reported BadCRC, READ FPDMA QUEUED and hard SATA link resets around ata2 and a Samsung 860 EVO. Those were real findings, but they need their own cable/port investigation.

To keep the problems apart, I relocated games from the SATA SSD to my system NVMe and also tried the other NVMe drive. The GPU hardlocks persisted at the time. That weakens the SATA SSD as a sole explanation. It does not mean the SATA link is healthy; cable and port tests are still on the to-do list.

The same S.T.A.L.K.E.R. route, a different OS

I kept a separate Windows 11 NVMe around, primarily for Battlefield. That gave me a useful control: same S.T.A.L.K.E.R. 2 save, Continue, spawn point and a lap around the base, with graphics broadly maxed out and the same RX 9060 XT. Under Windows that route completed without issue. Battlefield also ran as usual.

That is much more informative than comparing to an unrelated benchmark, but still not a mathematical proof against hardware. Windows and Linux exercise different graphics and driver paths, and every setting need not be identical. It made the Linux-specific execution path a stronger suspect.

112 watts: a hypothesis, not a miracle cure

I have capped WoW Classic at 60 FPS for years, even on a 144 Hz monitor. Why burn extra power for a game that already runs beautifully? Earlier Windows undervolting experiments had shown me substantial power savings. So I wondered: Could the PSU, power cable or a transient load be involved?

I cut the GPU power limit from 160 to 112 W. It prevented some hard crashes initially, but not reliably: the short stalls remained and not every full crash disappeared. Changing a power cap changes clocks and operating states as well. The result could not establish a broken power supply. At 112 W, Arma 3 still hard-crashed; Quake RTX lost display output while the game and LocalSend continued running. Black Flag Resynced still suffered intermittent blackouts after roughly 3–10 minutes of gameplay.

Beta kernels, Mesa versions – and finally 26.2.4

Deep in forum discussions I found other Linux/RDNA4 users with somewhat similar issues. That made a known or actively investigated software problem plausible. I tried a newer beta kernel; no improvement, sometimes worse. Other Mesa candidates were unsuccessful too. Two promising suggestions caused faster blackouts on my machine. I no longer know their exact version numbers and will not invent them.

My principle was to change one factor where possible and observe its result. Eventually I tried Mesa 26.2.4. This was the first version I tested where Arma 3 no longer crashed immediately on world entry and S.T.A.L.K.E.R. 2 was playable for longer periods at normal power. The kernel and firmware remained standard NixOS packages; I explicitly overrode Mesa in configuration.nix at that time, including its 32-bit component.

HISTORICAL NIXOS CONFIGURATION EXCERPT · 11 OCTOBER 2026
boot.kernelPackages = pkgs.linuxPackages_latest;
# Within hardware.graphics:
mesaVersion = "26.2.4";
# Both 64-bit and 32-bit Mesa are overridden

With that Mesa stack I raised the limit in controlled steps: 112 → 120 → 130 → 140 → 150 → 160 W, using S.T.A.L.K.E.R. as the test. The previous immediate failure did not return. This is evidence that changing the graphics software stack mattered in my tests. It does not identify a proven upstream fix commit.

After the earlier tests, I wanted evidence more useful than a black screen and a GPU fan at full speed. Cthulhu is now running Arch Linux with dwm again. To catch the next reproducible failure, I built a Cthulhu GPU Blackbox: a Bash utility with a separate systemd logger, once-per-second AMDGPU telemetry, a live kernel journal and a udev-triggered Linux devcoredump collector. It writes to a separately mounted NVMe, independently of X11 and the game window.

An unusually close upstream report

A separate VKD3D-Proton report (#3221) on an RX 9070 XT documents the same distinctive combination: SQC (data), PERMISSION_FAULTS: 0x3, 0x00701431, vkd3d_queue and the gfx_0.0.0 timeout. A matching signature does not prove an identical root cause. The trigger could still be in the game, VKD3D-Proton, RADV/Mesa, AMDGPU or hardware. In particular, the other reporter's FSR observations are not evidence about FSR in my S.T.A.L.K.E.R. test.

Conclusion of the 21 October capture: I have not resolved the cause of this failure. I now have a reproducible, timestamped crash with the first AMDGPU coredump automatically preserved by my own tool. For debugging, that is genuine progress.

22 October 2026: From Low to Epic – and my first actionable Unreal crash report

The GPU black-box capture from the previous day made a much more focused hypothesis possible. The additional RADV hang report contained a command stream of 9,376 DWORDs (37,504 bytes) that matched the AMDGPU devcoredump's main command stream word for word. The last reached trace marker was 0x37 (decimal 55), followed by a compute dispatch of 287 × 121 × 1 groups. A descriptor read in the shader was reconstructed at 0xffff80000e000100, on the exact GPU virtual-memory page reported by the fault. RADV's memory history recorded an earlier allocation and release for that region, and the tool warned of a potential use-after-free.

This is evidence for a specific hypothesis, not proof of its cause. Without the D3D12 Release/fence sequence and the precise GPU execution point, I cannot establish who released the buffer or conclusively assign blame to the game, VKD3D-Proton, RADV or a lower layer. I therefore tested one narrow change: hide the optional Vulkan VK_EXT_descriptor_buffer extension from VKD3D-Proton while retaining the existing RADV diagnostic options.

MY S.T.A.L.K.E.R. 2 STEAM LAUNCH OPTIONS · TEST ONLY, NOT A UNIVERSAL FIX
VKD3D_DISABLE_EXTENSIONS=VK_EXT_descriptor_buffer RADV_DEBUG=hang,noumr %command%
VKD3D_DISABLE_EXTENSIONS hides the named extension and is intended to select the alternative descriptor-set path. RADV_DEBUG=hang,noumr was already used for diagnostics. No Mesa or kernel packages were changed for this test. The later Proton logs from 23 October explicitly confirm filtering at runtime. For the two earlier comparison sessions I document the configured option without claiming an additional retrospective runtime verification.

What the UKI and firmware comparison now establishes

I subsequently compared the two saved UKIs without installing another package. The reported PE sections, including .linux and .cmdline, were byte-identical; .initrd differed. Extracted contents differed in AMDGPU firmware files and alias paths, as well as hwdb.bin. Both images returned the same five properties for this GPU's hwdb query. That verifies this particular GPU query, not equality of the entire hardware database.

Separately recorded runtime snapshots show ME/PFP/MEC/MES versions 0x0c12 / 0x0c76 / 0x0d7a / 0x93 for the September boot and 0x0c26 / 0x0c94 / 0x0da2 / 0x97 for my regular Arch boot on 23 October. These values are more precise than a UKI filename or package version. They still do not provide full hashes of every loaded blob or retrospectively establish the complete firmware set for the stable session in run 1. Matching component versions alone do not make the regular 23 October boot identical to my earlier unpublished October test build.

What I can establish from the new crash

For the first time in this particular return-to-baseline test I got a Stalker2 Crash Reporter instead of only black displays. The attached CrashContext.runtime-xml identifies CrashType=GPUCrash and GPU Crash dump Triggered; it reports SecondsSinceStart=1019, just under 17 minutes. The game's Stalker2.log records:

  Device 0 Removed: DXGI_ERROR_DEVICE_REMOVED

This establishes a D3D12 device loss as seen by the game. The Linux desktop remained responsive, allowing me to preserve the log, crash context and Unreal minidump. However, the reporter does not by itself prove that the kernel successfully reset the GPU, or that this was the identical fault seen on 21 October. The subsequent kernel-journal excerpt contained no matching new AMDGPU page-fault/reset messages; absence of such messages does not rule out a graphics-stack problem.

The captured figures also do not suggest straightforward VRAM exhaustion: local use was approximately 7.2 GB out of a 15.4 GB budget, and the crash context did not flag an out-of-memory event. DRED data was unavailable. The Windows version printed by Unreal is the Proton compatibility environment – the host was still Arch Linux.

I also inspected UEMinidump.dmp statically. It contains CPU process state, thread/module information and an exception/reporting path. It did not identify a failing GPU instruction or reconstruct the original D3D12 Release/fence sequence. Without DRED, matching internal symbols and additional driver telemetry, those questions remain open. Unreal's “GPUCrash” label does not supply the missing evidence.

My assessment on 22 October: Selecting the alternative descriptor-set path markedly changed the observed behavior. With the newer firmware test build, even the previously problematic higher presets and reloads were temporarily stable. On the saved September UKI, the game still suffered a GPU-related failure, but this time produced useful diagnostics rather than the complete blackout previously observed. This is a promising workaround candidate and material for an upstream report, not a proven fix or a definitive assignment of root cause.

23 October 2026: I trace where the submission fails

I could play normally, but dying and reloading still brought up the Stalker2 crash reporter. This time I wanted to find out what happened before DXGI_ERROR_DEVICE_REMOVED. I kept the graphics stack and disabled descriptor-buffer path unchanged and initially added only Proton logging:

MY LAUNCH OPTIONS FOR THIS CAPTURE
PROTON_LOG=1 VKD3D_DISABLE_EXTENSIONS=VK_EXT_descriptor_buffer RADV_DEBUG=hang,noumr %command%
The new log confirms Proton proton-11.0-2e-x86_64, VKD3D-Proton 212991fc2c266bc, RADV 26.2.5 and the disabled extension at runtime. The 464 repeated extension checks are not 464 crashes.

The first Proton capture already contained CS rejected (-2). To distinguish its possible origins, I then used my Python script cthulhu-cs-watch.py. Without installing additional packages, I set up three temporary kernel return probes: buffer-list creation, syncobj/fence lookup and the outer command-submission call. I recorded only returns of -2. On Linux that is ENOENT; its specific meaning depends on the function being observed.

I first ran the read-only prerequisite check, then armed the capture with --arm. The script stayed running in an open terminal while I played. After ordinary gameplay I repeated the known death/reload sequence once. The game exited again. I stopped the capture with Ctrl+C and preserved the accompanying Proton log.

What I have actually established

In the inspected Linux 7.2.11 source, -ENOENT from amdgpu_bo_list_create identifies a failed drm_gem_object_lookup: at least one supplied buffer handle could not be resolved in the corresponding DRM file context. That submission was rejected while its buffer list was being constructed. This does not establish that earlier, already submitted GPU work was fault-free. The separately observed fence lookup did not return ENOENT in this capture.

The accompanying new Proton log contains exactly one CS rejected (-2) message. It is followed by Vulkan errors vr -4 and VK_ERROR_DEVICE_LOST; Unreal records D3D12 device removal at 11:35:01 CEST (09:35:01.271 UTC). The inspected RADV source explains how a rejected submission can subsequently be reported as device loss. This does not establish a kernel GPU reset. The raw Wine prefix 18740.037 and tracefs timestamp 18740.289707 cannot be subtracted directly without a common calibration; I infer no microsecond interval across those layers.

The new log reports 7,109.09 MB local memory use against a 15,189.84 MB budget and again no DRED data. It also contains 26 FSR-retention diagnostics, approximately 1,006.7 seconds before device loss on the Wine timeline. The final new FSR SetName/retention activations occur about seven milliseconds before the first submit error. Those are leads worth investigating. They neither associate the missing handle with a specific FSR resource nor establish an executed GPU access to freed memory.

After this initial capture, I still did not know: Which handle failed? Had it been closed, was an incorrect value supplied, or was there another bookkeeping error? Which D3D12 resource does it represent, and where are its Release and GPU-completion events? This first capture did not answer those questions; the following captures narrow them further. Descriptor lifetime remains the leading hypothesis for the old GPUVM fault; a connection to this newer submission rejected before GPU execution remains unproven. I also have not established an immediate hardlock after removing the extension filter in a controlled comparison.

Later on 23 October: The missing handle had already been closed successfully

Over the course of the day, an error code became a concrete lifetime trail. I extended my capture step by step: first to GEM handle creation and closure, then to call stacks, and finally to targeted probes in Wine Vulkan. I retained the kernel, Mesa, Proton and launch options shown above. Instrumentation itself can affect timing, so these are captured cases rather than a controlled statistical test series.

My actual workflow: I always start the collector before the game because S.T.A.L.K.E.R. runs fullscreen. Each launch then requires roughly 10–15 minutes of shader compilation. In these captures I reached gameplay, and the familiar death/reload sequence ended at the crash reporter again. Four complete captures, four closed handles

With cthulhu-gem-watch.py, I recorded seven kernel probe points. Besides failed lookups, I captured successful handle creation, entry and return of handle deletion, and DRM file-context release. The crucial identity is the handle number within the same DRM file context: a number is not globally unique, and it can be reused even within one context. A successful close removes that identifier; it does not establish the end of every object/GPU reference or destruction of all backing GPU memory.

What the handle-6233 stack actually establishes

The stack variant captured native return addresses during handle 6233's closure. I obtained RADV and Wine Vulkan GNU build IDs and checked matching symbols or the supplied library itself. The RADV stack reaches radv_destroy_descriptor_pool and release of its buffer object. In the matching winevulkan.so, the recorded frame is in thunk64_vkDestroyDescriptorPool, immediately after its host-driver call. This establishes pool destruction for that latest recorded generation.

The crucial limit: handle 6233 had previously represented other objects, including a command buffer and an upload buffer. I still do not know which generation the rejected BO list intended to reference. The latest pool closure alone therefore does not prove premature D3D12 heap release or the complete Release/fence sequence. My bounded stack capture also ends before a higher VKD3D caller. I do not assign the other three handles to descriptor pools based on this one stack.

Registration does not establish capture coverage

For the next step I prepared Pool Caller Watch v3, with seven kernel probes and two extra uprobes in the Windows Wine Vulkan wrapper. I checked exact file hashes and machine instructions; the disabled probes registered successfully on Cthulhu and were removed cleanly. Nevertheless, both later captures had zero hits at both PE probes. Kernel events were present, but a higher pool caller was not captured. Zero hits do not establish that the game never destroyed a pool.

Source inspection offers a testable lead: Wine initially maps PE code writable before applying final protections, while Linux excludes writable mappings during certain uprobe installation paths. My trace shows a successful close of GEM handle 3229, followed 6.887 ms later by a failed lookup and amdgpu_bo_list_create = -ENOENT in the same DRM file context. That is a concrete host-side error, not proof of a use-after-free or of fault in one particular component. The original issue describes an NVIDIA setup; my comment explicitly distinguishes the AMD finding.

Where I stand on 25 October: Eight completed GravityMark runs are now tied to application logs and Blackbox telemetry. The 242 W and 224 W values are verified as reported sensor readings, not independently measured electrical peaks. I have sent separate upstream reports for Arma and Stalker, but have no confirmed root cause, permanent fix or developer response at this point. My original logs and traces are preserved for focused follow-up questions. No more deliberately triggered hardlocks just to collect more of the same evidence.
