CTHULHU CRASH LAB · MISSION 04

RX 9060 XT Linux Hardlocks: Mesa 26.2.4 and My Troubleshooting Journey

By Dennis Hilk~14 min + lab11 October 2026

A new Radeon that initially worked beautifully on Linux. Then games where both monitors suddenly went black and the GPU fan shot to full speed. No useful final error message. I did not return the card. I wanted to understand why.

Status: 11 October 2026. My practical results with Mesa 26.2.4: roughly ten hours of gaming at the normal 160 W cap with one hard crash. That is a major improvement, not proof of a permanent fix. The exact root cause and upstream commit remain unidentified. Timings and results below are firsthand observations, not controlled benchmarks.
22 October 2026 update: My GPU black box recorded an AMDGPU page fault and preserved the coredump. A subsequent focused VKD3D comparison produced an intriguing result: with Vulkan descriptor buffers disabled, S.T.A.L.K.E.R. 2 initially ran even at Epic settings. Back on the normal September-firmware Arch baseline, a save reload instead ended with a logged DXGI_ERROR_DEVICE_REMOVED and Unreal crash reporter, rather than the previous complete hardlock. This is not a confirmed fix.

Why I tried AMD at all

I have no particular allegiance to NVIDIA, AMD or Intel. A graphics card should run my software, perform well and, one small aesthetic requirement, preferably be white. But my RTX 3060 Ti sometimes caused trouble on self-configured Linux systems: Gentoo would be running well until a game, driver update or OBS problem started another round of troubleshooting.

I found an old ASUS Strix Radeon R9 380 for €20 on a local classifieds site. I installed another M.2 SSD, put the card in and freshly tried Arch, Debian and Gentoo. OBS, Steam, Lutris and WoW Classic all worked beautifully. Debian was sometimes the awkward one when I wanted newer drivers, but the basic compatibility test passed.

Then I loaded Baldur's Gate 3. The aging R9 380 simply did not have the performance. I had already seen the white PowerColor RX 9060 XT Hellhound Spectral White 16 GB online and liked it. When the next paycheck arrived, the €20 experiment became an actual GPU upgrade.

At first it all worked

With the Hellhound I mostly played Baldur's Gate 3 and WoW Classic. Both were excellent. The RX 9060 XT was not a failure from day one. I discovered the serious issues later, when I wanted to see some eye candy and started S.T.A.L.K.E.R. 2 and other titles that I had previously neglected.

System and graphics updates had happened in the meantime. I cannot prove that those updates caused the crashes: I had not performed comparable tests in those games before. Sequence in time is not proof of causality.

23 October 2026 addition: The problems affected several games. Arma 3 was also particularly troublesome for me. My impression from the tests so far was that problems became more common as the games I tried became more graphically demanding. Lighter games such as Quake II and Duke Nukem ran without problems in my sessions. This is an observation from everyday play, not a systematic load measurement or proof that every crash has the same cause. The previously described loss of display output in Quake II RTX remains a separate observation.

In particular, Quake RTX was not the same as a whole-system hardlock. A game accepting inputs and a computer receiving a network file demonstrate that important parts of the operating system were still functioning. Black displays alone do not identify whether the failure lies in display output, rendering, a driver, PCIe or the entire machine.

Cthulhu, at least say OUCH before you die

The frustrating part was how often there was no useful AMDGPU error in the journal immediately before the worst crash. I saw a previous Arch message resembling amdgpu: device lost from bus, but mostly got black monitors, a roaring GPU fan and a forced restart. I was not even demanding an instant fix; I wanted the machine to shout “Ow, my leg!” before going down.

During a violent GPU/PCIe failure, the kernel may not get a chance to generate or persist a meaningful message. Missing logs are not evidence of a healthy driver stack. I watched live journal output and tried to isolate hardware factors too.

EXAMPLE · Observe Linux kernel messages (not an original historic log)
journalctl -kf -o short-monotonic
This command follows new kernel messages. A severe lockup can still happen without a useful final line.

Riser out, NIC out, games moved between drives

I tested the GPU with the PCIe riser and directly in the motherboard. I varied BIOS/PCIe settings, even removed the 10 GbE network card connected to my NAS, and tested the PSU's original PCIe cables instead of the white sleeved extensions. None of those changes eliminated the crashes.

At one stage the observed PCIe topology was: 00:03.1 Root Port → 53:00.0 Switch Upstream → 54:00.0 Switch Downstream → 55:00.0 GPU, with a reported 8 GT/s ×8 link. Worth investigating, yes; proof of root cause, no – especially because direct-slot tests failed too.

My power supply is from Corsair, but I cannot currently verify its exact model or capacity. I remember one 6+2-pin and one 6-pin PCIe power connection. That already ruled out GPU choices requiring two full 8-pin leads. Toggling the Hellhound's OC/Silent dual BIOS produced no useful change. I considered firmware flashing strictly as a last resort; I never did it.

A second suspect: SATA CRC errors

Alongside game stalls, the kernel reported BadCRC, READ FPDMA QUEUED and hard SATA link resets around ata2 and a Samsung 860 EVO. Those were real findings, but they need their own cable/port investigation.

To keep the problems apart, I relocated games from the SATA SSD to my system NVMe and also tried the other NVMe drive. The GPU hardlocks persisted at the time. That weakens the SATA SSD as a sole explanation. It does not mean the SATA link is healthy; cable and port tests are still on the to-do list.

The same S.T.A.L.K.E.R. route, a different OS

I kept a separate Windows 11 NVMe around, primarily for Battlefield. That gave me a useful control: same S.T.A.L.K.E.R. 2 save, Continue, spawn point and a lap around the base, with graphics broadly maxed out and the same RX 9060 XT. Under Windows that route completed without issue. Battlefield also ran as usual.

That is much more informative than comparing to an unrelated benchmark, but still not a mathematical proof against hardware. Windows and Linux exercise different graphics and driver paths, and every setting need not be identical. It made the Linux-specific execution path a stronger suspect.

112 watts: a hypothesis, not a miracle cure

I have capped WoW Classic at 60 FPS for years, even on a 144 Hz monitor. Why burn extra power for a game that already runs beautifully? Earlier Windows undervolting experiments had shown me substantial power savings. So I wondered: Could the PSU, power cable or a transient load be involved?

I cut the GPU power limit from 160 to 112 W. It prevented some hard crashes initially, but not reliably: the short stalls remained and not every full crash disappeared. Changing a power cap changes clocks and operating states as well. The result could not establish a broken power supply. At 112 W, Arma 3 still hard-crashed; Quake RTX lost display output while the game and LocalSend continued running. Black Flag Resynced still suffered intermittent blackouts after roughly 3–10 minutes of gameplay.

Beta kernels, Mesa versions – and finally 26.2.4

Deep in forum discussions I found other Linux/RDNA4 users with somewhat similar issues. That made a known or actively investigated software problem plausible. I tried a newer beta kernel; no improvement, sometimes worse. Other Mesa candidates were unsuccessful too. Two promising suggestions caused faster blackouts on my machine. I no longer know their exact version numbers and will not invent them.

I changed one variable at a time, tested it, and only then moved to the next. Eventually I tried Mesa 26.2.4. This was the first version I tested where Arma 3 no longer crashed immediately on world entry and S.T.A.L.K.E.R. 2 was playable for longer periods at normal power. The kernel and firmware remained standard NixOS packages; I explicitly override Mesa in configuration.nix, including its 32-bit component.

SHORT EXCERPT / DESCRIPTION OF MY CURRENT NIXOS CONFIGURATION · 2026
boot.kernelPackages = pkgs.linuxPackages_latest;
# Within hardware.graphics:
mesaVersion = "26.2.4";
# Both 64-bit and 32-bit Mesa are overridden
Intentionally abbreviated illustration, not a full copy-and-paste Nix expression. Cthulhu configuration archive. No persistent extra AMDGPU kernel tuning is shown in the provided configuration.

With that Mesa stack I raised the limit in controlled steps: 112 → 120 → 130 → 140 → 150 → 160 W, using S.T.A.L.K.E.R. as the test. The previous immediate failure did not return. This is evidence that changing the graphics software stack mattered in my tests. It does not identify a proven upstream fix commit.

21 October 2026 update: My GPU black box captures a real crash

After the earlier tests, I wanted evidence more useful than a black screen and a GPU fan at full speed. Cthulhu is now running Arch Linux with dwm again. To catch the next reproducible failure, I built a Cthulhu GPU Blackbox: a Bash utility with a separate systemd logger, once-per-second AMDGPU telemetry, a live kernel journal and a udev-triggered Linux devcoredump collector. It writes to a separately mounted NVMe, independently of X11 and the game window.

Two seconds later it created an AMDGPU devcoredump and reported a gfx_0.0.0 ring timeout, followed by a successful ring reset. The game remained stuck, but TTY2 worked, so I could inspect the evidence and later reboot normally.

The black box had saved the dump automatically before I ran a manual capture. Its .info sidecar records complete=yes and bytes=423438: the file copy completed without a reported read error, not proof that every register has been decoded or that the underlying bug is understood. The readable dump contains the matching process, GPU context (PASID 79 / VMID 7), gfx_0.0.0 ring and fault address. The journal and dump therefore describe the same event.

These are one-second snapshots, not a high-resolution electrical trace. They show no obvious thermal runaway or exhaustion of available VRAM. A 95–100% busy reading during recovery is not necessarily ordinary rendering load. My earlier black-screen/full-fan hardlocks are not automatically the same failure mode as this recoverable ring timeout.

An unusually close upstream report

A separate VKD3D-Proton report (#3221) on an RX 9070 XT documents the same distinctive combination: SQC (data), PERMISSION_FAULTS: 0x3, 0x00701431, vkd3d_queue and the gfx_0.0.0 timeout. A matching signature does not prove an identical root cause. The trigger could still be in the game, VKD3D-Proton, RADV/Mesa, AMDGPU or hardware. In particular, the other reporter's FSR observations are not evidence about FSR in my S.T.A.L.K.E.R. test.

The tool: Cthulhu GPU Blackbox – source and instructions on GitHub → · View the Bash script (.sh). The logger and real dump capture worked on my Arch machine. Other Linux configurations are not automatically validated; scrub private hostnames, process details, paths and other metadata before sharing logs or dumps.

Conclusion of the 21 October capture: I have not fixed the underlying Navi44 bug. I now have a reproducible, timestamped crash with the first AMDGPU coredump automatically preserved by my own tool. For debugging, that is genuine progress.

22 October 2026: From Low to Epic – and my first actionable Unreal crash report

The GPU black-box capture from the previous day made a much more focused hypothesis possible. The additional RADV hang report contained a command stream of 9,376 DWORDs (37,504 bytes) that matched the AMDGPU devcoredump's main command stream word for word. The last reached trace marker was 0x37 (decimal 55), followed by a compute dispatch. A descriptor read in the shader was reconstructed at 0xffff80000e000100, on the exact GPU virtual-memory page reported by the fault. RADV's memory history recorded an earlier allocation and release for that region, and the tool warned of a potential use-after-free.

This is evidence for a specific hypothesis, not proof of its cause. Without the D3D12 Release/fence sequence and the precise GPU execution point, we cannot say who released the buffer or conclusively assign blame to the game, VKD3D-Proton, RADV or a lower layer. I therefore tested one narrow change: hide the optional Vulkan VK_EXT_descriptor_buffer extension from VKD3D-Proton while retaining the existing RADV diagnostic options.

MY S.T.A.L.K.E.R. 2 STEAM LAUNCH OPTIONS · TEST ONLY, NOT A UNIVERSAL FIX
VKD3D_DISABLE_EXTENSIONS=VK_EXT_descriptor_buffer RADV_DEBUG=hang,noumr %command%
VKD3D_DISABLE_EXTENSIONS selects the alternative descriptor-set path in this build. RADV_DEBUG=hang,noumr was already used for diagnostics. No Mesa or kernel packages were changed for this test. Remove the added VKD3D setting if it causes problems; it cannot guarantee protection from hardlocks.

There is an important limitation: Runs 1 and 2 used different firmware builds. It would be premature to claim that the Steam launch option alone repaired the card. Equally, run 2 shows that disabling descriptor buffers did not completely eliminate failures on the regular September baseline. Newer October firmware with descriptor buffers enabled was not tested in this comparison. A stable session alone also cannot rule out timing-sensitive bugs.

What the new crash actually tells us

For the first time in this particular return-to-baseline test I got a Stalker2 Crash Reporter instead of only black displays. The attached CrashContext.runtime-xml identifies CrashType=GPUCrash and GPU Crash dump Triggered; it reports SecondsSinceStart=1019, just under 17 minutes. The game's Stalker2.log records:

LogD3D12RHI: Error: GPU crash detected:
  Device 0 Removed: DXGI_ERROR_DEVICE_REMOVED
DRED: could not find DRED data
Local Budget: 15419.25 MB
Local Used:    7202.28 MB

This establishes a D3D12 device loss as seen by the game. The Linux desktop remained responsive, allowing me to preserve the log, crash context and Unreal minidump. However, the reporter does not by itself prove that the kernel successfully reset the GPU, or that this was the identical fault seen on 21 October. The subsequent kernel-journal excerpt contained no matching new AMDGPU page-fault/reset messages; absence of such messages does not rule out a graphics-stack problem.

The captured figures also do not suggest straightforward VRAM exhaustion: local use was approximately 7.2 GB out of a 15.4 GB budget, and the crash context did not flag an out-of-memory event. DRED data was unavailable. The Windows version printed by Unreal is the Proton compatibility environment – the host was still Arch Linux.

My current assessment: Selecting the alternative descriptor-set path markedly changed the observed behavior. With the newer firmware test build, even the previously problematic higher presets and reloads were temporarily stable. On the regular September UKI, the game still suffered a GPU-related failure, but this time produced useful diagnostics rather than the complete blackout previously observed. This is a promising workaround candidate and material for an upstream report, not a proven fix or a definitive assignment of root cause.

Like working on my TS 250 X: change a jet, then test

My troubleshooting rule is the same as when I work on my motorcycle. Just last week I was working on my TS 250 X. If I change the main jet, air filter and needle position at the same time, I can no longer tell which change had which effect. So I change one thing and test it.

I used the same principle for the riser, PCIe, storage, power cables, kernels and Mesa. No, I never seriously wanted to return the Hellhound. I had my own NVIDIA problems, and switching brands does not answer the question.

So my conclusion is neither “AMD is broken” nor “Mesa 26.2.4 definitely fixed everything.” It is: Do not give up. Finding out why something behaves that way means you learned something. Cthulhu is largely playable again; the final root cause remains an open question.
